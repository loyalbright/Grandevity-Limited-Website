Some works describe a landscape.

Some describe a life.

Fan Zhongyan’s **Yueyang Lou Ji** begins with a tower and a lake, and ends with the world.

> 先天下之憂而憂，後天下之樂而樂。
>
> To worry before all under heaven worry, and to rejoice only after all under heaven rejoice.

These words have endured for nearly a thousand years.

Yet they are not an isolated maxim placed at the end of an essay.

Fan Zhongyan builds an entire journey before reaching them.

First comes Dongting Lake:

> 銜遠山，吞長江，浩浩湯湯，橫無際涯。

It embraces the distant mountains and swallows the Yangtze River, vast and surging, stretching beyond visible limits.

Then the world divides into two emotional climates.

One is a world of ceaseless rain.

The other is spring light and calm water.

Storm produces sorrow.

Clear skies produce joy.

But Fan Zhongyan asks a more difficult question:

Must a person’s inner life always rise and fall with external circumstances?

The essay therefore moves from displaced officials and poets toward another ideal:

the ancient benevolent person.

Their principle is:

> 不以物喜，不以己悲。
>
> They do not rejoice because of external things, nor grieve because of themselves.

From there the scale expands again:

> 居廟堂之高則憂其民；處江湖之遠則憂其君。

In high office, they worry for the people.

Far from the court, they still remain concerned for the state.

Only after this long movement does Fan Zhongyan arrive at the declaration for which the essay is remembered:

> 先天下之憂而憂，後天下之樂而樂。

Grandevity Studio’s **Yueyang Lou Ji** sets Fan Zhongyan’s complete original classical text to newly composed music.

There is no modern lyrical expansion.

No contemporary paraphrase.

No newly written chorus explaining Fan Zhongyan on his behalf.

The prose creates its own progression:

Baling and Yueyang Tower.

Dongting Lake.

Storm and sorrow.

Spring light and joy.

The ancient benevolent person.

Court and wilderness.

Responsibility.

All under heaven.

The climax is not a quotation suddenly attached to the ending.

It is an answer the entire essay has travelled toward.

### A Famous Essay Fan Zhongyan Did Not Write at Yueyang Tower

**Yueyang Lou Ji** was written in 1046, the sixth year of the Qingli era of the Northern Song dynasty.

It begins:

> 慶曆四年春，滕子京謫守巴陵郡。
>
> In the spring of the fourth year of Qingli, Teng Zijing was demoted to govern Baling Commandery.

Teng Zijing, also known as Teng Zongliang, was a friend of Fan Zhongyan.

After being demoted to Yuezhou, Teng restored Yueyang Tower, enlarged its former structure and had poems and prose by writers of the Tang dynasty and his own age inscribed there.

He then asked Fan Zhongyan to compose a record of the restored tower.

One historical fact is essential to understanding the work:

Fan Zhongyan was not in Yueyang when he wrote **Yueyang Lou Ji**.

He was in Dengzhou.

The famous vision of Dongting Lake is therefore not simply an eyewitness travel diary.

The central question is not merely:

What did Fan Zhongyan see?

It is:

What did he see through the idea of this lake?

---

### Holding the Distant Mountains, Swallowing the Yangtze

The second movement begins:

> 予觀夫巴陵勝狀，在洞庭一湖。

As Fan Zhongyan considers the magnificent scenery of Baling, its greatness is centred upon Dongting Lake.

Then come two extraordinarily powerful verbs:

> 銜遠山，吞長江。

The lake embraces the distant mountains.

It swallows the Yangtze.

It is immense.

It moves.

It changes.

Morning light changes it.

Evening shadow changes it.

Storm changes it.

Moonlight changes it.

Fan Zhongyan therefore asks:

> 覽物之情，得無異乎？

Could the feelings produced by viewing these different scenes possibly remain the same?

The next two movements provide the answer.

---

### Endless Rain: When the Landscape Becomes Sorrow

The first Dongting Lake is a world beneath prolonged rain.

Cold winds howl.

Turbid waves rise toward the sky.

The sun and stars lose their light.

Mountains disappear.

Merchants and travellers cease their journeys.

Masts lean.

Oars break.

At twilight, darkness deepens.

Those who ascend the tower in such conditions think of exile, home, slander and humiliation.

The result is:

> 滿目蕭然，感極而悲者矣。

Everything before them appears desolate, and emotion reaches its extreme in sorrow.

The storm exists on the lake.

It also exists within the observer.

---

### Bright Spring: The Same Lake Becomes Another World

Then Fan Zhongyan turns the entire landscape over:

> 至若春和景明，波瀾不驚。

Spring becomes mild.

The light becomes clear.

The waves become calm.

Sky and water open into an immense field of green.

Gulls fly and settle.

Fish move beneath the surface.

Fragrant plants and orchids grow along the shores and islets.

At night:

> 皓月千里，浮光躍金，靜影沉璧。

The moon shines for a thousand li.

Floating light leaps like gold.

Its still reflection sinks like a jade disc.

Fishing songs answer one another.

The observer now becomes open-hearted and refreshed, forgetting favour and disgrace.

The same tower.

The same lake.

Two entirely different emotional worlds.

Yet Fan Zhongyan refuses to end the argument there.

---

### Not Rejoicing Because of External Things, Not Grieving Because of Oneself

With:

> 嗟夫！

the prose changes direction.

Fan Zhongyan asks about the mind of the ancient benevolent people.

Why might their response differ from the two emotional states already described?

His answer is:

> 不以物喜，不以己悲。

They do not rejoice because of external things.

Nor do they grieve because of themselves.

This does not mean the absence of emotion.

It means that one’s moral centre is no longer wholly governed by weather, success, failure, honour or disgrace.

The lake may be storm-darkened.

The lake may be radiant.

A career may advance.

A career may collapse.

But a human being may choose to care about something larger than the self.

---

### High in Government, Far Among Rivers and Lakes

Fan Zhongyan then writes:

> 居廟堂之高則憂其民；
> 處江湖之遠則憂其君。

In high government office, they worry for the people.

Far away among rivers and lakes, they remain concerned for their ruler.

Thus:

> 是進亦憂，退亦憂。

They worry when advancing.

They worry when retreating.

This produces the essay’s decisive question:

> 然則何時而樂耶？

Then when can they be joyful?

Only now does Fan Zhongyan give the answer.

---

### To Worry Before All Under Heaven

> 先天下之憂而憂，
> 後天下之樂而樂。

The statement does not reject happiness.

It changes the order between the self and the wider world.

Responsibility comes before personal ease.

One worries before others must worry.

One allows personal joy to come only after the wider world can share in joy.

This is the final expansion of scale in **Yueyang Lou Ji**.

A tower became a lake.

The lake became emotion.

Emotion became character.

Character became responsibility.

Responsibility became all under heaven.

---

### “Without Such People, With Whom Could I Return?”

The essay does not actually end with its most famous declaration.

Fan Zhongyan adds:

> 噫！微斯人，吾誰與歸？

Alas.

Without such people, with whom could I return?

The emotional temperature changes again.

After the grandeur of “all under heaven,” there is sudden solitude.

Who else shares this ideal?

Who can walk the same path?

The final moment is therefore both elevated and deeply human.

The MV does not turn the ending into a triumphal tableau.

It returns instead to solitude:

one figure,

one lake,

and the unanswered search for a kindred spirit.

### The MV: A Changing Lake and an Expanding Moral Horizon

The visual world is grounded in a Northern Song historical setting.

The early movements belong to Dongting Lake:

waterways,

sails,

clouds,

storms,

spring light,

moonlight.

The storm sequence gradually removes light from the world.

Water becomes violent.

Travel stops.

The harbour empties.

The emotional world of the displaced scholars contracts with the landscape.

Then, with spring light, the film opens again.

Water clears.

Gulls return.

Fish move beneath the surface.

Plants cover the banks.

Moonlight stretches across the lake.

Fishing songs answer one another.

But the visual argument ultimately moves beyond scenery.

The later sections introduce Fan Zhongyan, the imagined court, distant life among rivers and lakes, and ordinary people.

By the time the text reaches:

> 先天下之憂而憂

the camera is looking directly at human lives.

Because all under heaven is not merely a landscape.

It is people.

### From One Tower to All Under Heaven

The lasting power of **Yueyang Lou Ji** is not simply that it contains memorable quotations.

Fan Zhongyan changes the scale of the reader’s attention.

First:

a tower.

Then:

a lake.

Then:

weather.

Then:

emotion.

Then:

the moral centre of a human being.

And finally:

all under heaven.

The journey is from what we see,

to what we feel,

to what we choose to care about.
