## Du Fu: a poet who saw beyond the splendour

**Du Fu (712–770)**, courtesy name Zimei, stands among the great poets of the Tang dynasty. Later generations honoured him as the **Poet Sage** and described his work as a form of **poetic history**, reflecting how closely his writing attends to political upheaval and ordinary human experience.

*Li Ren Xing* is commonly dated to the spring of **753 CE**, in Emperor Xuanzong's Tianbao era, though its exact dating has been discussed. Du Fu was then in Chang'an, pursuing public office with little success. The imperial capital was dazzlingly prosperous, yet the concentration of favour and influence around certain court families had become a subject of concern.

## Spring at Qujiang: the Shangsi festival and the Yang family

The poem opens on the **third day of the third lunar month**, associated with the **Shangsi festival**, a traditional occasion for spring outings. **Qujiang**, southeast of Tang Chang'an, was a celebrated place for pleasure excursions and banquets. Du Fu begins with a crowd of elegantly dressed women before singling out **Lady Guo** and **Lady Qin**, two sisters of the imperial consort **Yang Guifei** who enjoyed exceptional court favour.

The gold-thread peacocks, silver-thread qilin, intricate hair ornaments and pearls are not incidental decorations. Each detail builds the image of a social world in which beauty and privilege have become almost inseparable. The description then turns to rare dishes and precious serving vessels. In the line about untouched rhinoceros-horn chopsticks, abundance has passed into indifference: even extraordinary food no longer arouses the diners' appetite.

## A warning concealed in a beautiful scene

Palace attendants ride swiftly; delicacies arrive in unending succession; musicians perform; guests and retainers throng the entrances. Later, a rider approaches, pauses and steps from his horse onto brocade. Traditional commentators identify him as **Yang Guozhong**, a kinsman of Yang Guifei who became right chancellor in **752**.

The willow catkins, water plants and bluebird carrying a red kerchief have long attracted symbolic interpretations. Some traditional commentaries read them as a veiled allusion to relations between Yang Guozhong and Lady Guo. That remains a literary reading of the poem, not documentary proof of any particular private encounter.

The closing couplet delivers the sting: power is so intense that one must beware of approaching the chancellor and provoking his displeasure. Du Fu has not needed a speech of denunciation. His precise account of silk, food, status and deference has already made the argument.

The **An Lushan Rebellion began in 755**, only a few years after the commonly accepted date of the poem. Seen in retrospect, this springtime scene captures the uneasy proximity of brilliance and fragility; it should not, however, be treated as a literal prediction of later events.

三月三日天氣新，
長安水邊多麗人。
態濃意遠淑且真，
肌理細膩骨肉勻。

繡羅衣裳照暮春，
蹙金孔雀銀麒麟。
頭上何所有？
翠微㔩葉垂鬢唇。
背後何所見？
珠壓腰衱穩稱身。

就中雲幕椒房親，
賜名大國虢與秦。

紫駝之峰出翠釜，
水精之盤行素鱗。
犀箸饜飫久未下，
鸞刀縷切空紛綸。

黃門飛鞚不動塵，
御廚絡繹送八珍。
簫鼓哀吟感鬼神，
賓從雜遝實要津。

後來鞍馬何逡巡，
當軒下馬入錦茵。
楊花雪落覆白蘋，
青鳥飛去銜紅巾。

炙手可熱勢絕倫，
慎莫近前丞相嗔！

## English Reading Translation (Not Sung)

On the third day of the third month, the air is fresh;  
By the waters of Chang'an, beautiful women abound.  
Rich in bearing, distant in gaze, graceful and poised;  
Their skin is fine, their forms harmoniously shaped.

Embroidered silks shine upon the late spring;  
Gold-thread peacocks and silver qilin adorn their robes.  
What ornaments crown their heads?  
Jade-green floral pieces hang beside their temples.  
And what appears behind them?  
Pearls weigh down the folds at their waists, keeping every line in place.

Among the cloudlike curtains sit imperial favourites,  
Ladies granted the noble titles of Guo and Qin.

Camel-hump delicacies emerge from green cauldrons;  
White-scaled fish pass by on plates of crystal.  
Sated, they leave their costly chopsticks untouched;  
Fine knives slice endlessly, all that bustling labour in vain.

Palace attendants race on horseback, hardly stirring the dust;  
The imperial kitchens send eight rare delicacies without pause.  
Flutes and drums sound so plaintively they could move spirits;  
Guests and retainers crowd the very gates of influence.

Then comes a horseman, lingering with self-assurance;  
He dismounts before the pavilion and steps onto brocade.  
Willow catkins fall like snow upon the water plants;  
A bluebird flies away carrying a crimson kerchief.

His power burns so fiercely that none can rival it;  
Take care not to draw near, lest the chancellor take offence.

This English rendering is a reading aid rather than an alternative lyric set. The poem also carries irony, allusion and textual ambiguities that no single translation can fully reproduce.
