On a lonely riverbank in the ancient State of Chu, an exiled statesman encounters a fisherman. One refuses to sacrifice his integrity for acceptance; the other believes that the wise must respond to the world as it changes. Neither persuades the other. The fisherman rows away, and the river continues to flow.

## 📖 Qu Yuan, Exile, and the Authorship of *Yu Fu*

Qu Yuan (traditionally identified as Qu Ping) was a statesman and literary figure associated with the State of Chu during China's Warring States period. Sima Qian's *Records of the Grand Historian* describes his service at the court of King Huai of Chu, his loss of royal favor amid political intrigue, and his subsequent exile. Over the centuries, Qu Yuan became an enduring symbol of loyalty, moral commitment, and the tragedy of a principled voice unable to prevent political decline.

*Yu Fu* opens with Qu Yuan already exiled. He wanders through the river marshes, reciting verses, his face tired and his body thin. A fisherman recognizes him as the former Grand Master of the Three Clans and asks why he has fallen so far. With almost no elaborate exposition, a dialogue begins that still challenges readers today.

Authorship deserves an important qualification. The poem is traditionally attributed to Qu Yuan, and the Eastern Han commentator Wang Yi explicitly treated it as his work. Its actual authorship and date, however, remain subjects of scholarly debate: some scholars understand it as a literary portrait of Qu Yuan composed by another writer. The riverbank conversation is therefore a scene preserved in classical literature, not an independently verified transcript of a historical meeting.

## 🌊 “The World Is Muddy; I Alone Am Clear”

Qu Yuan's response is uncompromising. He sees a world in which he must choose between public acceptance and personal integrity. His declaration of being clear while the world is muddy — awake while others are drunk — makes purity and intoxication metaphors for moral discernment, political compromise, and the loneliness of conviction.

The fisherman answers with a different philosophy: “A sage does not remain fixed and constrained by things, but can move and change with the world.” He counsels flexibility rather than an unyielding confrontation with circumstances. The water can be clear or muddy, and a wise person may find different ways to live in either condition.

Qu Yuan insists that the clean should not voluntarily take on the world's dirt. The fisherman does not defeat him in argument; nor does Qu Yuan convert the fisherman. They simply part. The open ending allows their opposing ways of life to remain in tension.

## 🛶 The Canglang Song: When Water Changes

The fisherman departs with a brief song: when the Canglang water is clear, it may wash the ribbons of a cap; when muddy, it may wash one's feet. The song transforms a practical observation into a philosophy of adapting one's actions to circumstances.

A related Canglang verse also appears in the *Mencius*, “Li Lou I,” with textual variants equivalent to “my cap ribbons” and “my feet.” These are part of a wider textual tradition. This musical release follows the finalized wording of the *Chu Ci* source and does not substitute the *Mencius* variant into the sung text.

## 🎼 Ancient Prose, New Cinematic Orchestration

The composition moves at 72 BPM in a flowing 6/8 meter. Piano, guqin, xiao flute, cello, orchestral strings, and French horns shape a contemporary Mandarin ballad with cinematic scale. A mature, resonant male voice expresses Qu Yuan's sorrow and resolve, while a warmer, weathered voice suggests the fisherman's calm acceptance.

The music grows in intensity around the declaration of moral clarity and the image of “radiant whiteness.” When the fisherman finally sings the Canglang refrain, the orchestration begins to settle. The closing silence matters as much as the final notes: no one wins the argument, and neither man's life is reduced to a slogan.

## 🎞️ The Music Video: Two Ways of Living by One River

The MV imagines a late-autumn riverscape of Warring States Chu: muted reeds, muddy banks, cold mist, distant hills, and a long, low-sided wooden fishing boat without a sail. Qu Yuan wears a restrained pale Chu-style cross-collar robe; the older fisherman wears rough brown fabric and moves naturally with the river.

The dramatic separation takes place without confrontation. The fisherman rows into the distance while Qu Yuan remains alone on shore. The film closes on reeds, twilight, and water — a visual reminder that a debate can remain unresolved while history keeps moving.

## 📖 English Reading Translation — Not Sung Lyrics

The following translation is provided for comprehension. The recording is sung in Mandarin Chinese using the original classical text.

[Opening]
After Qu Yuan had been exiled, he wandered beside a river and marsh,
chanting as he walked along the shore.
His complexion was worn and his appearance gaunt.

The fisherman saw him and asked:

“Are you not the Grand Master of the Three Clans?
What has brought you to this state?”

Qu Yuan replied:

“All the world is muddy, yet I alone am clear;
all others are drunk, yet I alone am awake.
That is why I have been banished.”

The fisherman said:

“A sage does not remain fixed and constrained by things,
but can move and change with the world.
If the world is muddy, why not stir its mud and raise its waves?
If everyone is drunk, why not eat the lees and sip the strained wine?
Why hold yourself apart with such lofty thoughts
and bring exile upon yourself?”

Qu Yuan replied:

“I have heard it said:
One who has just washed his hair should dust his cap;
one who has just bathed should shake out his clothes.
How could a person keep his body spotless
only to accept the grime of the world?
I would sooner cast myself into the Xiang River
and be buried in the bellies of river fish.
How could I let bright, unblemished whiteness
be covered by the dust of common life?”

The fisherman smiled,
struck his oar against the boat, and departed, singing:

“When the Canglang waters are clear,
I can wash the ribbons of my cap.
When the Canglang waters are muddy,
I can wash my feet.”

Then he went away and spoke no more to Qu Yuan.
