## Tao Hongjing: The Scholar Known as the "Prime Minister in the Mountains"

**Tao Hongjing (456–536 CE)**, courtesy name Tongming, was a Chinese scholar, writer, Daoist thinker, physician and calligrapher.

Born in Danyang Moling, in the area of present-day Nanjing, he lived through the Liu Song, Southern Qi and Liang dynasties.

As a young man, he served at the Southern Qi court, including as a tutor to imperial princes.

In 492 CE, he resigned from official service and withdrew to Mount Juqu, part of the Maoshan mountain area in present-day Jiangsu Province.

There, he adopted the name **Huayang Yinju**, meaning the Recluse of Huayang.

When Emperor Wu established the Liang dynasty in 502 CE, Tao's scholarly reputation remained influential.

Although he declined to return to formal government service, the emperor frequently consulted him on important state affairs.

His extraordinary position—living in seclusion while advising the imperial court—earned him the celebrated title **"Prime Minister in the Mountains."**

Tao's writings reflect this distinctive relationship between intellectual life, public responsibility and withdrawal into nature.

For him, the mountains were not merely beautiful scenery.

They also offered a place for reflection, scholarship and spiritual freedom.

This background helps explain why his landscape writing can feel both intensely personal and remarkably detached from worldly ambition.

## A Letter to Someone Who Appreciated Nature

The Chinese title *Da Xie Zhongshu Shu* (答謝中書書) can be understood as **"A Letter in Reply to Xie, a Secretariat Official."**

The first character, *da*, means "to reply."

The final *shu* means "letter."

*Xie Zhongshu* refers to a correspondent named Xie who held an office associated with the imperial secretariat.

The precise identity of the recipient remains debated.

Scholars have proposed several candidates, including Xie Zheng, Xie Tiao, Xie Lan and Xie Fei.

Identifying the correspondent as Xie Zheng is a common interpretation, but it is not the only proposal in the scholarly literature.

The exact date of composition and the geographical location of the described landscape are also uncertain.

Consequently, the imagery should not be presented as a documented description of one precisely identifiable valley or mountain.

What the letter communicates, however, is unmistakable:

a delight in the natural world and a desire to share that delight with someone capable of appreciating it.

During the Southern Dynasties, landscape writing developed into a major form of literary and intellectual expression.

Tao Hongjing's letter belongs to that tradition.

Its subject is not simply scenery, but the relationship between natural beauty and the mind that perceives it.

## "The Beauty of Mountains and Rivers": A Shared Experience Across Time

The letter opens with:

> 山川之美，古來共談。

*The beauty of mountains and rivers has been admired and discussed since ancient times.*

Tao does not begin by naming a particular peak or explaining the circumstances of his journey.

He begins with something broader.

Across generations, people have found beauty in mountains and flowing water.

The words **"since ancient times"** connect this private letter to a shared literary tradition.

The recipient may be a particular friend, but the pleasure being described is one that readers from many different periods can recognise.

That is also the starting point for Grandevity Studio's musical adaptation.

The instruments and production belong to the present.

The words belong to the sixth century.

Yet the experience of admiring the landscape can still be shared.

## High Peaks and Clear Streams: An Entire Landscape in Eight Characters

Tao brings the landscape into focus:

> 高峰入雲，清流見底。

*High peaks rise into the clouds; clear streams reveal their beds.*

The contrast is immediate.

The mountains ascend toward the sky, while the water reveals what lies below.

Height and depth, distance and clarity, are expressed in only a few words.

The first phrase directs the gaze upwards.

The second invites the eye to look into the transparent stream.

The balance of the two clauses also creates an audible rhythm.

This natural symmetry makes the text especially suitable for a musical setting without the need for newly written lyrics.

## The Colours of the Cliffs and the Green of the Forest

Tao continues:

> 兩岸石壁，五色交輝。
>
> 青林翠竹，四時俱備。

The stone cliffs on either bank shine with varied colours.

Green forests and emerald bamboo flourish throughout the seasons.

The expression "five colours" conveys richness and variety rather than requiring us to identify exactly five fixed hues on every cliff face.

The phrase "all four seasons" suggests the enduring vitality of the wooded landscape.

The imagery is compact, but the sense of space grows.

The cliffs bring shape and texture.

The forests and bamboo bring colour and life.

The result is a landscape that feels layered and alive, even before the animals begin to move.

## When the Morning Mist Clears

One of the most memorable passages begins:

> 曉霧將歇，猿鳥亂鳴；

*As the morning mist begins to disperse, gibbons and birds call across the mountains.*

The character *xie* (歇) means "to cease" or "to disperse" in this context.

It does not mean that the mist is resting.

As the vapour lifts, the landscape gradually becomes clear.

At the same moment, the calls of birds and gibbons begin to fill the valley.

The passage moves naturally from a visual transformation to an auditory one.

The natural world is no longer silent.

The mountains become a space filled with distant, overlapping sounds.

## Sunset and Fish Leaping from the Water

The morning scene is paired with:

> 夕日欲頹，沉鱗競躍。

*As the evening sun sinks toward the horizon, fish from the depths leap and splash.*

The word *tui* (頹) here refers to the sun descending.

The phrase *chen lin* (沉鱗), literally "submerged scales," poetically refers to fish beneath the water.

The descending sun and leaping fish create two opposite movements.

The sun moves slowly downward.

The fish suddenly spring upward.

Stillness gives way to motion.

The two moments—morning and evening—give the landscape a temporal rhythm.

The prose is carefully balanced, yet it never feels mechanical.

Its language moves naturally from vast scenery to intimate details, bringing the landscape to life.

These paired images make the passage almost musical before any composition has begun.

## "An Immortal Paradise Within the Human World"

After describing the mountains, streams and living creatures, Tao declares:

> 實是欲界之仙都。

*Truly, this is an immortal paradise within the human world.*

The expression *yujie*, or "realm of desire," comes from Buddhist cosmology.

In the context of the letter, it points to the human world.

*Xiandu* refers to an immortal realm or paradise.

Together, they express an extraordinary idea:

one does not have to leave the human world to encounter something worthy of paradise.

Tao Hongjing was deeply engaged with Daoist scholarship, yet the language of this passage also draws upon terminology associated with Buddhist thought.

This illustrates the richness of intellectual and religious vocabulary during the Southern Dynasties.

The phrase **"實是欲界之仙都"** is the most direct moment of wonder in the letter.

In this musical adaptation, it also becomes the principal melodic refrain.

Its return through the chorus and closing sections is not a modern lyrical invention.

It is a repeated setting of Tao's own words.

## Xie Lingyun and the Rarity of True Appreciation

The final sentence introduces Kangle:

> 自康樂以來，未復有能與其奇者。

*Since the time of Kangle, there has scarcely been anyone capable of appreciating its extraordinary beauty.*

Kangle is a reference to **Xie Lingyun (385–433 CE)**, the influential landscape poet who held the hereditary title Duke of Kangle.

Xie Lingyun was a major figure in the development of Chinese landscape poetry and was celebrated for his love of mountains, rivers and exploration.

By invoking him, Tao places his appreciation of nature within a longer literary tradition.

The expression *yu qi qi* (與其奇) means to participate in, appreciate or fully grasp the extraordinary beauty of the landscape.

The final thought is therefore as much about the rarity of true appreciation as it is about the landscape itself.

Beauty may be visible to many people.

To recognise it deeply—and to share it with someone who understands—is another matter.

That is the emotional centre of this brief letter.

It is not only a celebration of nature.

It is also an expression of the desire for a kindred spirit.

## 📖 Complete English Translation of the Original Prose

**Tao Hongjing — Reply to Xie Zhongshu**

The beauty of mountains and rivers has been admired and discussed since ancient times.

High peaks rise into the clouds; clear streams reveal their beds.

The stone cliffs on either bank shine with a multitude of colours.

Green forests and emerald bamboo flourish through all four seasons.

As the morning mist begins to disperse, gibbons and birds call out across the mountains.

As the evening sun sinks toward the horizon, fish from the depths leap and splash.

Truly, this is an immortal paradise within the human world.

Since the time of Kangle, there has scarcely been anyone capable of appreciating its extraordinary beauty.
