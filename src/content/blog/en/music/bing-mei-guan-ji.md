The plum trees were not born diseased.

Some grew straight.

Some leaned naturally toward the light.

Some carried abundant branches, while others stood with only a few blossoms scattered across their limbs.

Each followed its own nature.

Then people began to decide which shapes were beautiful.

They admired the crooked.

Rejected the straight.

Praised the sparse.

Condemned the dense.

And in their determination to manufacture beauty, they wounded the very trees they claimed to love.

> 而江、浙之梅皆病。

And so the plum trees of Jiangsu and Zhejiang became afflicted.

Gong Zizhen's **Record of the Hall of Ailing Plum Trees** (*Bing Mei Guan Ji*, 病梅館記) is not simply an essay about horticulture.

It is a meditation on what happens when rigid standards are imposed upon living things.

It is also a story about compassion, courage and the decision to restore what has been harmed.

Grandevity Studio presents a complete new musical setting of the project-established original Classical Chinese text.

The production combines Modern C-Pop, Neo-Traditional Chinese music, acoustic guitar, gentle guzheng and an expressive Mandarin male vocal.

There are no newly written lyrics.

No modern lyrical expansion.

No rewritten Chinese passages.

No newly invented chorus.

No repeated original sentences merely to extend the composition.

The prose itself provides the journey:

Beauty.

Distortion.

Anger.

Compassion.

And the possibility of renewal.

### Gong Zizhen: A Thinker in an Age of Uncertainty

**Gong Zizhen (1792–1841)**, courtesy name Seren and literary name Ding'an, was one of the most important Chinese writers and thinkers of the later Qing dynasty.

He came from Renhe County in Zhejiang, in the region of present-day Hangzhou.

Born into an educated official family, Gong developed a strong interest in historical scholarship, political institutions, social conditions and the cultivation of talent.

In 1829, the ninth year of the Daoguang reign, he passed the imperial jinshi examination.

He subsequently held several government positions, including posts associated with the Grand Secretariat and the Ministry of Rites.

Yet official life did not lead him to accept the political order uncritically.

During the early nineteenth century, the Qing state faced growing administrative rigidity, fiscal pressures and social tensions.

Gong believed that inherited institutions required reconsideration and that a country could not renew itself while suppressing independent thought and restricting the development of talented individuals.

In 1839, the nineteenth year of the Daoguang reign, he resigned from government service and travelled south.

That year he also wrote his celebrated series of poems, *Ji Hai Miscellaneous Poems* (*Ji Hai Za Shi*).

Among their best-known lines are:

> 我勸天公重抖擻，
> 不拘一格降人材。

I urge Heaven to rouse itself once more, and bring forth talent without confining it to a single pattern.

**Bing Mei Guan Ji** was also written in 1839.

The prose essay and the poems are different literary works, but they belong to the same intellectual world.

Both reflect Gong's concern with talent, individuality and the urgent need for renewal.

Reading the ailing plum trees as an allegory for restricted human potential is an established literary interpretation; it should not erase the essay's literal account of plum-tree cultivation and the author's response to it.

### Three Places, One Tradition of Plum Blossoms

The essay begins with geography:

> 江寧之龍蟠，
> 蘇州之鄧尉，
> 杭州之西谿，
> 皆產梅。

Longpan in Jiangning, Dengwei in Suzhou, and Xixi in Hangzhou all produced plum trees.

These places belong to the cultural geography of Jiangnan, the region south of the lower Yangtze River.

Jiangning broadly corresponds to present-day Nanjing in Jiangsu Province.

Dengwei lies in the Guangfu area of modern Suzhou and has long been associated with plum blossom appreciation.

Xixi is situated in western Hangzhou, a region known for its waterways and wetland landscapes.

Gong begins with familiar scenery rather than an overt political argument.

The reader enters a world of refined taste.

Some people say that plum trees are beautiful when curved, because straight ones lack graceful form.

That leaning trees are picturesque, while upright ones lack interest.

That sparse branches are elegant, while dense growth lacks distinction.

Gong does not immediately reject these preferences.

He answers:

> 固也。

Granted.

The problem is not that curved or sparse trees can be beautiful.

The problem begins when these preferences become compulsory standards.

### When Aesthetic Taste Becomes a Form of Harm

Gong explains how the preferences of scholars and painters become known to those who sell plum trees.

The merchants discover a commercial opportunity.

To obtain higher prices, they begin producing the fashionable shapes artificially.

> 斫其正，
> 養其旁條，
> 刪其密，
> 夭其稚枝，
> 鋤其直，
> 遏其生氣，
> 以求重價。

Upright growth is chopped away.

Side branches are deliberately encouraged.

Dense growth is removed.

Young shoots are stunted.

Straight branches are cut back.

The trees' natural vitality is suppressed.

All to command a higher price.

The result is devastating.

> 文人畫士之禍之烈至此哉！

How terrible the harm brought about by these scholars and painters!

The passage is powerful because it turns aesthetic preference into a moral question.

Who decides what is beautiful?

What happens when a preferred form becomes the only acceptable one?

And what is lost when natural differences are treated as defects?

In the traditional interpretation of **Bing Mei Guan Ji**, the wounded plum trees stand for individuals and talents constrained by rigid institutions and social expectations.

The scholars' narrow aesthetic preferences become a metaphor for systems that reward conformity and discourage independent development.

Yet Gong's criticism begins with something even more direct.

A living thing has been harmed in the name of beauty.

### Three Hundred Wounded Trees

The emotional centre of the essay arrives when Gong describes his own response.

> 予購三百盆，
> 皆病者，無完者，
> 既泣之三日。

He recounts purchasing three hundred pots of plum trees.

All were damaged.

Not one was sound.

He grieved for three days.

But the story does not end in sorrow.

> 乃誓療之、縱之、順之。

He vows to heal the trees, release them from constraint and allow them to follow their natural growth.

This is the decisive turning point.

He does not propose another fashionable way to shape plum trees.

He does not replace one compulsory standard with another.

Instead, he begins removing the conditions that made the trees sick.

He breaks their restrictive pots.

Returns their roots to the open earth.

And loosens the palm-fibre cords binding their branches.

Then he makes a promise:

> 以五年為期，
> 必復之全之。

He will devote five years to restoring their health and wholeness.

The distinction matters.

Restoration is not about making the trees more decorative.

It is about allowing them to recover their own capacity for growth.

---

### Why Five Years Matter

The promise of five years gives the essay an important sense of reality.

A damaged branch cannot be restored simply by removing a cord.

A weakened root system does not recover the moment a pot is broken.

Healing takes time.

Space.

Attention.

And patience.

This is why the music video does not portray the trees as suddenly healed through magic.

The film shows the beginning of restoration rather than its miraculous completion.

The restraints are removed.

The roots return to the soil.

Morning light reaches branches that still bear their scars.

The damage remains visible.

But the trees can begin growing again.

### The Hall of Ailing Plum Trees

Gong writes:

> 予本非文人畫士，
> 甘受詬厲，
> 闢病梅之館以貯之。

He is willing to endure criticism and establish a place to shelter the wounded trees.

The Hall of Ailing Plum Trees is therefore both a literal and symbolic refuge.

A place for damaged plants.

A place of care.

And, through the essay's wider meaning, an imagined refuge for individuality and talent.

Near the end, Gong expresses a wish:

If only he had more leisure and more land, he could shelter the damaged plum trees of Jiangning, Hangzhou and Suzhou.

He would willingly devote the rest of his life to their care.

> 窮予生之光陰以療梅也哉？

Could I devote all the remaining years of my life to healing these plum trees?

Gong died in 1841, about two years after writing the essay.

Whether the five-year restoration described in the text was ever completed cannot be firmly established from the surviving historical record.

The literary promise, however, outlived him.

The image of freeing wounded plum trees continues to express a larger hope:

that people, too, should have the opportunity to develop according to their own nature.

### 📖 Complete English Translation

**Gong Zizhen — Record of the Hall of Ailing Plum Trees**

**The Beauty of Plum Trees**

Longpan in Jiangning, Dengwei in Suzhou and Xixi in Hangzhou all produce plum trees.

Some say that plum trees are beautiful when curved, for straight ones lack graceful form; that they are beautiful when leaning, for upright ones lack picturesque appeal; and that they are beautiful when sparse, for dense ones lack elegance.

**The Standards of Scholars and Painters**

Granted.

These scholars and painters understand such preferences, yet they cannot openly proclaim them as rules by which every plum tree in the world must be judged.

Nor can they command ordinary people everywhere to chop off straight branches, thin dense growth and remove upright shoots, making a livelihood from stunting and damaging plum trees.

Indeed, the naturally leaning, sparse and curved forms of plum trees are not things that people merely seeking money can necessarily produce through their own knowledge and ability.

**The Making of Ailing Plum Trees**

Yet someone reveals the peculiar hidden preferences of these scholars and painters to the merchants who sell plum trees.

The sellers then chop away upright growth, encourage side branches, remove dense shoots, stunt young twigs, cut back straight branches and suppress the trees' vitality, all to demand higher prices.

Thus the plum trees of Jiangsu and Zhejiang become afflicted.

How terrible the harm caused by the scholars and painters!

**The Promise to Restore Them**

I purchased three hundred pots of plum trees.

All were ailing; not one was whole.

I wept over them for three days.

Then I vowed to heal them, release them from constraint and let them follow their nature.

I broke their pots, planted them all in the earth and untied their palm-fibre bindings.

I set myself five years to restore them fully.

I am no scholar-painter of that kind.

I am willing to endure reproach and abuse.

So I established the Hall of Ailing Plum Trees to shelter them.

**The Final Wish**

Alas!

If only I had more days of leisure, and more unused land, so that I might shelter even more of the ailing plum trees of Jiangning, Hangzhou and Suzhou!

Would that I could devote all the remaining years of my life to healing them!

*Translation note: The English text above is a literary rendering for international readers. The music video is performed in Classical Chinese, using the established Chinese text reproduced above.*

### Why Bing Mei Guan Ji Still Matters

Many forms of restriction do not initially appear cruel.

They may be presented as refinement.

As improvement.

As tradition.

As the pursuit of excellence.

Or simply as an accepted standard of beauty.

Gong Zizhen asks what happens when those standards become more important than the living beings subjected to them.

A tree may be beautiful when curved.

It may also be beautiful when straight.

Its worth does not depend on whether it satisfies someone else's preferred shape.

The essay's continuing relevance lies in this distinction:

**Appreciation is not the same as control.**

Care is not the same as enforced perfection.

And restoration is not the same as reshaping something into another imposed ideal.

Sometimes the most meaningful act of care is to remove the restraints.

Then allow life to grow.
