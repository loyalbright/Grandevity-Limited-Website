## 1. Su Shi in Exile: The Making of a Literary Masterpiece

Su Shi (1037–1101), also known as Su Dongpo, was one of the most influential writers and calligraphers of China's Northern Song dynasty. In 1079, after the political prosecution known as the Crow Terrace Poetry Case (*Wutai shi'an*), he was demoted to Huangzhou, in present-day Hubei province. Cut off from the political center, he cultivated land, lived modestly, and found new depth in the landscapes and everyday experiences around him.

In 1082 he wrote two companion works: *Former Red Cliff Fu* after a seventh-month river outing, and *Later Red Cliff Fu* after a return visit on the full-moon night of the tenth lunar month. The site was **Red Nose Cliff (Chibi/Chibiji) near Huangzhou**, not the main historical battlefield of the Three Kingdoms Battle of Red Cliffs. For Su Shi, it became both a physical landscape and an imaginative stage on which to contemplate history and human existence.

## 2. Two Red Cliff Rhapsodies, Two Emotional Journeys

The earlier *Fu* moves from the beauty of moon and river toward philosophical dialogue about change and permanence. The later work is more dramatic and elusive. Su Shi meets two companions on the way home; an unexpectedly caught fish and wine saved by his wife make another excursion possible. At the cliffs he finds the river transformed by the season: water has receded, stones are exposed, and the familiar landscape seems newly strange.

Climbing alone through rugged rocks and tangled vegetation, he cries out into the mountains. The echo initially feels immense, but awe turns to sadness and fear. He descends, boards the boat, and lets the current choose the course. Then a solitary crane flies across the river—and later a mysterious Daoist appears in a dream. The work ends not with an explanation but with an empty doorway.

## 3. What Does the Crane Mean?

The crane's white and dark feathers, its piercing cry, and the Daoist's feathered garment invite an association between the creature and the dream visitor. Yet Su Shi deliberately leaves the relationship unresolved. The scene can be read through Daoist imagery of transcendence, the uncertainty of dreams, or the longing for freedom felt by a man living under political constraint. These are interpretive possibilities, not an explicit verdict supplied by the text.

## 4. Music and Cinematic Visual Storytelling

The composition unfolds in a flowing **6/8 meter at 72 BPM**, combining contemporary Mandopop and neo-soul with Rhodes electric piano, nylon guitar, subtle bass and brushed drums, cello, refined strings, guqin harmonics and bamboo *xiao*. A warm, mature AI-generated Mandarin baritone delivers the classical prose with restrained expression and clear diction.

The MV preserves visual continuity with *Former Red Cliff Fu*: the recognizable Su Shi, his companions, the small canopied wooden boat, warm paper lantern and misty Huangzhou cliffs. Its Northern Song setting shifts from ordinary human companionship to lonely mountain heights and finally to the crane and Daoist dream. Rather than illustrating a literal supernatural transformation, it preserves the source text's ambiguity.

## 6. English Reading Translation — Not Sung Lyrics

This literary translation is provided to help non-Chinese readers follow the narrative. **It is not part of the recorded song, which uses Su Shi's Chinese original only.**

It was the full moon of the tenth month that year.
Walking from Snow Hall, I was on my way back to Lingao.
Two guests followed me across the Huangni slope.
Frost and dew had fallen; the leaves were nearly gone.
Our shadows lay upon the earth. We raised our heads and saw the bright moon,
and, delighted, sang as we walked, answering one another.

Presently I sighed:
“Guests, but no wine; wine, but no food—
with so pure a moon and so clear a breeze, what shall we do with such a beautiful night?”

A guest said:
“At dusk today I cast a net and caught a fish,
large of mouth and fine of scale, like the perch of Songjiang.
But where could we find wine?”

I returned home to consult my wife. She said:
“I have a measure of wine, kept for a long time,
against just such an unexpected need.”

So we took the wine and the fish and once more went beneath Red Cliff.
The river flowed audibly; the sheer banks rose a thousand feet.
The mountains were high, the moon small; the waters had fallen, the rocks emerged.
Only a little time had passed,
yet I could scarcely recognize the mountains and river.

Gathering up my robe, I climbed,
treading jagged rock and pushing through tangled growth,
perching on rocks like crouching tigers and leopards, climbing roots like coiling dragons.
I reached toward a precarious bird's nest
and looked down into the imagined hidden palace of the river god.
My two guests could not follow me.

I let out a long, piercing cry.
Trees and grasses trembled;
mountains rang and valleys answered;
the wind rose and the waters surged.

Suddenly I too felt sorrow and a solemn fear,
a chill conviction that I could not remain.
I returned to the boat and let it drift into midstream,
resting wherever the current might carry us.

It was nearly midnight. All around was desolate and still.
Just then a solitary crane came eastward across the river,
its wings like great wheels, its dark lower plumage beneath a white robe of feathers.
It gave a long, clear call, skimmed past our boat, and flew west.

Before long the guests departed, and I went to sleep.
I dreamed of a Daoist in a fluttering feather robe,
passing below Lingao.
He greeted me with clasped hands and asked:
“Did you enjoy your visit to Red Cliff?”
I asked his name, but he lowered his head and gave no answer.

“Ah! Now I understand.
The one that flew past me, calling, on that former night—
was it not you?”

The Daoist turned back and smiled; I awoke with a start.
I opened the door and looked outside.
There was no trace of him.
