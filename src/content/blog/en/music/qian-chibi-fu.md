## Su Shi and the Historical Background

Su Shi (1037–1101), also known as Su Dongpo, was one of the Northern Song dynasty’s great writers, poets, calligraphers and artists. In 1079, his writings drew him into the politically charged **Crow Terrace Poetry Case** (烏臺詩案). Following imprisonment and interrogation, he was sent into restricted service at Huangzhou. The hardships of this period reshaped his literary and philosophical outlook.

On the **sixteenth day of the seventh lunar month in 1082**, Su Shi travelled by boat with companions near **Red Nose Cliff (Chibiji)** outside Huangzhou, in present-day Hubei. The excursion inspired *Former Red Cliff Fu*. He returned to the site later that year and wrote *Latter Red Cliff Fu* (《後赤壁賦》). Importantly, **Huangzhou’s Red Cliff is not generally identified with the principal battlefield of the famous Battle of Red Cliffs**. Su Shi’s references to Cao Cao and Zhou Yu evoke history through the landscape rather than establish the battle’s precise location.

In 1083, Su Shi wrote out the essay in his own hand for a friend. The celebrated manuscript is now held by the **National Palace Museum in Taipei**, linking the work’s literary and calligraphic legacy.

## The Journey from Sorrow to Release

The essay opens in serenity: a breeze moves across the river, yet hardly disturbs the water. Moonlight joins river and sky, and the tiny boat seems to float beyond the limits of ordinary life. Su and his guest drink, recite and sing, momentarily free of worldly cares.

Then a bamboo flute changes the mood. Its plaintive sound leads the guest to recall Cao Cao’s vast fleet and the brilliant rival commander Zhou Yu. If even the heroes of history have vanished, what hope does an ordinary human life have of lasting? The famous image of the **mayfly and the single grain in the sea** crystallises this grief.

Su’s response turns the conversation toward the river and the moon. Seen as change, everything is fleeting; seen from another perspective, the processes of existence continue without end. He does not claim that sorrow is unreal. Instead, he shifts attention from possession and permanence to the pleasures freely available in the present: the breeze one may hear, the moon one may see, and the companionship one may share.

The final lines are quietly transformative. The friends smile, drink again, and fall asleep aboard their boat, unaware that daybreak has arrived. Nothing has been conquered; nevertheless, a measure of inward peace has been found.

## Complete Original Lyrics — Sung in Chinese

The Chinese text below preserves the final approved performance wording and Taiwanese Traditional Chinese orthography, based on the designated *Siku Quanshu* edition of *Dongpo Quanji*, juan 33. Variant readings in other editions are not used to revise the finished song. Stage directions, opening captions and instrumental passages are not part of the lyrics.

壬戌之秋，七月既望，蘇子與客泛舟遊於赤壁之下。清風徐來，水波不興。舉酒屬客，誦明月之詩，歌窈窕之章。

少焉，月出於東山之上，徘徊於斗牛之間。白露橫江，水光接天。縱一葦之所如，凌萬頃之茫然。浩浩乎如馮虛御風，而不知其所止；飄飄乎如遺世獨立，羽化而登仙。

於是飲酒樂甚，扣舷而歌之。歌曰：「桂棹兮蘭槳，擊空明兮溯流光；渺渺兮予懷，望美人兮天一方。」

客有吹洞簫者，倚歌而和之。其聲嗚嗚然，如怨如慕，如泣如訴；餘音嫋嫋，不絕如縷，舞幽壑之潛蛟，泣孤舟之嫠婦。

蘇子愀然，正襟危坐，而問客曰：「何為其然也？」客曰：「月明星稀，烏鵲南飛，此非曹孟德之詩乎？西望夏口，東望武昌，山川相繆，鬱乎蒼蒼，此非孟德之困於周郎者乎？方其破荊州，下江陵，順流而東也，舳艫千里，旌旗蔽空，釃酒臨江，橫槊賦詩，固一世之雄也，而今安在哉？況吾與子漁樵於江渚之上，侶魚蝦而友麋鹿，駕一葉之扁舟，舉匏尊以相屬。寄蜉蝣於天地，渺滄海之一粟。哀吾生之須臾，羨長江之無窮。挾飛仙以遨遊，抱明月而長終。知不可乎驟得，託遺響於悲風。」

蘇子曰：「客亦知夫水與月乎？逝者如斯，而未嘗往也；盈虛者如彼，而卒莫消長也。蓋將自其變者而觀之，則天地曾不能以一瞬；自其不變者而觀之，則物與我皆無盡也，而又何羨乎？且夫天地之間，物各有主，苟非吾之所有，雖一毫而莫取。惟江上之清風，與山間之明月，耳得之而為聲，目遇之而成色；取之無禁，用之不竭。是造物者之無盡藏也，而吾與子之所共適。」

客喜而笑，洗盞更酌。肴核既盡，杯盤狼籍。相與枕藉乎舟中，不知東方之既白。

## English Literary Translation (For Reading Only)

**The following is an interpretive English translation for readers. It is not sung, added to the recording, or used as a replacement for the original Chinese lyrics.**

In the autumn of the year renxu, on the day after the full moon of the seventh lunar month, Master Su and his guest went boating beneath Red Cliff. A gentle breeze arose; the water remained unruffled. Raising his cup, he offered wine to his guest, recited the poem of the bright moon, and sang the stanza about the graceful beloved.

Before long, the moon rose above the eastern hills and lingered between the constellations Dipper and Ox. White mist stretched across the river, and water and sky met in a continuous glow. We let our reed-like boat drift wherever it might go, crossing the vast and trackless waters. How boundless it felt, as though riding the wind through empty space, not knowing where we would stop; how light and free, as though we had left the world behind and grown wings to ascend among the immortals.

We were delighted with our wine, and Su tapped the side of the boat and sang: “O cassia-wood oar and orchid paddle, strike the clear water and follow the streaming light. Far, far away my longing reaches, toward the beloved at the far end of the sky.”

The guest, who had brought a bamboo flute, played in harmony with the song. Its sound was mournful: like grievance and yearning, like weeping and pleading. Its lingering notes wound on as delicately as an unbroken thread, as though they could make hidden dragons dance in deep ravines and bring widows in lonely boats to tears.

Su grew solemn, straightened his robe, sat upright, and asked, “Why do you sound so sorrowful?” The guest replied, “‘The moon is bright, the stars are few; crows and magpies fly south’—are these not words from Cao Mengde’s poem? To the west lies Xiakou; to the east, Wuchang. Mountains and waterways entwine, dark and luxuriant. Was this not where Mengde was checked by young Zhou? When he conquered Jingzhou and descended upon Jiangling, his forces sailed east with the current. His warships stretched for a thousand li; his banners seemed to cover the sky. He poured wine beside the river and composed verse with his spear held across him. Truly he was a hero of his age—but where is he now?

“And you and I? We fish and gather wood on these riverbanks, keeping company with fish and shrimp and befriending the deer. We ride a small, leaf-like boat, lifting gourd cups as we offer one another wine. We are mayflies between heaven and earth, a single grain in the boundless sea. I mourn how brief our lives are, and envy the endless river. I would travel with flying immortals and embrace the bright moon forever; knowing this cannot be gained in an instant, I entrust the last echoes of my sorrow to the passing wind.”

Su said, “My friend, do you also understand the river and the moon? What flows away is like this water, yet it has never truly gone; what waxes and wanes is like that moon, yet in the end it neither diminishes nor grows. If we look at things in terms of change, heaven and earth cannot remain unchanged even for an instant. If we look at what endures, all things and we ourselves are inexhaustible—so what is there to envy?

“Moreover, all things between heaven and earth have their proper owners. If something is not ours, we should not take so much as a single hair of it. But the clear breeze upon the river and the bright moon among the mountains: our ears receive the breeze as sound, and our eyes meet the moon as colour. No one forbids us to take them, and their enjoyment is never exhausted. These are the boundless treasures of creation, which you and I may share.”

The guest smiled with delight. We rinsed the cups and poured more wine. The food and fruit were gone, and the dishes lay scattered. Resting against one another in the boat, we did not notice that the eastern sky had already grown light.
