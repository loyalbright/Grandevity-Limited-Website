### Li Bai and an Old Musical-Poetic Form

Li Bai (701–762 CE), courtesy name Taibai and literary sobriquet Qinglian Jushi, is among the most celebrated poets of the High Tang period.

He is traditionally called the "Immortal of Poetry."

His writing is renowned for imaginative freedom, musical language, emotional intensity and an extraordinary ability to move between exhilaration and sorrow.

During the Tianbao era of Emperor Xuanzong, Li Bai briefly served at the imperial court in Chang'an.

But he did not achieve the lasting political role he had hoped for.

He subsequently continued his travels and produced much of the poetry for which he is remembered.

*Gulang Yue Xing* is a five-character-line poem written under an older *yuefu* title.

*Lang Yue Xing* belonged to the poetic repertoire later collected among the "Miscellaneous Songs" in the *Yuefu Shiji*.

The fifth-century poet Bao Zhao, who lived during the Liu Song period of the Southern Dynasties, also wrote a poem under the title *Lang Yue Xing*.

Li Bai reused the old title but reshaped its emotional direction.

His moonlit vision moves from a child's delight to a troubling meditation on the loss of light.

The poem is therefore far more than a simple celebration of the full moon.

### When Was Gulang Yue Xing Written?

The exact year of composition is not securely known.

One influential later interpretation places the poem amid political unease near the end of Emperor Xuanzong's Tianbao era, before the An Lushan Rebellion began in 755 CE.

The Qing scholar Chen Hang, in *Shi Bixing Jian* (詩比興箋), associated the poem with concern over An Lushan's impending revolt.

According to this interpretation, the darkening moon may serve as a political allegory.

The loss of celestial brightness reflects anxiety about the political order.

Yet this remains an interpretation advanced by later commentators.

It is not a definitively established composition date or a personal explanation left by Li Bai himself.

The lunar imagery can also be understood more broadly, as a poetic movement from innocence toward disillusionment.

These readings need not be mutually exclusive.

But the distinction between historical evidence and later literary interpretation should remain clear.

### The White Jade Plate and the Mirror of the Jade Terrace

The opening lines present the moon not through astronomy but through imaginative discovery.

> 小時不識月，
> 呼作白玉盤。
> 又疑瑤臺鏡，
> 飛在青雲端。

A child sees the round brilliance of the moon and imagines a white jade plate.

Then another possibility appears.

Perhaps it is a mirror from the celestial Jade Terrace, flying among blue-green clouds.

The verbs "call" and "suspect" are central to the passage.

First, the child gives the unfamiliar object a name.

Then the imagination produces another explanation.

Li Bai does not insist that every child has actually seen a jade plate.

The jade plate is the poet's own imaginative comparison.

What matters is the sudden leap from what is visible to what might be imagined.

The moon becomes more than a distant celestial body.

It becomes an opening into another world.

### The Immortal, the Cassia Tree and the Jade Rabbit

The poem continues:

> 仙人垂兩足，
> 桂樹何團團。

An immortal seems to sit within the moon, with both feet hanging down.

A lush cassia tree spreads its rounded branches.

These images belong to traditional Chinese lunar mythology.

Then the poem introduces the jade rabbit:

> 白兔搗藥成，
> 問言與誰餐？

The white rabbit has finished pounding medicine.

But for whom is it intended?

That final question is unexpectedly intimate.

The moon is filled with immortals and magical imagery, yet the poet approaches it with ordinary curiosity.

Who will take the medicine?

Who is it being prepared for?

The distant celestial realm suddenly feels almost familiar.

Until this point, the poem remains bright, curious and full of wonder.

The emotional turning point comes with the next couplet.

---

### The Lunar Eclipse: When Brightness Begins to Disappear

> 蟾蜍蝕圓影，
> 大明夜已殘。

The toad consumes the circle of moonlight.

The great brightness of the night has been diminished.

In ancient mythological explanations, a toad was associated with the darkening or devouring of the moon.

Here, the once-whole lunar disk begins to lose its light.

*Daming*, or "great brightness," refers to the moon in this context.

The change is dramatic.

The same moon that invited childhood wonder has become a source of uncertainty and distress.

This is a mythic and poetic account of lunar darkness.

It should not be treated as evidence that Li Bai was documenting a particular astronomical eclipse on a known date.

The moon now carries an emotional weight that was absent from the opening lines.

---

### The Archer Yi and the Nine Sun-Crows

Li Bai then recalls another myth:

> 羿昔落九烏，
> 天人清且安。

Long ago, the legendary archer Yi shot down nine excessive suns.

In traditional mythology, the world suffered beneath the heat of ten suns, until Yi brought down nine and restored balance.

The phrase "nine crows" refers to the mythic sun-crows associated with the solar bodies.

Why does a poem about the moon suddenly invoke a hero who shot down suns?

The contrast is striking.

An ancient hero once removed a cosmic danger.

He restored clarity and peace to heaven and humanity.

Yet the present moon remains obscured.

Li Bai moves from the imaginative world of a child to a much larger vision of celestial order and disorder.

In some traditional interpretations, the contrast carries political significance.

In a broader poetic reading, it also suggests an unsettling question:

When something precious loses its light, who can restore it?

---

### The Yin Essence and the Loss of Light

> 陰精此淪惑，
> 去去不足觀。

*Yin essence* is another poetic designation for the moon.

The moon is now darkened and confused.

The speaker seems ready to turn away.

Why continue watching a light that has become so diminished?

Yet sorrow remains.

The poem ends:

> 憂來其如何？
> 悽愴摧心肝。

What can one do when sorrow comes?

Grief strikes the very heart.

The opening four lines are filled with possibility.

The closing lines are filled with distress.

Li Bai does not fully explain the cause of the grief.

That absence gives the poem much of its interpretive power.

Some traditional commentators read the eclipse as an allegory for a darkened political court and endangered order.

Other readers may recognize the broader experience of discovering that something once luminous can become obscured.

Both approaches can illuminate the poem, provided that a later political reading is not presented as an indisputable historical fact.

### 📖 Complete English Translation

**Li Bai — Gulang Yue Xing (Ancient Moon Song)**

When young, I did not know the moon;  
I called it a white jade plate.  
Then I wondered if it were a mirror from the Jade Terrace,  
Flying along the edge of azure clouds.

An immortal lets both feet hang down;  
How lush and rounded is the cassia tree!  
The white rabbit has finished pounding medicine—  
For whom, I wonder, is it meant to be eaten?

The toad consumes the circle of moonlight;  
The great light of the night is already diminished.  
Long ago Yi shot down nine sun-crows,  
And heaven and humankind were clear and at peace.

Now the moon, the essence of yin, is darkened and confused;  
Away, away—it is no longer worth beholding.  
What can I do when sorrow comes?  
Grief strikes me to the very heart.

*Translator's note: The English translation is supplied for literary understanding. The recorded performance is sung entirely in the project-established Classical Chinese text, not in English.*
