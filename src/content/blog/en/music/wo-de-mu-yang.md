Some people are not born stronger than everyone else.

They simply step forward when hardship is about to fall upon someone they love.

Some can put on iron armour and cross ten thousand miles of mountain passes.

They can also lay down the dust of war and return to one lamp, one meal and a familiar window.

**The Shape of Who I Am** is an early-2000s Taiwanese Mandopop narrative R&B song inspired by **The Ballad of Mulan**, the Northern Dynasties folk ballad also known as **Mulan Ci**.

It is not a word-for-word musical setting of the complete classical poem.

The song begins when Mulan stops her loom and discovers her father’s name upon the military summons. It then follows her beside the Yellow River, across frontier passes, through winter storms and ten years of war, before returning with her to the lamp that has waited at home.

This Mulan is not born without fear.

She struggles not to look back.

She sits alone in the midnight camp, measuring the road home again and again within her heart.

Yet she still chooses to stand at the front.

The lyrics directly quote several of the classical ballad’s most enduring lines:

> Generals die in a hundred battles;  
> brave soldiers return after ten years.

> The male hare’s feet scurry;  
> the female hare’s eyes are blurred.

> When two hares run close to the ground,  
> how can one tell whether I am male or female?

Within **The Shape of Who I Am**, these lines become more than symbols of military achievement, concealed identity or gender.

They return us to a deeply human question.

Must a person abandon tenderness in order to wear armour?

When Mulan returns to her former clothes, arranges her hair and places the yellow adornment upon her forehead, does the warrior she once appeared to be become a disguise?

The song answers:

**Neither side of her was ever an act.**

Walking into wind and frost for those she loves is her choice.

Returning to her family when the war has ended is also her choice.

What allows Mulan to travel through more than a thousand years of cultural memory is not only that she fought in her father’s place.

It is that neither departure nor return takes away her right to define herself.

### From the Loom to the Military Summons: Courage Is Not the Absence of Fear

**The Ballad of Mulan** does not begin upon a battlefield, beneath military banners or amid galloping horses.

It begins with the sound of a loom in an ordinary family home.

Mulan sits weaving, but the sound of the shuttle is replaced by her sighs.

The military rolls have been issued, and every copy carries her father’s name.

**The Shape of Who I Am** renders that turning point through four quiet lines:

> That night, the loom suddenly fell silent.  
> The military summons lay open by the window, its paper washed in moonlight.  
> Again and again, I made certain it was my father’s name.  
> And in that moment, I chose to make the long journey in his place.

The sudden silence is more than an atmospheric detail.

It marks the interruption of an entire life.

The unfinished cloth, the moonlight beside the window, the fear she has not spoken and the ordinary days that might otherwise have continued all come to a halt within that night.

The song does not portray Mulan as someone who sees the summons and instantly becomes fearless.

She understands what she is about to leave.

She also knows that she may never return.

She therefore sings:

> No one knew that when I crossed the threshold,  
> how much strength it took not to turn and look back.

True courage does not mean feeling nothing when faced with loss.

It means knowing the fear, attachment and danger clearly—and still choosing to move forward.

### Taking Her Father’s Place: A Hero Chooses Responsibility

Mulan does not first leave home in pursuit of fame.

She is not attempting to prove that she is superior to anyone.

Nor does she depart because she already sees herself as the legendary hero later generations will remember.

She simply sees a burden that is about to fall upon her ageing father.

The song states:

> I was not born stronger than anyone else.  
> I only chose to stand against the wind and frost  
> that should have fallen upon my father’s shoulders.

These lines express the song’s central understanding of heroism.

A hero is not necessarily someone born with an extraordinary identity that places them above ordinary people.

A hero may simply be an ordinary person who accepts a responsibility that could have been avoided when the moment is most difficult.

The chorus therefore does not announce that Mulan was born invincible.

She openly acknowledges the fear within:

> I crossed mountain passes,  
> and crossed the panic within my heart.

The mountain passes are the outward road.

The panic is the inward road.

What she crosses is not only a series of borderlands and harsh landscapes. She must also cross the part of herself that remains afraid, misses her parents and still has to keep moving.

When the song asks:

> If the meaning of a hero is to shoulder responsibility when the hour is hardest,  
> why distinguish whether the one who crossed wind and snow beside you was man or woman?

the central question is no longer whether Mulan succeeds in appearing sufficiently male.

It is whether human worth should be decided by gender, clothing or outward identity when everyone must face the same cold, danger and death.

### Ten Years of Beacon Fires and Unwritten Memorials

Traditional heroic narratives often compress war into a handful of spectacular moments:

departure, battle, victory and reward.

Ten years of war, however, cannot consist only of glory.

They also contain one winter after another, repeated farewells and people who will never return to their own homes.

**The Shape of Who I Am** states:

> Ten years of beacon fires, ten years of wind and frost.  
> How many comrades were never able to return home?

The song then quotes **The Ballad of Mulan**:

> Generals die in a hundred battles;  
> brave soldiers return after ten years.

Immediately afterwards, it adds:

> Those who survive also carry monuments without inscriptions.

The dead remain upon distant ground.

The living carry them back through memory.

The “unwritten monument” is not a literal stone engraved with names. It is the weight that those who survive must continue bearing.

This prevents Mulan’s homecoming from becoming a simple celebration of triumph.

When the final wisp of beacon smoke disappears, her first response is not exultation.

She can barely believe that she has truly walked out of the battlefield.

She brings home more than honour.

She carries exhaustion, grief, remembrance and the silence known only by those who have lived beside death.

### Armour, Former Clothes and the Yellow Forehead Adornment

One of the most familiar scenes in **The Ballad of Mulan** occurs after Mulan returns home:

> By the window she arranges her cloud-like hair;  
> before the mirror she places the yellow adornment.

**The Shape of Who I Am** reimagines the moment as:

> By the window I gently arranged my cloud-like hair;  
> before the mirror I softly placed the yellow adornment.  
> The brows and eyes in the mirror were still the same,  
> yet the gaze held more weathered years.

The face in the mirror has not fundamentally changed.

What has changed is the gaze.

Ten years earlier, she left through this window and doorway.

Now she sits beside them again.

She remains the same Mulan, yet she can never return completely to the person she was before the war.

The song does not present armour and feminine clothing as mutually exclusive identities.

When she wears iron armour, she is not pretending to become somebody else.

When she returns to her former clothes and places the yellow adornment upon her forehead, she is not rejecting the person she was on the frontier.

Her comrades, who have travelled beside her for more than ten years, stare in astonishment when they see her dressed as a woman.

Mulan simply responds:

> I merely smiled—which side of me had ever been a disguise?

This is the true meaning of the title **The Shape of Who I Am**.

A person does not have to possess only one fixed form.

Strength and tenderness can coexist.

Responsibility and exhaustion can coexist.

Departure and return do not have to cancel one another.

She can defend the coldest frontier.

She can also sit beneath the lamp and listen to her family speak of ordinary things.

Both lives belong to her.

### The Two Hares and the Right to Define Oneself

**The Ballad of Mulan** ends with the image of two hares running together:

> The male hare’s feet scurry;  
> the female hare’s eyes are blurred.  
> When two hares run close to the ground,  
> how can one tell whether I am male or female?

The ending carries the quickness and wit of an oral folk ballad, while also allowing Mulan’s story to move beyond a single narrative of war.

**The Shape of Who I Am** retains the hare metaphor and develops it into the lines:

> We had gone to battle together and passed through life and death together.  
> Who can judge who stands higher or lower by a set of clothes?

In the presence of genuine danger, clothing cannot shoulder responsibility on anyone’s behalf.

A title cannot determine who is more courageous.

Mulan’s comrades trust her not because they have mistaken her for a man, but because she has faced danger with them, defended the same ground, protected fellow soldiers and travelled through the same years.

The song does not deny Mulan’s identity as a woman.

It rejects the belief that ability, dignity and choice may be judged solely through outward appearance.

Mulan does not need to become a predetermined image in order to prove her value.

What she finally possesses is the right to define herself.

### Early-2000s Taiwanese Narrative R&B: Refusing the Oversized War Anthem

**The Shape of Who I Am** avoids grandiose historical spectacle, operatic singing and declamatory heroic scoring.

Its musical language is early-2000s Taiwanese Mandopop narrative R&B.

An 82 BPM mid-tempo pulse allows the long-form lyrics to breathe naturally. Fender Rhodes, nylon-string guitar, warm neo-soul bass and soft hip-hop drums establish a restrained, intimate and melodic soundscape.

Guzheng and erhu are not used merely to create a decorative surface of “ancient Chinese style”.

They appear with restraint at important emotional transitions, becoming part of the moonlight, river sound, winter wind and memory.

The female vocal remains clear, warm and controlled.

The verses approach quiet narration, allowing Mulan’s fear, longing and uncertainty to remain audible.

The pre-choruses gradually increase emotional pressure.

The choruses open into broader melody and harmony without becoming theatrical shouting.

The song’s power does not come from volume.

It comes from Mulan calmly saying:

> Then this time, let me stand at the front.

The final answer is equally restrained:

> True courage means walking the path you choose for yourself.

This treatment brings Mulan closer to a living human being rather than a distant historical emblem.

She is a woman capable of fear, exhaustion, grief and renewed choice.

### The MV: One Face Across Ten Years, from Late Autumn to Early Spring

The entire MV remains within a Northern Dynasties world, with Northern Wei culture serving as its primary visual reference.

The story begins during a late-autumn night in a fortified northern village.

Mulan is twenty-two years old, healthy and athletic, with a steady gaze and disciplined physical presence. She sits before a wooden loom within a home of rammed-earth walls, dark timber beams, lattice windows and a small clay oil lamp.

From the opening image, cold moonlight and warm lamplight establish the MV’s central visual contrast:

* **Moonlight** represents departure, winter, warfare and unspoken loneliness.
* **Lamplight** represents her parents, ordinary conversation, waiting and the place to which she may return.

Mulan leaves home in late autumn.

She spends her first night beside the Yellow River, crosses the northern passes and enters the deep winter of the frontier.

The battlefield is never treated as a fantasy world.

Military tents, lamellar armour, weapons, horse tack, rammed-earth border walls and timber watchtowers remain practical, weathered and restrained.

Mulan’s horse is a dark bay with a small white star upon its forehead.

It accompanies her departure, military journey, frontier service and homecoming, becoming a second quiet thread of continuity throughout the film.

Ten years later, Mulan has matured naturally.

Her features remain recognisable, but her face is slightly leaner, while her gaze carries fatigue, stillness and the gravity of survival.

She returns in early spring.

The family courtyard, wooden gate, lattice window, stone threshold and old loom remain in their original positions. Only the branches have begun to bud, while her parents have visibly aged.

This architectural continuity allows the decade to exist as more than dialogue.

The audience can see how time has altered Mulan, her parents, the courtyard and the way they look at one another.

The mirror imagery in the later scenes places the armoured Mulan and the Mulan wearing the yellow forehead adornment within the same face.

They are not two conflicting people.

They are the complete form of one life under different circumstances.

The ending contains no grand celebration.

The warhorse rests quietly outside the courtyard.

Moonlight falls upon the lattice window.

Inside, only one lamp remains, together with Mulan’s silhouette beside the loom and the return of ordinary life.

### Why Mulan Is Still Sung After More Than a Thousand Years

Mulan’s story does not belong only to an age of warfare.

It has endured because every generation can recognise its own questions within her journey.

When a family needs someone to carry a burden, how far are we willing to go?

When the world insists upon recognising us through only one standard, can we still preserve the whole of who we are?

When a long responsibility finally ends, do we remember the place to which we truly wanted to return?

Mulan’s greatness lies not only in surviving a military life beyond the ability of most people.

It also lies in her refusal to allow achievement, reward, the imperial court or later legend to take ownership of her future.

The emperor asks what she desires.

She seeks no high office.

She wants only to go home.

That choice is not lesser than her decision to depart.

Freedom is not merely the ability to travel towards distant places.

It is also the right to leave applause behind when the mission is complete and return to the life one genuinely values.

The final lines of **The Shape of Who I Am** state:

> What later generations remembered was not only that she once travelled far,  
> but that every departure and every return was decided by herself.

Departure is her choice.

Responsibility is her choice.

Homecoming is also her choice.

She does not have to remain permanently inside iron armour in order to prove her courage.

Nor must she deny the years of war in order to live quietly again.

True courage does not mean becoming the image that others expect.

It means understanding responsibility, cost, love and freedom—and still walking the path one has chosen.

The song moves through fear, responsibility, family devotion, departure, warfare, grief, identity, personal choice and homecoming.

### About the Project

**The Shape of Who I Am** is part of Grandevity Studio’s *Classical Literature Reimagined in Music* series.

The project seeks to bring classical literature beyond textbooks, annotation, recitation and memorisation, allowing it to re-enter contemporary melody, sound, visual storytelling and emotional experience.

For more than a thousand years, Mulan has been repeatedly written, sung and reimagined.

She is remembered because she served in her father’s place.

She is also remembered because she crossed the fixed boundaries through which society attempted to understand identity, gender and heroism.

Beyond every grand version of the legend, however, we wanted to see the human being:

a daughter capable of fear;

a soldier willing to accept responsibility;

a survivor who remembers her comrades;

and a person who, after completing everything demanded of her, still knows where she truly wishes to return.

May we possess the strength to enter wind and frost for those we love.

May we also possess the courage to hear our own hearts again when responsibility has been fulfilled.

**True courage is walking the path you choose for yourself.**
