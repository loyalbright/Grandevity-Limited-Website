Some things seem frightening not because they are truly powerful.

They seem frightening because we do not yet know what they can do.

> 虎見之，龐然大物也，以為神。
>
> The tiger saw it, an enormous creature, and took it for something divine.

A tiger that has never seen a donkey encounters a massive and completely unfamiliar animal.

It hides in the forest.

Watches.

Moves closer.

Withdraws.

Then the donkey suddenly brays.

The tiger flees in terror.

But the central change in the story is not that the donkey becomes weaker.

It is that the tiger begins to understand it.

Observation after observation.

Test after test.

Fear becomes familiarity.

Mystery becomes measurable ability.

Finally, the irritated donkey reveals its move:

> 蹄之。
>
> It kicks.

The tiger now understands:

> 技止此耳！
>
> Your skill goes no further than this!

### Liu Zongyuan and The Three Admonitions

Liu Zongyuan lived from 773 to 819.

His courtesy name was Zihou.

His ancestral affiliation was Hedong Xie, in the broader area of present-day Yuncheng and Yongji in Shanxi, while Liu himself was born in Chang’an, today’s Xi’an.

Together with Han Yu, he became one of the central figures associated with the Classical Prose Movement of the Tang dynasty.

Later literary history counted him among the Eight Great Prose Masters of the Tang and Song.

In 805, after participating in a court reform movement, Liu was demoted when that political effort collapsed.

His years away from the political centre, especially those associated with Yongzhou, became an extraordinarily important period of literary production.

His prose frequently combines precision, irony and an unsentimental view of human behaviour.

**The Donkey of Qian** belongs to the group of animal allegories known as **The Three Admonitions**, together with **The Deer of Linjiang** and **The Rats of a Certain Man of Yong**.

The animals are not merely animals.

They provide a way to examine human judgment.

### “There Were No Donkeys in Qian”

The opening is exceptionally short:

> 黔無驢。
>
> There were no donkeys in Qian.

Those words contain the psychological premise of the entire fable.

The tiger has no prior experience with a donkey.

It therefore cannot classify what it sees.

Someone transports a donkey into Qian by boat.

Once there, it proves useless to its owner and is released at the foot of a mountain.

To the human owner, it is an animal with no obvious practical use.

To the tiger, it is something else entirely:

> 龐然大物也，以為神。
>
> An enormous creature, taken for something divine.

The unknown magnifies appearance.

---

### When the Unknown Looks Divine

The donkey has not yet demonstrated any ability.

The tiger has already imagined extraordinary power into it.

It therefore:

> 蔽林間窺之。
>
> hides among the trees and watches.

Then:

> 稍出近之，憖憖然，莫相知。

It gradually emerges and moves closer, cautious and uncertain.

The tiger’s initial relationship with the donkey is controlled not by verified strength,

but by missing information.

---

### One Bray, and the Tiger Flees

Then:

> 他日，驢一鳴，虎大駭，遠遁。

One day the donkey brays.

The tiger is greatly startled and flees.

It even believes:

> 以為且噬己也，甚恐。

that the donkey may be about to devour it.

The scene is comic.

Its psychology is not.

When information is missing, imagination fills the gap.

And imagination often chooses the most threatening possibility first.

---

### Observation Begins to Replace Fear

The tiger nevertheless returns:

> 然往來視之，覺無異能者。

It repeatedly watches the donkey and gradually notices no extraordinary ability.

Then:

> 益習其聲。

It grows accustomed to the sound.

The bray that once caused terror becomes familiar.

The tiger moves in front and behind the donkey.

Still:

> 終不敢搏。

It does not attack yet.

This is what makes the tiger more interesting than a simple symbol of brute force.

It revises its judgment incrementally.

---

### Familiarity Destroys the Aura

> 稍近，益狎，蕩倚衝冒。

The tiger comes closer.

Becomes increasingly familiar.

Brushes against the donkey.

Presses.

Provokes.

The physical strength of both animals has remained unchanged from the beginning.

Only the tiger’s information has changed.

As uncertainty falls,

the imagined aura of power collapses with it.

---

### The Donkey Reveals Its Move

At last:

> 驢不勝怒，蹄之。

The donkey, unable to contain its anger, kicks.

From the donkey’s perspective, this is retaliation.

From the tiger’s perspective, it is evidence.

For the first time, the unknown creature reveals what it can actually do.

The tiger responds:

> 因喜。

It is pleased.

Because uncertainty has disappeared.

---

### “Your Skill Goes No Further Than This”

The tiger calculates:

> 技止此耳！

That is the true reversal of the fable.

At first:

something divine.

Now:

nothing more than this.

The donkey has not suddenly weakened.

The tiger has not suddenly become stronger.

Knowledge has changed.

The tiger now understands the donkey’s apparent limit.

It attacks:

> 因跳踉大㘚，斷其喉，盡其肉，乃去。

The ending follows from recognition.

---

### Liu Zongyuan’s Final Turn

Liu Zongyuan does not end when the tiger leaves.

He writes:

> 噫！

Alas.

Then:

> 形之龐也類有德，聲之宏也類有能。

Its huge form made it appear to possess virtue.

Its powerful voice made it appear to possess ability.

The crucial idea is appearance.

It seems.

It resembles.

But seeming is not being.

---

### If the Donkey Had Never Revealed Its Skill

Liu continues:

> 向不出其技，虎雖猛，疑畏，卒不敢取。

Had the donkey not revealed its skill, even though the tiger was fierce, uncertainty and fear might ultimately have prevented it from attacking.

This gives the allegory a sharper edge.

The donkey’s problem is not simply that it possesses limited ability.

Its apparent power survives only while its true capacity remains untested.

Once the boundary is exposed,

the illusion collapses.

Thus:

> 今若是焉，悲夫！

Now it has come to this.

How lamentable.

---

### Beyond the Idiom 黔驢技窮

The later idiom **黔驢技窮** is commonly used for someone who has exhausted a limited set of tricks or abilities.

But the original fable contains another important lesson.

The tiger is wrong at first too.

It dramatically overestimates the donkey.

Its initial fear is false.

What allows the tiger to escape that error is not force alone.

It:

observes,

waits,

tests,

learns,

and revises.

So **The Donkey of Qian** is not merely a satire of empty appearance.

It is also a lesson in responding to the unknown:

do not allow fear to finish your judgment for you.

Late autumn moving toward early winter.

Daylight gradually moves toward dusk without entering night.

The opening shows a mist-filled river valley.

A wooden boat arrives.

A sturdy grey-brown Chinese domestic donkey is released beneath the mountains.

Human presence then recedes.

The true visual protagonists become the tiger and the donkey.

In the early scenes, distance is essential.

The tiger remains partially hidden behind brush, tree trunks, stone slopes and shadow.

The donkey occupies more open ground.

After the first bray, the tiger flees.

With every later observation, the distance becomes shorter.

By:

> 益狎

the tiger has entered the donkey’s immediate physical space.

At:

> 蹄之

the donkey finally exposes its visible defensive move.

Fear becomes understanding.

> 噫！

the film returns to empty landscape.

Rock.

Autumn grass.

What do we fear:

real power,

or something we have not yet understood?

### From “Something Divine” to “Nothing More Than This”

The real drama of **The Donkey of Qian** is not simply that the tiger wins.

It is that we can watch judgment change.

At first, the tiger trusts appearance.

After the first bray, it trusts fear.

Then it begins to trust observation.

Finally, it trusts a boundary of ability that has been tested.

Liu Zongyuan therefore leaves us with more than a warning about empty appearance.

He also leaves us with a method:

first impressions can be wrong.

Fear can be wrong.

Reliable judgment requires observation,

testing,

and revision.
