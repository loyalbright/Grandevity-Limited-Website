More than thirteen centuries ago, the early Tang poet Wang Bo (王勃) visited Tengwang Pavilion in Hongzhou, in what is now Nanchang, Jiangxi. His visit became associated with two enduring works of Chinese literature: the celebrated *Preface to Tengwang Pavilion* (《滕王閣序》) and the eight-line *Tengwang Pavilion Poem* (《滕王閣詩》).

The prose preface is remembered for its magnificent descriptions of autumn scenery and its reflections on ambition, adversity, and human destiny.

The accompanying poem is much shorter—just 56 Chinese characters.

Yet within those eight lines, Wang Bo captures a profound contrast between vanished human glory and the enduring natural world.

## 📖 Wang Bo and the Four Paragons of the Early Tang

Wang Bo, courtesy name Zi'an, came from Longmen in Jiangzhou, corresponding to present-day Hejin in Shanxi Province.

He is remembered as one of the Four Paragons of the Early Tang, alongside Yang Jiong, Lu Zhaolin, and Luo Binwang.

These four writers helped shape a new literary sensibility during the early Tang dynasty, contributing to the development of poetry and prose beyond the conventions inherited from earlier court literature.

Wang Bo was recognized for his literary talent at an unusually young age. His official career, however, proved troubled.

After serving in the household of a Tang prince and later as an official in Guozhou, he became involved in a serious legal case. Although he escaped execution through an amnesty, he lost his position.

His father, Wang Fuzhi, was also implicated and demoted to an official post in Jiaozhi, a region corresponding broadly to present-day northern Vietnam.

Wang Bo subsequently embarked on a long journey south to visit him.

According to the commonly accepted chronology, in 675 CE, during the reign of Emperor Gaozong, Wang Bo passed through Hongzhou and attended a gathering at Tengwang Pavilion.

It was during this occasion that he composed the famous preface and its accompanying poem.

The precise year of composition has been debated in historical scholarship, although 675 remains the widely adopted date.

## 🏯 Tengwang Pavilion: From Royal Splendor to Literary Immortality

Tengwang Pavilion was originally built in 653 CE under the patronage of Li Yuanying, Prince Teng, a son of Emperor Gaozu, the founder of the Tang dynasty.

Standing beside the Gan River, with views toward the western hills, the pavilion became associated with banquets, music, dancing, and refined recreation.

By the time Wang Bo visited, Li Yuanying had long since departed from Hongzhou.

The pavilion remained, but its former royal patron was no longer there.

This distinction is important when reading the poem.

In its final couplet, the question “Where is the prince who once occupied this pavilion?” does not necessarily refer to the prince's death. Rather, it evokes his absence from the place that had once embodied his splendor.

The pavilion stands. Its former gatherings are gone.

It is precisely this contrast that gives the poem much of its emotional force.

## 🌤️ From Morning Clouds to the Passage of Time

The poem begins with a magnificent architectural image:

“The lofty pavilion of Prince Teng rises above the riverbank.”

It then turns immediately to the sounds of former celebrations—jade ornaments, bells, songs, and dances that have fallen silent.

In the next couplet, the pavilion's painted beams and pearl curtains frame clouds over the southern shore and evening rain descending upon the western mountains.

The building remains magnificent, but its human history has changed.

The poem then shifts from the passing hours of a single day to the passing of years:

“Idle clouds and their reflections drift through endless days; the stars have shifted—how many autumns have passed?”

This is the poem's great emotional turning point.

The clouds continue to drift. Their reflections remain upon the water.

Yet people grow old, powerful figures depart, and the seasons return without pause.

Wang Bo finally asks:

“Where is the prince who once occupied this pavilion?”

His answer is neither a historical explanation nor a moral judgment:

“Beyond the railing, the great river flows on.”

The poem concludes with an image of continuity beyond human ambition.

Nothing is forced. The river simply continues.

## 🌊 Wang Bo’s Final Journey

The poem takes on additional poignancy when considered alongside Wang Bo's own life.

Under the commonly accepted dating of the pavilion gathering, he continued south after composing his famous works, still hoping to visit his father in Jiaozhi.

According to the *Old Book of Tang* and the *New Book of Tang*, Wang Bo later suffered an accident while crossing the sea and died at a young age.

The historical accounts differ slightly in their descriptions of his death and his age. His exact year of death also remains disputed.

Nevertheless, the central irony is striking.

A poet who wrote so movingly about the passage of years would have little time left to experience them himself.

His life was brief.

His words survived.

More than thirteen centuries later, they continue to be read, remembered, and reinterpreted through new artistic forms.

## 🎼 Turning 56 Classical Chinese Characters into Mandopop Soul

The new composition is a cinematic Mandarin pop soul ballad at 72 BPM in 6/8 time.

Its arrangement draws on the warmth and melodic sophistication of early-2000s Mandarin pop, blending contemporary soul-ballad textures with restrained traditional Chinese instrumentation.

A mature male baritone delivers the original poem with clear Mandarin diction and emotionally controlled phrasing.

Elegant grand piano, expressive cello, lush live strings, rounded bass, and soft drums form the musical foundation. Subtle guqin and xiao colors add a refined historical atmosphere without turning the song into a theatrical period soundtrack.

The opening verses evoke the grandeur of the pavilion and the fading echoes of its royal gatherings.

The central couplet becomes the song's melodic heart:

「閒雲潭影日悠悠，物換星移幾度秋。」

The first chorus is intimate and reflective.

The second repeats the same melody with fuller orchestration, layered octave vocals, and a brief, controlled full-voice emotional lift.

The final two lines are sung only once.

Here the music gradually withdraws from its emotional peak toward a quiet resolution, leaving the listener with the image of the river flowing beyond the pavilion.

The production adds no contemporary lyrics, no narrative verses, and no lyrical paraphrases. The full sung text remains Wang Bo's original poem.

## 🌍 English Reading Translation — Not Sung

The following is a reading translation of the original eight-line poem, presented once in its classical order.

It is provided for literary understanding only. The recording contains no English lyrics.

**Tengwang Pavilion Poem — Wang Bo**

The lofty pavilion of Prince Teng overlooks the riverbank;  
The jingling jade and bells, the songs and dances, have fallen silent.

At dawn, clouds from the southern shore drift past its painted beams;  
At dusk, rain over the western hills meets its pearl curtains.

Idle clouds and their reflections linger through the endless days;  
Things change, stars shift—how many autumns have passed?

Where is the prince who once occupied this pavilion?  
Beyond the railing, the great river flows on.
