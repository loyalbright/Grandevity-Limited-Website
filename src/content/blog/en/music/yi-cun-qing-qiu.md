Some people stand upon a high tower and still cannot see home.

Others cross a long bridge, railway lights and a rain-washed city before the quiet sound of a bronze door ring finally tells them they have arrived.

**An Inch of Clear Autumn** is a Mandarin acoustic folk ballad inspired by Cui Hao’s celebrated Tang-dynasty poem **“Yellow Crane Tower.”**

The first verse preserves the complete original poem without alteration. Verse 2, the choruses, bridge and outro are newly written lyrics by Geng Yeqian, extending Cui Hao’s ancient homesickness into a contemporary world of bridges, railway tracks, glowing windows, an unsent message, a wristwatch, osmanthus shadows, a brass door ring and a single ripe pomegranate.

From the Yellow Crane Tower, Cui Hao asks:

> At dusk, where is my homeland?

More than a thousand years later, **An Inch of Clear Autumn** does not answer with a grand declaration.

It allows a window lamp to remain lit.

It lets osmanthus shadows fall across rain-washed steps.

It lets the bronze ring return softly against the wooden door.

And it allows the words that were never sent to be quietly gathered into the sound of coming home.

### Beginning with “Yellow Crane Tower”: A Poem That Opens with Absence

Cui Hao does not begin his poem with the pleasure of sightseeing.

He begins with someone who has already gone:

> The people of old have departed on the Yellow Crane;  
> this place is left with only Yellow Crane Tower.

The tower remains, but the legendary figure has vanished.

Between “the people of old” and “only the tower remains,” the poem establishes its deepest sense of time: what can be seen is still present, while what truly mattered can no longer be recovered.

The following couplet extends this loss into a far greater scale:

> Once the Yellow Crane departed, it never returned;  
> for a thousand years, white clouds have drifted on in emptiness.

The crane does not return.

The clouds continue.

Human departure is final, while the movement of the world remains indifferent to individual loss.

**An Inch of Clear Autumn** preserves these lines and the unresolved emptiness within them.

A genuine homecoming must sometimes begin by accepting that the past itself cannot be entered again.

---

### A Landscape Clearly Seen, a Homeland Still Invisible

Cui Hao continues:

> Across the clear river, the trees of Hanyang stand distinctly;  
> on Parrot Isle, fragrant grasses grow lush and deep.

The river has cleared.

The trees of Hanyang can be distinguished in the distance, while the grasses of Parrot Isle spread richly across the landscape.

Everything is visible.

The water, trees, island and evening sky are all arranged with remarkable clarity.

Yet the greater the visual clarity becomes, the more painfully the unseen homeland is felt.

The poet can see the river.

He can see the distant shore.

He can see the descending light.

He cannot see where he belongs.

This tension is not limited to the Tang dynasty.

A modern city may provide maps, live positioning, timetables, mobile screens and a wristwatch capable of dividing time into seconds.

Yet roads can be precisely marked while a true place of belonging remains difficult to name.

### “At Dusk, Where Is My Homeland?”: The Question the Original Poem Leaves Unanswered

The final couplet reads:

> At dusk, where is my homeland?  
> Mist and waves upon the river fill the heart with sorrow.

Dusk is not merely a time of day.

It is the moment when light begins to withdraw, direction becomes uncertain and the traveller realises that the day is ending before the journey has found its destination.

The question is larger than geography.

It asks:

What place truly belongs to me?

What light would allow me to stop searching?

When the remembered homeland has changed, can a person still find somewhere to become inwardly still?

Cui Hao ends with sorrow.

**An Inch of Clear Autumn** begins its modern continuation from that unanswered line.

### “Overnight Rain on the Long Bridge Washes the New Autumn Clean”

The expanded verse begins:

> Overnight rain on the long bridge washes the new autumn clean.

The Tang-dynasty river view enters the present.

The water beneath Yellow Crane Tower becomes a rain-washed urban bridge. The ancient traveller’s dusk becomes the hour of a modern journey home.

The rain is no longer falling heavily.

It came during the night and has only recently stopped.

It leaves the bridge reflective, cools the last warmth of summer and reveals the first clear texture of autumn.

Autumn does not arrive as desolation.

It arrives through clean air, wet stone and newly visible light.

The original poem moves from clarity into river mist.

The modern verse moves from the rain-washed city toward a lamp that can finally be reached.

---

### The Window Lamp and the Pomegranate

> At the market entrance, a window lamp illuminates a pomegranate.

The enormous river landscape contracts into a small domestic image.

A light remains in the window.

A single ripe pomegranate rests nearby.

Rain-softened light touches its surface, turning it into both a sign of autumn and the image of an illuminated heart.

The pomegranate is not decorative scenery.

It suggests ripeness, reunion and feeling safely contained within.

Bridges, railway tracks and city streets all express movement.

The window and the pomegranate express arrival.

Coming home does not always require a dramatic welcome.

Sometimes it is enough that one familiar object remains in its place and one light has not been extinguished.

---

### Osmanthus, Tea and Wood: Recognising Home Through Scent

The expanded lyrics continue:

> Osmanthus shadows follow the steps, holding drops like jade;  
> tea fragrance passes the shop and curls around the curtain hook.

The city is not only seen.

It is breathed in.

Memory is often awakened not by monumental architecture, but by a nearly forgotten scent:

osmanthus after rain,

warm tea passing through an open shop,

or the fragrance of a wooden door cooled by evening air.

The “scent of wood” that returns later in the song comes from the moment when hand, brass ring and wooden door meet.

Home is no longer an abstract direction seen from afar.

It becomes a material presence that can be touched and recognised before language is able to explain it.

---

### The Silver Screen, Railway Lights and Wristwatch

> Small words on the silver screen are moistened like marks upon a letter;  
> sparse lights along the railway flow one after another.  
> A faint glow on the wristwatch divides the seconds finely;  
> after the bronze ring has knocked, the scent of wood turns deep and quiet.

The words on the screen form a modern unsent letter.

They may be a message that was written but never delivered, or a confession that remained too difficult to send.

The phrase “marks upon a letter” places the cold surface of a digital screen beside the older world of paper, ink and rain.

Railway lights continue to pass.

The wristwatch continues to count.

The machinery of the city remains in motion.

But when the hand touches the bronze ring, time changes from abstract measurement into physical sound.

The ring meets the door.

The fragrance of wood rises.

The words on the screen no longer need to travel anywhere.

---

### “Before the Steps, an Inch of Clear Autumn”

The chorus sings:

> After rain, the long bridge is newly clear; before the steps, an inch of clear autumn.  
> The window lamp takes the road home and lights it into a pomegranate.

“An inch” is not a literal measurement.

It is an emotional scale.

Cui Hao looks outward from Yellow Crane Tower toward a world of thousand-year clouds, distant trees, fragrant grasses and river mist.

The modern song gradually narrows that immense space:

from the city to the bridge,

from the bridge to the street,

from the street to the doorway,

and finally to a small patch of rain, lamplight and osmanthus shadow upon the steps.

The world is vast.

A place of belonging may be very small.

It may be as small as one pomegranate.

One sound of a bronze ring.

One window lamp still willing to illuminate the road behind us.

### An Acoustic Arrangement Guided by Breath Rather Than Drums

The final production of **An Inch of Clear Autumn** is intimate, restrained and acoustically focused.

Its sound is built from:

* **A delicate, clear Mandarin female vocal**, maintaining natural diction and the closeness of a private performance.
* **Fingerpicked wooden acoustic guitar as the principal instrument**, carrying harmony, pulse and emotional movement.
* **Very subtle guzheng harmonics**, appearing only between vocal lines like momentary light upon rain-washed water.
* **A distant xiao flute**, used sparingly so that it never covers the words or turns the song into theatrical historical spectacle.
* **No drums**, allowing rhythm to emerge from fingerpicking, phrasing, silence and natural resonance.
* **Spacious pauses**, preserving the quiet of walking, waiting, remembering and standing before the door.
* **A poetic and restrained melody**, allowing the chorus to become memorable through return rather than dramatic force.
* **An intimate fading ending**, in which the final image of wood, brass, lamplight and autumn night disappears naturally into silence.

The production is neither an oversized historical anthem nor an operatic recitation.

It feels closer to a person quietly singing a Tang poem while walking home after rain.

### The MV: From the Tang-Dynasty River to a Modern Doorway

The MV moves through two distinct but connected periods.

The ancient sequence centres on the Yellow Crane Tower, the Yangtze River, white clouds, the trees of Hanyang, Parrot Isle and the mist of dusk.

Cui Hao appears upon the tower and beside the river. His homesickness is expressed through stillness, distant vision and silence rather than exaggerated performance.

The modern sequence enters an imagined contemporary Chinese city.

A woman in her early thirties wears an oatmeal-grey trench coat, an ivory blouse and a dark grey skirt. Her long straight black hair moves gently in the cool air after rain.

She crosses the long bridge, passes the elevated railway, moves through the market streets and climbs wet stone steps.

The message on her phone remains unsent.

The wristwatch continues to count.

Railway lights flow across the city as she approaches a modern Chinese-style residence composed of grey stone steps, a warm dark wooden door, a circular bronze ring, a glowing lattice window and an osmanthus tree.

The MV does not end upon the heroine’s face.

After she leaves the frame, the camera remains with the pomegranate, warm window, wooden door, stone steps, osmanthus shadows and motionless bronze ring.

Every emotion is gathered into a single quiet night composition.

The song fades within that final frame.

### Not a Replacement for “Yellow Crane Tower,” but a Continuation Beyond It

**An Inch of Clear Autumn** does not remove Cui Hao’s homesickness.

It does not rewrite “mist and waves upon the river fill the heart with sorrow” into an uncomplicated celebration of reunion.

The complete original poem remains.

The Yellow Crane still does not return.

The white clouds still drift.

The poet still stands beside the river at dusk, unable to see where home lies.

The modern lyrics allow someone from another age to receive that question.

We may no longer live in the place traditionally called our homeland.

We may travel through unfamiliar cities, bridges and railway systems.

We may write many words on a screen without ever sending them.

Yet when a window lamp illuminates the road behind us, when osmanthus shadows fall upon stone steps and when the hand recognises the wood of a familiar door, home may cease to mean only the place of birth.

It may also mean the place where we are finally able to stop looking into the distance.

### About the Project

**An Inch of Clear Autumn** is part of Grandevity Studio’s *Classical Literature Reimagined in Music* series.

The project seeks to bring classical Chinese poetry beyond textbooks, annotation, memorisation and isolated famous lines, allowing it to re-enter contemporary cities, music, imagery and emotional life.

More than a thousand years ago, Cui Hao asked:

> At dusk, where is my homeland?

Today, transport is faster, maps are more precise and cities are brighter.

Yet there is still no standard answer to the question of where a person may truly belong.

May everyone who crosses the long bridge and the autumn night eventually see a window lamp left glowing for them.

May every homesickness that cannot be fully spoken find a quiet resting place within an inch of clear autumn.
