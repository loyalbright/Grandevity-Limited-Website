“The setting sun melts like gold; evening clouds gather like a jade disc. Where am I now?”

A Lantern Festival can be the brightest night of the year—and the loneliest. Crowds gather beneath lanterns, carriages move through the streets, and laughter drifts from every doorway. Yet for a woman whose world has been transformed by war, exile and bereavement, the celebration may only make the distance from her past more painful.

## Li Qingzhao: from Northern Song prosperity to displacement

**Li Qingzhao (1084–c. 1155)**, also known by the literary name **Yi'an Jushi**, was born in the Jinan region. A gifted writer from a scholarly family, she became a defining voice in the Chinese lyric-poetry tradition known as *ci*. Her marriage to **Zhao Mingcheng** was marked by a shared interest in inscriptions, antiquities, painting and calligraphy.

In **1127**, Jin forces captured the Northern Song capital of **Bianjing** (present-day Kaifeng), an upheaval known as the **Jingkang Incident**. The Song court retreated south, while Li and Zhao were caught in the dislocations of the period. Zhao died in **1129**. Much of the couple's cherished library and art collection was lost or scattered over the ensuing years. Memories of a vanished home and earlier happiness became central to the emotional world of Li's later writings.

*Yong Yu Le · Sunset Like Molten Gold* is generally understood as a poem of remembrance written after the southern migration. Its exact date and place of composition remain uncertain, however; it should not be treated as a dated account of a single documented Lantern Festival evening.

## A festival of light—and a question of belonging

The poem begins with extraordinary visual splendour: the setting sun resembles molten gold, while dusk clouds glow like a polished jade ornament. Then comes the startling question: **“Where am I now?”** The scenery is beautiful, but the speaker can no longer take her place in it as she once did.

Misty willows and the plaintive flute tune *Plum Blossom Falling* suggest the arrival of spring. The holiday weather is gentle, yet the poet asks whether wind and rain may soon follow. After years of upheaval, the possibility of change has become impossible to ignore. Friends invite her out in fine carriages; she declines.

This is not simply an aversion to celebration. The city's joy has ceased to be the joy of the person she remembers being.

## “The prosperous days of Zhongzhou”: the old Lantern Festival

In the second half, the poem turns to **“the prosperous days of Zhongzhou”**, understood here as a recollection of the Northern Song capital, Bianjing. **“Three-five”** is a traditional shorthand for the fifteenth day of the first lunar month—the night of the Lantern Festival.

Li remembers women with time to prepare for the occasion, dressing their hair with delicate green-feather ornaments and *snow-willow* festival decorations. The colloquial phrase translated as “elegantly dressed” preserves a lively trace of the language of her own time. For a moment, the past appears complete, bright and tangible.

Then comes the devastating turn: **“Now I am worn and weary; my hair is dishevelled, my temples frosted.”** She will not go out into the night. Instead, she chooses to stay behind the curtain, listening to others laugh and talk. The poem speaks of personal loss and the historical rupture of a lost homeland without turning either into a grand declaration. Its force lies in restraint.

## 🎼 Music: Taiwanese/Hong Kong Mandopop Neo-Soul in 6/8

The song moves at **70 BPM in 6/8**, with a mature, warm and intimate female mezzo-soprano vocal timbre and carefully articulated Mandarin. Rhodes electric piano, nylon-string guitar, deep rounded neo-soul bass and brushed drums form its contemporary foundation. Cello and chamber strings shape the emotional arc, while guqin harmonics and occasional *xiao* flute colour the arrangement without overwhelming the words.

The opening is luminous yet distant. The memory of old Bianjing widens the harmony and warms the texture. At the words **“Now I am worn and weary,”** the arrangement contracts abruptly. The final original lines are repeated just once, more quietly rather than more grandly, before piano and cello carry the music to its close.

## 🎞️ The MV: one face, two ages, two Lantern Festivals

The music video unfolds across two imagined historical settings. In **Southern Song Lin'an** (modern Hangzhou), an older Li Qingzhao hesitates at the edge of the lantern-lit celebrations before withdrawing indoors. In the memories of **Northern Song Bianjing**, her younger self moves through an elegant Shangyuan festival, with fine garments, ornamented hair and streets alive with lanterns. The two ages share the same face, allowing the viewer to see memory and the present as parts of one life.

Architecture, dress, lighting and interiors draw on Song-dynasty culture, but the precise settings, facial likenesses and dramatic encounters are **AI-assisted cinematic interpretations**, not documented reconstructions of specific events in Li Qingzhao's biography. In the final image, the public celebration continues outside while she remains quietly behind the curtain.

## English Reading Translation (Not Sung)

The setting sun gleams like molten gold;  
Evening clouds gather like a disc of jade.  
Where am I now?  
Mist deepens among the budding willows;  
A flute laments the tune of *Plum Blossom Falling*.  
How much of spring is truly here?

It is the Lantern Festival, and the weather is mild;  
Yet might wind and rain not soon follow?  
Friends of wine and poetry come to invite me,  
With fine carriages and splendid horses;  
I thank them, but decline.

I remember the prosperous days of Zhongzhou,  
When there was leisure behind our doors,  
And we especially cherished the fifteenth night.  
We wore green-feathered festival headpieces,  
Gold-twined snow-willow ornaments,  
Each vying to be elegantly dressed.

Now I am worn and weary;  
My hair is dishevelled, my temples touched with frost.  
I dread going out into the night.  
Better to remain beneath the curtain,  
Listening to other people laugh and talk.

*For the recorded reprise, the final two Chinese lines are sung a second time. This translation is a reading aid and is not performed as a separate lyric set.*
