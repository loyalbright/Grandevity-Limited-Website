## Xin Qiji and Beigu Pavilion: poetry on the eve of war

Xin Qiji was born in **1140** near Jinan, then under the rule of the Jurchen Jin dynasty. As a young man, he joined anti-Jin resistance forces and entered Southern Song territory in **1162**. A major figure in the *ci* lyric tradition, he was also a writer of military and political proposals. His hope of recovering the north outlived repeated frustrations in public service.

The poem dates to **1205**, during the **Kaixi era** of Emperor Ningzong. Xin was serving in **Jingkou**, present-day **Zhenjiang, Jiangsu**, while the Southern Song court was planning an offensive against the Jin. Beigu Hill rises near the Yangtze, a place layered with memories of rulers, generals and contested borders. Xin supported the objective of recovering lost territory but warned against a campaign pursued without adequate preparation.

The poem is therefore more than an elegy for vanished heroes: it places military ambition under the judgment of history, and asks whether an aging patriot will ever be trusted to serve.

## Six historical allusions that give the poem its force

### Sun Zhongmou: where have the heroes gone?

“Across the ancient landscape, nowhere can I find the place of Sun Zhongmou.” **Sun Zhongmou** refers to **Sun Quan**, the ruler of Eastern Wu during the Three Kingdoms period. Jingkou was an important base in his domain. The dance terraces, pleasure pavilions and the glory of his age have vanished. The landscape remains, raising a difficult question: where are leaders with comparable courage and judgment now?

### Liu Yu, nicknamed Jinu: “golden spears and iron horses”

**Jinu** was the childhood name of **Liu Yu**, who rose from modest origins in the Jingkou region to become the founding emperor of the Liu Song dynasty. He led major northern campaigns, overthrowing Southern Yan and Later Qin and recovering important cities, including Luoyang and Chang’an. Xin’s celebrated image of mounted armies sweeping forward “like a tiger” praises Liu Yu’s achievement while revealing Xin’s own unrealized aspirations.

### The Yuanjia campaign: ambition without preparation

“Yuanjia, pursued in haste … only to turn north in panic.” **Yuanjia** was the reign era of **Liu Yilong**, Liu Yu’s son and the Liu Song emperor Wen. An ill-prepared campaign against Northern Wei in **450 CE** ended disastrously, and Northern Wei forces counterattacked toward the Yangtze. The phrase **“sealing Mount Langjuxu”** recalls the much earlier triumph of the Han general **Huo Qubing**, who commemorated victory on his northern campaign. Xin places a dream of martial glory beside a warning about rash action.

### Forty-three years: the fires of Yangzhou

“Forty-three years—and I still remember the fires along the Yangzhou road.” The number connects Xin’s arrival in the south in **1162** to the composition of the poem in **1205**. The reference to the Yangzhou region is not decorative nostalgia: it evokes the war and displacement that shaped his public purpose.

### The Foli Shrine: when historical wounds become ordinary life

**Foli** was a childhood name of **Tuoba Tao**, Emperor Taiwu of Northern Wei. Following the collapse of the Yuanjia offensive, his army reached the **Guabu** area on the north bank of the Yangtze. A site later associated with his temporary residence became known as the **Foli Shrine**. The poem hears crows scavenging offerings and drums sounding at local rituals. What was once a place marked by invasion has become part of everyday worship. The apparent calm makes the poet wonder how much history has been forgotten.

### Lian Po: who will ask whether he can still serve?

Xin ends with an allusion to the *Records of the Grand Historian*. The veteran Zhao general **Lian Po** was living in exile in Wei when the Zhao ruler sent an envoy to examine whether he remained fit for command. Lian Po ate heartily and mounted a horse in armor to demonstrate his vigor. A bribed envoy, however, falsely discredited him, and he was never recalled.

“Who is there to ask: Lian Po is old—but can he still eat?” Xin Qiji identifies with a man whose remaining strength is not the problem. What wounds him is the possibility that no one will recognize it or entrust him with a task. The grand historical panorama closes in one intensely human question.

## 🎼 Musical design: from martial drive to wounded restraint

At **80 BPM in 4/4**, this arrangement joins early-2000s Hong Kong–Taiwan Mandopop ballad writing to a Rock-Soul palette. A warm, mature Mandarin male baritone/low-tenor delivery keeps the classical text intelligible. Piano and Rhodes provide intimate harmony; electric and acoustic guitar, bass, live-style drums, cello, cinematic strings, and discreet traditional instrumental colours shape the wider arc.

The restrained opening gives way to a forceful rise at **“金戈鐵馬，氣吞萬里如虎”**. The harmony turns darker with the warning of Yuanjia and the remembered devastation of Yangzhou. At **“廉頗老矣，尚能飯否？”**, the voice and piano expose the poem’s vulnerable centre before the arrangement gathers one last surge of emotion.

In the outro, the original opening lines return: **“千古江山，英雄無覓，孫仲謀處。”** The same words now feel older, heavier, and more intimate. This reprise adds no newly written lyrics.

The music video imagines Xin Qiji at Beigu Pavilion in late autumn, between the fading warmth of sunset and cold moonlight over the Yangtze. His solitary presence is intercut with cinematic visions of Sun Quan, Liu Yu’s northern campaigns, the failed Yuanjia campaign, fires in the Yangzhou region, rituals at Foli Shrine, and the remembered fate of General Lian Po.

Costumes, architecture, river vessels, lanterns and weapons draw on historical visual references. ## English Reading Translation (Not Sung)

Across the ages, this landscape endures;  
Yet nowhere is the hero Sun Zhongmou to be found.  
The terraces of dancing and singing—  
Their former splendour has been swept away  
By wind and rain.

In the slanting sun, trees and grasses line ordinary streets;  
People say that Jinu once lived here.  
I think of those days:  
Golden spears and iron cavalry,  
A force that swept across vast lands like a tiger.

The Yuanjia campaign was undertaken in haste,  
Dreaming of the triumph at Mount Langjuxu,  
Only to end in a fearful glance toward the north.  
Forty-three years have passed;  
Looking out, I still remember  
The beacon fires along the road to Yangzhou.

How can I bear to look back?  
Beneath the Foli Shrine,  
Crows gather over offerings and drums sound for village rites.  
Who is there to ask:  
Lian Po is old—  
But can he still eat?

*This is an English reading aid, not an alternative sung lyric. The recording repeats the opening Chinese phrases in its outro.*
