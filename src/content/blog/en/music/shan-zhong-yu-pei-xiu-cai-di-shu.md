## Wang Wei and Pei Di: A Friendship Formed in the Mountains

Wang Wei, courtesy name Mojie, was an eighth-century Tang poet, painter, musician and government official.

He eventually held the office of **Shangshu Youcheng**, a senior position from which his later designation "Wang Youcheng" derives.

His deep engagement with Buddhist thought helped establish his reputation as the **Poet Buddha**.

His landscapes are admired for the way they convey both the outer world and a quiet inner life.

Wang Wei maintained a country estate in the Wangchuan valley, in the Lantian region of present-day Shaanxi Province, near Xi'an.

According to the *Old Book of Tang*, he and his friend Pei Di went boating, played music and composed poems there.

The two poets also contributed paired poems to the celebrated *Wangchuan Collection*, making this landscape an enduring place in the history of Chinese literature.

Their friendship was shaped not merely by shared literary interests but by the experience of returning to the same rivers, hills, paths and secluded places.

**A Letter to Pei Di from the Mountains** is an intimate prose letter, not a poem with a conventional stanzaic structure.

Its precise date of composition is uncertain.

The word *xiucai* in the Chinese title is a historical designation for a scholar and should not be treated as the equivalent of a modern academic degree.

What survives is a remarkably personal piece of writing.

Wang Wei is not addressing a ruler.

He is not preparing an official report.

He is writing to someone who understands the kind of beauty he wishes to share.

## Near the End of the Twelfth Lunar Month

The letter begins:

> 近臘月下，景氣和暢，故山殊可過。

The twelfth lunar month is drawing to a close.

The mountain weather is unexpectedly pleasant.

The old hills are well worth visiting.

Yet Pei Di is occupied with his studies.

Wang Wei writes:

> 足下方溫經，猥不敢相煩。

Not wishing to interrupt his friend, he decides to go into the mountains alone.

This is a modest detail, but an important one.

Their friendship is not presented as a demand for constant company.

Wang Wei recognizes that Pei Di has obligations of his own.

He sets out without him.

And it is precisely this solitary journey that leads him to remember the times they spent together.

---

## Ganpei Temple, the Ba River and Huazi Ridge

Wang Wei continues:

> 輒便往山中，憩感配寺，與山僧飯訖而去。

He enters the mountains, rests at Ganpei Temple, shares a meal with the monks and then departs.

The journey continues into the evening:

> 北涉玄灞，清月映郭。
>
> 夜登華子岡，輞水淪漣，與月上下。

The Ba River is associated with the eastern part of the present-day Xi'an region.

Huazi Ridge is among the places associated with Wang Wei's Wangchuan landscape.

The moon shines clearly.

The Wang River ripples below.

Its reflection seems to rise and fall with the moving water.

The phrase "與月上下" transforms a still celestial body into part of the river's movement.

The moon does not literally rise and fall with the waves.

Its reflected image does.

With very few words, Wang Wei makes water, light and night feel alive.

## Distant Fires, Dogs and Temple Bells: The Sound of Stillness

One of the most beautiful passages follows:

> 寒山遠火，明滅林外；
>
> 深巷寒犬，吠聲如豹；
>
> 村墟夜舂，復與疏鐘相間。

Fires flicker beyond the woods on the distant mountains.

Dogs bark in the deep lanes of a village, their calls fierce as a leopard's.

The sound of grain being pounded after dark alternates with occasional temple bells.

The striking quality of the passage is that the landscape is quiet, yet full of sound.

There are human beings living beyond the darkness.

Homes.

Work.

Animals.

A temple.

Each sound seems to emerge from a different distance.

Instead of destroying the peace, these sounds give the night depth.

Wang Wei creates an acoustic landscape long before the term *soundscape* existed.

For a musical adaptation, this is especially significant.

The original prose already contains a carefully balanced sequence of auditory images.

The arrangement can follow that sequence without inventing new words.

## "I Sat Alone": When a Landscape Becomes a Memory

The emotional direction changes:

> 此時獨坐，僮僕靜默，多思曩昔。

Wang Wei sits alone.

His attendant is silent.

The mountain night is peaceful.

But the quiet brings memories.

> 攜手賦詩，步仄徑，臨清流也。

He remembers walking with Pei Di.

Composing poetry together.

Following narrow paths.

Standing beside clear streams.

These are not spectacular historical events.

They are moments of companionship.

Their simplicity is precisely what makes them memorable.

The hills and water remain beautiful when Wang Wei is alone.

But they become emotionally different because he remembers the friend who once shared them.

The landscape is no longer only a place to be observed.

It has become a place where friendship lives.

---

## The Arrival of Spring—In the Imagination

The letter then makes a sudden but gentle transition:

> 當待春中，草木蔓發，春山可望。

When spring comes, vegetation will flourish.

The mountains will be lovely to behold.

This is an anticipated future scene, not an instantaneous change of season within the actual winter-night journey.

Wang Wei imagines:

> 輕鯈出水，白鷗矯翼，露濕青皋，麥隴朝雊。

Small fish darting out of the water.

White gulls spreading their wings.

Dew moistening the green riverbanks.

Pheasants calling at dawn among the wheat.

The images are full of movement and renewal.

The palette changes from winter moonlight and distant fires to green vegetation, flowing water and the warmth of morning.

Yet the spring scene is not merely a display of descriptive skill.

Wang Wei gives Pei Di a reason to imagine being there.

The landscape becomes an invitation.

---

## "Would You Come Wandering with Me?"

At last, the essential sentence appears:

> 斯之不遠，儻能從我遊乎？

The season is not far away.

Would you come wandering with me?

There is no command.

No elaborate celebration.

No promise of advancement or reward.

Only the hope of experiencing a beautiful landscape together.

Wang Wei continues:

> 非子天機清妙者，豈能以此不急之務相邀？

If you did not possess such a finely attuned and naturally perceptive spirit, how could I invite you to something so seemingly unimportant?

The phrase "不急之務" describes an activity that appears unnecessary or lacking immediate practical value.

But Wang Wei does not believe that such an experience is meaningless.

He says:

> 然是中有深趣矣，無忽！

Yet within this lies a profound delight.

Do not overlook it.

This is the quiet philosophical heart of the letter.

Not everything valuable must be urgent.

Not everything meaningful must lead to status or achievement.

There are forms of happiness that consist simply in appreciating the world with someone who understands.

---

## The Closing Words of a Private Letter

Wang Wei concludes:

> 因馱黃蘗人往，不一。
>
> 山中人王維白。

*Huangbo* refers to a plant known for its bark, used in traditional medicine and other applications.

Wang Wei sends the letter with someone travelling while carrying this material.

The expression "不一" is a conventional way of bringing a letter to an end without elaborating on every detail.

He signs himself as a man of the mountains.

The close is strikingly understated.

There is no definitive account of whether Pei Di accepted this particular invitation.

There is no constructed triumph.

The letter simply ends, as a personal message might.

Its lasting appeal comes from the emotional clarity of an ordinary wish:

To return to a place of beauty with a friend.

- Occasional temple bells
- Quiet memories of friendship

This distinction is essential to the narrative.

Plants begin to flourish.

Birds cross the water.

Dew settles on the grass.

The world seems to open.

These scenes are cinematic interpretations of Wang Wei's written imagery, not a documentary record of his actual movements or a claim that winter changed into spring overnight.

Tang-inspired clothing, buildings, boats, tools and lighting preserve the historical atmosphere, without modern roads, glass lamps, electric lighting or unrelated later-period decoration.

The central idea remains simple:

Even when travelling alone, a person may already be imagining the happiness of sharing the next journey with a friend.

## 📖 Complete English Translation of the Original Prose

**Wang Wei — A Letter to Pei Di from the Mountains**

The twelfth lunar month is drawing to a close. The scenery and the air are mild, and the old mountains are especially worth visiting. Since you are studying the classics, I did not dare trouble you. I went into the mountains by myself, rested at Ganpei Temple, shared a meal with the monks, and then left.

I crossed the dark Ba River northward, while the clear moon shone on the town walls. At night I climbed Huazi Ridge. The Wang River rippled, and the moon's reflection rose and fell with the water. Far-off fires in the cold mountains flickered beyond the woods. Dogs barked in the deep lanes, their calls fierce as a leopard's. From the village came the sound of grain being pounded at night, alternating with the occasional temple bell.

I sat alone then, while my young attendant remained silent, and often thought of earlier days: how we walked together composing poems, followed narrow paths, and stood beside clear streams.

When spring arrives, plants will flourish, and the mountains will be lovely to behold. Small fish will dart from the water; white gulls will spread their wings. Dew will moisten the green riverbanks, and pheasants will call at dawn among the ridges of wheat. That season is not far away. Might you come wandering with me?

If you did not possess such a finely attuned and naturally perceptive spirit, how could I invite you to something so seemingly unimportant? Yet within it lies a profound delight. Do not dismiss it!

As someone carrying huangbo bark is travelling your way, I am sending this letter with that person. I shall not write at greater length.

Wang Wei, a man of the mountains, respectfully writes.
