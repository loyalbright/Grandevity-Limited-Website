### Han Yu and the Young Scholar Who Headed North

**Han Yu (768–824)**, courtesy name Tuizhi, was one of the great prose writers of the Tang dynasty and a leading advocate of the Classical Prose Movement.

He was from Heyang, in the region of present-day Mengzhou, Henan Province, and became known to later generations as Han Changli.

He is counted among the Eight Great Prose Masters of the Tang and Song.

Han Yu argued that prose should carry serious ideas and moral substance rather than exist merely as an exercise in ornamental expression.

His writing is admired for its clarity, force and ability to create tension between what is stated and what the reader must infer.

*Farewell Preface to Dong Shaonan on His Journey to Hebei*, also known by the shorter Chinese title *Song Dong Shaonan Xu*, is an example of this literary precision.

Dong Shaonan came from Anfeng in Shouzhou, near present-day Shouxian in Anhui Province.

He had repeatedly failed to gain success in the *jinshi* examinations.

Unable to find a satisfactory path into official life, he decided to seek recognition in Hebei.

Han Yu himself had experienced examination setbacks.

He could therefore understand the frustration of a gifted scholar who had worked hard but found few opportunities to use his abilities.

At one level, the essay is simply a farewell from one concerned friend to another.

But Hebei was not an ordinary destination.

Its political context gives the essay a second, more complicated meaning.

The precise year in which Han Yu wrote the preface is not securely established. Its meaning is generally understood against the political circumstances of the Middle Tang period.

### Hebei and the Military Governors: Opportunity with Political Risk

After the An Lushan Rebellion, the Tang imperial court struggled to maintain effective control over several powerful regional military commands.

In parts of Hebei, military governors exercised substantial autonomy over troops, finances and official appointments.

Their relationships with the central government could be difficult or openly tense.

For scholars who had failed to gain recognition through the regular examination system, the entourage or administration of a regional military governor could offer an alternative career.

A governor might value their talents.

He might offer employment, influence or the prospect of advancement.

Yet accepting such opportunities could also raise questions about political allegiance.

To Han Yu, who placed great importance on the political and moral order of the Tang state, service under a powerful regional commander was not necessarily an uncomplicated success.

This gives the farewell its central tension.

Han Yu wants Dong to find recognition.

But he does not want his friend to lose the ability to judge the people who offer it.

### “The Lands of Yan and Zhao Have Long Been Known for Men of Heroic Song”

The essay begins:

> 燕趙古稱多感慨悲歌之士。

The ancient lands of Yan and Zhao were traditionally associated with people of bold spirit, deep feeling and a willingness to act according to principle.

The opening sounds admiring.

Han Yu then describes Dong:

> 董生舉進士，
> 屢不得志於有司，
> 懷抱利器，
> 鬱鬱適茲土。

Dong has sought success through the *jinshi* examinations but has repeatedly been disappointed by the officials responsible.

He possesses genuine ability, yet departs in frustration.

The phrase:

> 懷抱利器

literally evokes carrying a sharp instrument.

In this context, however, it is a metaphor for talent and cultivated learning, not a claim that Dong is travelling with a weapon.

Han Yu continues:

> 吾知其必有合也。
> 董生勉乎哉！

He expresses confidence that someone will appreciate Dong's abilities.

At this point, the encouragement seems sincere and unqualified.

But the next passage changes the direction of the essay.

---

### Can the Present Still Be Judged by the Past?

Han Yu asks:

> 然吾嘗聞風俗與化移易，
> 吾惡知其今不異於古所云邪？

Customs change under the influence of political order and moral teaching.

How can anyone be certain that the people of Yan and Zhao today are the same as those celebrated in antiquity?

This question is one of the most important turns in the entire work.

A region may have been famous for courageous and principled people in the past.

That reputation does not guarantee that its present rulers are equally worthy.

Nor does the promise of advancement necessarily justify the choice of one's employer.

Han Yu does not directly accuse the people Dong hopes to meet.

Instead, he leaves the uncertainty unresolved:

> 聊以吾子之行卜之也。
> 董生勉乎哉！

Let us use your journey to discover whether the old reputation still holds.

Dong, take heart!

The exhortation appears a second time.

But its emotional meaning has changed.

The words now carry affection, concern and doubt.

---

### The Lord of Wangzhu: Talent, Patronage and Betrayal

Han Yu next asks:

> 為我弔望諸君之墓。

He wants Dong to pay his respects at the tomb of the Lord of Wangzhu.

The title belonged to **Yue Yi**, the celebrated Warring States commander who served King Zhao of Yan.

Yue Yi received the king's trust and led successful military campaigns against Qi.

But after a change of ruler, political suspicion undermined his position.

He eventually left Yan for Zhao.

His story raises a question directly relevant to Dong's journey.

What does it mean for a talented individual to find a patron?

How secure is recognition when it depends upon the favour of powerful men?

And what happens when trust gives way to suspicion?

Han Yu's reference is therefore more than an invitation to visit an ancient tomb.

It asks Dong to remember that the relationship between talent and political power can be both rewarding and dangerous.

---

### The Dog Butcher of the Yan Marketplace

Han Yu also asks:

> 而觀於其市，
> 復有昔時屠狗者乎？

He wonders whether the marketplace still contains men like the dog butcher of earlier times.

The allusion recalls the world described in Sima Qian's *Records of the Grand Historian* (*Shiji*).

In the "Biographies of Assassins," Jing Ke is associated with an unnamed dog butcher in the marketplace of Yan and with **Gao Jianli**, a musician skilled at playing the *zhu*, an ancient stringed instrument.

The dog butcher and Gao Jianli are two different people.

Together, these figures evoke the bold, unrestrained spirit once associated with ordinary people in Yan.

Han Yu is not simply celebrating ancient adventurers.

He is asking whether courage and integrity still survive beneath the surface of contemporary political life.

The marketplace becomes a test of the old reputation of Yan and Zhao.

Do the people of the present truly resemble the heroes of the past?

---

### “An Enlightened Son of Heaven Is Upon the Throne”

The final message is:

> 為我謝曰：
> 「明天子在上，可以出而仕矣。」

Han Yu asks Dong to convey a message to the people he may encounter:

An enlightened Son of Heaven is upon the throne.

They may come forward and serve.

This is not merely a courteous farewell formula.

It expresses Han Yu's preference for service to the Tang imperial court rather than to autonomous regional power.

The words convey his political standpoint; they should not be mistaken for a neutral historical judgement that all contemporary political conditions were ideal.

The concluding sentence brings the essay's two central concerns together.

Han Yu understands Dong's need for opportunity.

He also believes that the choice of whom one serves matters.

The result is an unusually layered farewell.

It expresses sympathy for a frustrated scholar.

Admiration for courage and historical virtue.

And a warning against accepting advancement without considering political responsibility.

### Why the Same Exhortation Appears Twice

The line:

> 董生勉乎哉！

appears twice in the original essay.

It was not introduced as a modern musical hook.

The first occurrence follows Han Yu's belief that Dong will find recognition.

It carries hope.

The second follows the questions about changing customs and uncertainty.

It carries greater emotional complexity.

This distinction guides the musical setting.

There is no need to invent a modern chorus when the original already contains such a carefully positioned return.

The meaning of the repeated words changes because the journey of thought has changed.

### 📖 Complete English Translation of the Original Prose

**Han Yu — Farewell Preface to Dong Shaonan on His Journey to Hebei**

The lands of Yan and Zhao have long been known for men moved to lamentation and heroic song.

Dong has presented himself for the *jinshi* examinations, but again and again the officials have denied him success. He possesses fine abilities, yet in dejection he makes his way to that land. I believe he will surely find someone with whom he is in accord. Dong, take heart!

Given that you have not met with favorable times, even those who merely admire righteousness and strive to practice benevolence would cherish you. How much more so would the men of Yan and Zhao, if these virtues spring from their very nature!

And yet I have heard that customs change with the influence of governance and teaching. How can I know that what is found there today does not differ from what was said of old?

Let us use your journey to discover the answer. Dong, take heart!

Your departure has stirred certain thoughts in me.

On my behalf, pay your respects at the tomb of the Lord of Wangzhu, and look around the marketplace: are there still dog butchers such as those of earlier days?

Give them this message from me:

"An enlightened Son of Heaven is on the throne; you may come forward and serve."

*Translator's note: This English rendering is provided for literary understanding. The recorded song is performed entirely in the project-established original Classical Chinese, not in English.*

### A Farewell Without a Certain Destination

What makes this preface compelling is that Han Yu never dismisses Dong's frustration.

The young scholar's disappointment is real.

His desire to find recognition is understandable.

But opportunity alone cannot resolve every question.

Even after talent is recognized, a person must still decide whom to follow and what principles to preserve.

That is why the two exhortations remain so moving.

> 董生勉乎哉！
>
> Dong, take heart!

The first is a blessing.

The second carries concern.

Both are sincere.

The destination remains unknown, but the responsibility of friendship has already been expressed.
