The moon is not rare.

Neither are bamboo and cypress.

What may be rare is the ability, in the middle of a life that has not gone according to plan, to stop and truly see the moonlight, and to discover that someone beside you sees it too.

> 何夜無月？何處無竹柏？
> 但少閒人，如吾兩人者耳。
>
> What night is without moonlight?
> What place is without bamboo and cypress?
> What is rare are people with such leisure as the two of us.

**A Night Visit to Chengtian Temple** is a Mandarin male-vocal musical setting of Su Shi’s complete Northern Song prose masterpiece.

No modern lyrics, paraphrased verse or newly written chorus have been added.

Every sung word comes from Su Shi’s original text. Selected original lines, including “庭下如積水空明,” “何夜無月,” “何處無竹柏,” and “但少閒人，如吾兩人者耳,” are repeated only to create musical form and emotional development.

We have not written another chorus for Su Shi.

We have not given this night another ending.

The intention is simply to allow the moonlight of 1083 to leave the textbook page and enter contemporary melody, rhythm, sound and image.

### A Moonlit Night in 1083, Written During Exile

Su Shi wrote **A Night Visit to Chengtian Temple** in the sixth year of the Yuanfeng era, 1083.

Only a few years earlier, his life had changed violently.

In 1079, during the political episode later known as the **Crow Terrace Poetry Case**, Su Shi was arrested and imprisoned after his writings were accused of criticising government policy.

After his release, he was sent to Huangzhou as Assistant Military Training Commissioner.

The title sounds more powerful than the reality.

For Su Shi, it was essentially a nominal appointment attached to political exile. He was placed under local supervision and forbidden from signing or conducting normal official business.

He still possessed the status of an official.

He did not possess the authority normally associated with public office.

The move from political life near the centre of government to enforced marginality in Huangzhou was therefore not an ordinary transfer.

It was a collapse of position, expectation and identity.

The years in Huangzhou forced Su Shi to reconsider a fundamental question:

When rank, influence and familiar structures have been taken away, what remains capable of sustaining a life?

Some of his greatest writing emerged from this period, including the two **Red Cliff Rhapsodies**, **“Nian Nu Jiao: Remembering the Past at Red Cliff,”** and works associated with the emotional and philosophical transformation of his Huangzhou years.

Yet **A Night Visit to Chengtian Temple** contains no grand river, battlefield or historical meditation.

It records something much smaller.

A man has taken off his clothes and is preparing to sleep.

Then moonlight enters the room.

### “Moonlight Entered the Door, and I Rose in Delight”

> 解衣欲睡，月色入戶，欣然起行。
>
> I had undressed and was about to sleep when moonlight entered the doorway. Delighted, I rose and went out.

The word that matters here is **欣然**, delightedly, gladly.

The person who writes this is not a man enjoying effortless success.

He is a politically displaced official living in Huangzhou without meaningful administrative authority.

Yet moonlight is still enough to move him.

The external world may restrict office, rank and circumstance.

It cannot necessarily decide whether a person remains capable of being surprised by beauty.

That is one of the deepest transformations of Su Shi’s Huangzhou years.

Difficulty remains real.

But difficulty does not have to become the whole of life.

---

### “I Thought There Was No One with Whom to Share the Pleasure”

> 念無與為樂者，
> 遂至承天寺，尋張懷民。
>
> I thought there was no one with whom to share this pleasure, so I went to Chengtian Temple to find Zhang Huaimin.

After seeing the moon, Su Shi does not simply go outside alone.

His next thought is relational.

This night would be better if there were someone with whom to share it.

So he thinks of Zhang Huaimin.

That decision quietly transforms the piece from a solitary night walk into a work about companionship.

The truly moving moment comes when Su Shi reaches the temple:

> 懷民亦未寢。
>
> Huaimin, too, had not yet gone to sleep.

There is no long conversation.

No explanation.

Su Shi does not tell us why Zhang Huaimin is awake.

One man arrives late at night.

The other happens to be awake.

Then:

> 相與步於中庭。
>
> Together we walked in the central courtyard.

The phrase is almost weightless.

And that is why it is so powerful.

Some friendships do not require elaborate declarations.

What matters is knowing whom to seek when the world suddenly becomes beautiful and finding that person awake.

---

### “The Courtyard Was Like Clear Water Pooled in Emptiness”

The most celebrated visual passage in the work is astonishingly brief:

> 庭下如積水空明，
> 水中藻荇交橫，
> 蓋竹柏影也。

There is no water in the courtyard.

Su Shi first makes us see the ground as a pool of luminous, transparent water.

Then he introduces what appear to be aquatic plants crossing beneath its surface.

Only at the end does he reveal the truth:

They are the shadows of bamboo and cypress.

The brilliance of the passage lies in its refusal to say merely:

The moon was beautiful.

The shadows were beautiful.

Instead, perception transforms the courtyard.

Moonlight becomes water.

Shadows become water plants.

An ordinary temple courtyard becomes an almost transparent world.

The term **空明**, often rendered as clear, empty, luminous or crystalline, describes the quality of the moonlit ground.

But it also feels remarkably close to the inner clarity Su Shi was learning to cultivate in Huangzhou.

There are still shadows.

The shadows simply no longer prevent the world from being luminous.

---

### “Only People with Leisure Like the Two of Us Are Rare”

The work ends:

> 何夜無月？
> 何處無竹柏？
> 但少閒人，如吾兩人者耳。

This is far more complicated than a simple celebration of leisure.

The word **閒人** can suggest people who have time to spare.

But in Su Shi’s circumstances, it carries another edge.

He himself had been made “idle” by political reality.

A man who had once expected to participate actively in public affairs now held a nominal post in Huangzhou without meaningful official power.

“Leisure,” therefore, contains self-mockery.

It contains exclusion.

It contains political disappointment.

Yet Su Shi does not leave the word there.

The very condition that removed him from political urgency also gave him the temporal and psychological space to walk through Chengtian Temple on an ordinary night and see something others might pass without noticing.

The “idle man” therefore becomes something else as well:

a person who retains enough inner space to perceive.

The ending contains disappointment and freedom at the same time.

Bitterness and pleasure.

Political marginality and spiritual independence.

This tension is one reason the final sentence remains unmistakably Su Shi.

### Melancholic Mandopop R&B: Giving a Nine-Centuries-Old Night a Contemporary Pulse

The original prose is extremely short.

Read aloud at ordinary speed, it is over almost immediately.

Rather than adding modern lyrics to extend the song, this setting builds musical duration through selective repetition of Su Shi’s own lines, instrumental passages, silence, harmonic development and changing texture.

The sound centres on **Melancholic Mandopop R&B / Dynamic 90s Urban Groove / Chinese Fusion**:

- Acoustic guitar provides the warm, intimate backbone associated with 1990s Mandarin pop.
- Guzheng introduces an unmistakably Chinese melodic colour without pushing the arrangement toward theatrical ancient-style convention.
- Sweeping strings gradually widen the emotional scale of the moonlit courtyard.
- Subtle vinyl crackle creates a sense of memory, age and late-night intimacy.
- An R&B rhythm section keeps the Classical Chinese text moving as song rather than accompanied recitation.
- Layered male harmonies and controlled falsetto runs create space around a very short text without adding new words.

At:

> 庭下如積水空明！

the arrangement opens for the first major emotional expansion.

At:

> 何夜無月？何處無竹柏？

the music begins to move beyond one historical courtyard.

The question becomes universal.

And when the song reaches:

> 但少閒人，如吾兩人者耳。

no modern answer is necessary.

Su Shi has already written the ending.

### The MV: From “Moonlight Entered the Door” to “People Like the Two of Us”

The music video remains entirely within a Northern Song visual world.

There is no modern parallel narrative.

No time travel.

No later-dynasty costume vocabulary.

No fantasy or xianxia aesthetic.

Su Shi is portrayed at approximately forty-six years of age: lean, refined and contemplative, carrying the quiet severity of a man whose public life has been abruptly altered.

The opening begins with Huangzhou under a clear late-autumn moon.

The camera enters his residence.

He is preparing to sleep.

Moonlight crosses the wooden lattice and falls into the room.

He rises.

Leaves.

Walks through the silent streets.

Approaches Chengtian Temple.

Lighting throughout the temple sequence is restricted to historically appropriate sources: oil lamps, paper-covered lanterns and traditional flame lighting. No glass lamp shades, electrical lighting or modern fixtures appear.

When Zhang Huaimin enters the story, the visual grammar changes from solitude to companionship.

The two men do very little.

They walk.

Pause.

Look upward.

Look downward.

Observe bamboo and cypress.

Observe their shadows.

During “庭下如積水空明,” the stone courtyard becomes visually water-like beneath the moon.

The interlaced shadows of bamboo and cypress create the illusion of aquatic plants crossing a clear pool.

Toward the ending, the two men become smaller within the architecture.

The courtyard grows quieter again.

Moonlight remains.

Shadows remain.

The roofline remains.

Two figures remain beneath the moon.

Because what survives from this night is not an event.

It is a state of mind.

### Perhaps What Is Rare Is Not the Moon

**A Night Visit to Chengtian Temple** is very short.

Its entire narrative can almost be reduced to one sentence:

Su Shi sees moonlight at night, goes to Chengtian Temple to find Zhang Huaimin, and the two walk together in the courtyard.

There is no dramatic conflict.

No reversal of fate.

No grand conclusion.

Yet more than nine centuries later, the work remains unforgettable.

Perhaps because our lives have never lacked moonlight.

Nor bamboo.

Nor shadows.

What we lack is often the time and the inner availability to see them.

Rarer still is the moment when we wish to share that moonlight with someone, and discover that the other person is awake.

Su Shi does not end by claiming that his troubles have disappeared.

He says only:

> 但少閒人，如吾兩人者耳。

Perhaps freedom does not require the world finally to behave as we wish.

Perhaps it begins when, even in a life that has not gone according to plan, a small space remains clear.

Clear enough to see the moon.

Clear enough to see bamboo shadows.

Clear enough to know whom we want beside us.

### About This Project

**A Night Visit to Chengtian Temple** is part of Grandevity Studio’s **Classical Literature Reimagined in Music** project.

The aim is to allow Classical Chinese literature to live beyond textbooks, annotations, translation, recitation and examination.

Some texts do not need to be rewritten.

Their language is already precise enough.

Beautiful enough.

Powerful enough.

What may need to change is simply the way we approach them.

On a night in Huangzhou in 1083, a man pushed to the political margins saw moonlight enter his room.

He did not close the window.

He rose.

Went outside.

And searched for another person who was still awake.

More than nine centuries later, we remain surrounded by work, anxiety, ambition, loss, information and endless demands upon attention.

The moon is still there.

Perhaps the question is still the same:

Can we still see it?

And when we do,

is there still someone beside us who can see it too?
