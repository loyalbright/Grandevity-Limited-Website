Some people fear that asking questions will diminish their standing. A seventeen-year-old scholar chose to learn anyway.

In his renowned Tang-dynasty essay *Shi Shuo* (*On Teachers*), Han Yu challenges the idea that social position, age or prestige determines whose knowledge matters. More than twelve centuries later, his argument remains startlingly direct: **a teacher is found wherever there is something worth learning.**

## Han Yu, the Tang court, and a culture reluctant to learn

Han Yu (768–824), courtesy name Tuizhi, was a leading writer and thinker of the Middle Tang period. Associated with the Classical Prose Movement, he argued for prose grounded in ideas and ethical purpose rather than literary ornament alone. Later generations counted him among the Eight Great Prose Masters of the Tang and Song.

*Shi Shuo* is generally dated to around 802 CE, when Han Yu served as a Four Gates Doctor (*simen boshi*) in the imperial educational establishment. Some scholar-officials regarded learning from another person as a loss of dignity—particularly when the prospective teacher was younger or lower in official standing. Han Yu disputes this social habit and replaces it with an uncompromising criterion: whoever understands something worth knowing may be a teacher.

The essay reveals its personal occasion at the end. Li Pan, a seventeen-year-old who loved classical prose and had studied the Confucian Classics and their commentaries, was willing to learn from Han Yu despite prevailing attitudes. Han Yu admired him and wrote the essay as a gift. Relatively little is securely known of Li Pan’s subsequent life; it is the courage of his choice that endures in literature.

## “Passing on the Way, teaching, and resolving doubts”

The opening sentence—“Those who sought learning in antiquity all had teachers”—makes education a universal human need. Han Yu defines a teacher as someone who conveys the Way, imparts learning and resolves uncertainty. The point is greater than literacy or memorization: education also involves reasoning, judgment and the humility to recognize what one does not yet understand.

The essay then unfolds through contrasts. Ancient sages continued to seek instruction; some later scholars refused. Parents hired tutors for their children but were too proud to ask for help themselves. Ritual specialists, physicians, musicians and artisans could exchange skills freely, while certain scholar-officials mocked anyone who dared to call another person “teacher.”

Han Yu invokes traditions about Confucius learning from Tanzi, Chang Hong, Shi Xiang and Lao Dan, leading to his most expansive conclusion: a student need not be inferior to a teacher, and a teacher need not excel beyond a student in every field. People encounter understanding at different times, and different disciplines demand different kinds of mastery.

The reference to the “Six Arts” in Li Pan’s description concerns the Confucian Classics and their commentarial tradition in this context, not a list of modern extracurricular activities.

## The music: a prose argument that sings without a manufactured chorus

At 74 BPM in 4/4, the arrangement combines intimate Chinese acoustic folk with polished Mandopop production. Fingerpicked wooden guitar leads a restrained palette of guqin harmonics, xiao, cello, gentle acoustic bass and brushed percussion. A warm, clear Mandarin male voice follows the rhythm and phrasing of the Classical Chinese, favoring quiet conviction over theatrical singing.

Han Yu’s sighs of dismay—*jiehu* (嗟乎) and *wuhu* (嗚呼)—create natural changes in emotional intensity. The music moves from measured argument to social criticism, from Confucius’ example to the tenderness of the dedication to Li Pan. Short instrumental passages, silence and sustained endings give the work breadth without introducing additional lyrics.

## The MV: learning across generations

Most scenes imagine Chang’an around 802 CE: Han Yu and Li Pan study together in a wooden hall and autumn courtyard. Artisans, musicians and a healer demonstrate how expertise circulates beyond rank. When the lyric invokes Confucius, the video shifts to a Spring and Autumn–period setting, dramatizing traditional accounts of the sage seeking knowledge from different teachers.

Costumes, buildings, tools and manuscripts are designed to suggest the appropriate periods. Character appearances and reconstructed conversations are creative interpretations, not documented records of particular encounters. In the final images, Han Yu gives Li Pan the scroll, and the young student continues into a sunlit street—a visual reminder that learning does not end when a lesson does.

## English Reading Translation | Not Sung

The following is a reading translation to aid understanding. The actual recording is performed in the original Classical Chinese only.

Those who sought learning in antiquity all had teachers. A teacher is someone through whom one comes to understand the Way, receives instruction, and resolves doubts. No one is born knowing everything; who can be free of uncertainty? Yet if a person has doubts and will not learn from a teacher, those doubts will remain unresolved.

If someone was born before me and understood the Way before I did, I follow that person and take them as my teacher. If someone was born after me but has already understood the Way, I likewise follow that person as my teacher. I seek to learn the Way; why should I concern myself with whether that person was born earlier or later than I? Thus, neither high rank nor low rank, neither age nor youth, determines who may teach. Wherever the Way is found, there a teacher is found.

Alas! The tradition of learning from teachers has long been neglected. How difficult it has become for people to free themselves from confusion! The sages of antiquity stood far above ordinary people in understanding, yet still sought teachers and asked questions. People today, though far beneath those sages, are ashamed to learn from a teacher. Thus the wise become wiser, and the foolish more foolish. Is this not why the sages are sages and the foolish remain foolish?

People love their children and choose teachers to instruct them; yet when it comes to themselves, they are ashamed to seek teachers. How bewildering! The teachers who teach children to read books and divide sentences are not the teachers I mean when I speak of passing on the Way and resolving deeper doubts. When someone cannot divide a sentence correctly, they seek a teacher; when they cannot resolve a question of principle, they do not. They attend to lesser learning while abandoning what matters greatly. I cannot call that wise.

Ritual specialists, physicians, musicians, and artisans are not ashamed to learn from one another. Among the scholar-officials, however, if someone speaks of teachers and disciples, others gather to laugh. Ask them why, and they reply: “Those two are about the same age, and their understanding is much alike. To learn from a person of lower standing would be humiliating; to learn from an official of higher rank would seem like flattery.” Alas! One can see why the way of teaching has declined. People whom gentlemen refuse to acknowledge now surpass those same gentlemen in understanding. How extraordinary this is!

Sages do not have one fixed teacher. Confucius learned from Tanzi, Chang Hong, Shi Xiang, and Lao Dan. People such as Tanzi did not surpass Confucius in overall virtue or achievement. Yet Confucius said: “When three people walk together, there is surely someone among them from whom I can learn.” A disciple, then, need not be inferior to a teacher, nor must a teacher be superior to a disciple in every respect. Some encounter the Way earlier, others later; different fields of knowledge and skill call for different specializations. That is all.

Li Pan, a young man of seventeen, loved ancient prose and had studied the Confucian Classics and their commentaries broadly. Unconstrained by the prevailing fashion, he came to learn from me. Because I admired his willingness to follow the old practice of seeking instruction, I wrote *Shi Shuo* and presented it to him.
