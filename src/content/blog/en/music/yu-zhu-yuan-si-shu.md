> 風煙俱淨，天山共色。

The haze has cleared. The sky and mountains seem to share one colour.

A small boat follows the river between Fuyang and Tonglu, moving wherever the current carries it. Beneath the surface, fish and small stones appear almost impossibly clear. Ahead, rapids race like arrows, waves surge like galloping horses, and mountains rise as if competing to touch the sky.

This is the world of **A Letter to Zhu Yuansi** (*與朱元思書*), one of the best-known passages of Chinese landscape prose, traditionally attributed to **Wu Jun (469–520)** of the Southern Liang dynasty.

The passage does more than describe a scenic journey. It moves from visual beauty to sound—and, eventually, from sound to a transformation of the mind.

> 鳶飛戾天者，望峰息心；經綸世務者，窺谷忘反。

Those driven to soar after worldly success find their ambition quietened by the mountains. Those occupied with governing affairs look into the valleys and forget to return.

Grandevity Studio has set the original Classical Chinese text to a new **4-minute, 12-second** musical work. No modern verses, paraphrased lyrics, fictional dialogue or added chorus replace the original. The river, mountains, water and sounds already provide the structure.

## Wu Jun: A Writer of the Southern Liang Dynasty

**Wu Jun**, courtesy name Shuxiang, lived around **469–520 CE**. He came from Guzhang in Wuxing, broadly corresponding to the modern Anji area of Zhejiang Province. He was a writer and historian active during the Liang dynasty, one of the Southern Dynasties that ruled parts of southern China during the larger era of Northern and Southern division.

Wu Jun became known for prose of clarity, elevation and distinctive rhythm. The influence of his style was strong enough for later readers to describe an identifiable *Wu Jun style* (*Wu Jun ti*).

Chinese landscape writing was developing rapidly during his era. Mountains and rivers were no longer merely backdrops for political events or the movements of historical figures; the experience of viewing nature became a subject with aesthetic and philosophical significance of its own.

The work is popularly published as **Yu Zhu Yuansi Shu** (*A Letter to Zhu Yuansi*). Some transmitted textual collections, including *Liu Chao Wen Jie*, carry the title **Yu Song Yuansi Shu** (*A Letter to Song Yuansi*). These differences reflect textual transmission and the naming of the addressee; the available evidence does not justify treating the identity of the original recipient as unquestionably settled. The Grandevity Studio release consistently uses the title **A Letter to Zhu Yuansi**. The surviving passage is generally treated as an extract from a longer letter rather than proof that the entire original correspondence survives.

## Fuyang to Tonglu: A Journey Along the Fuchun River

> 自富陽至桐廬，一百許里，奇山異水，天下獨絕。

The Fuchun River runs through the Fuyang and Tonglu region of what is now Hangzhou municipality in Zhejiang Province. The original phrase *one hundred or so li* is an approximate expression in an old text; it should not be equated mechanically with a modern road or navigation distance.

The journey begins with freedom:

> 從流飄蕩，任意東西。

Drifting with the current, moving east or west as circumstances allow.

Then comes the celebrated summation:

> 奇山異水，天下獨絕。

Extraordinary mountains, remarkable water—unrivalled under heaven.

Wu Jun develops this claim in three movements: water, mountains, and the effect of the landscape upon the human heart.

## Water: Crystal Clarity and Sudden Speed

> 水皆縹碧，千丈見底。游魚細石，直視無礙。

The river is clear blue-green. Fish and fine stones can be seen beneath the surface. The suggestion that the bottom remains visible even at a thousand *zhang* is a literary expression of extreme transparency, not a scientific report of river depth.

Immediately, the character of the river changes:

> 急湍甚箭，猛浪若奔。

The rapids are swifter than arrows; the fierce waves resemble galloping horses.

The same river contains both stillness and violent movement. The prose changes speed almost as soon as its imagery changes.

## Mountains Written as Living Movement

> 夾岸高山，皆生寒樹，負勢競上，互相軒邈，爭高直指，千百成峰。

Along both banks, steep mountains are covered in dense trees that give the valley an atmosphere of cool shade. The peaks seem to compete as they rise and reach into the distance.

Mountains cannot literally race, yet Wu Jun's verbs make the landscape dynamic. It is an extraordinary shift in scale: from tiny fish and stones beneath the water to hundreds of peaks above the traveller's head.

The phrase *cold trees* evokes a sense of coolness and shade. It should not be used by itself to establish that the trip took place in winter.

## The Sounds of an Entire Valley

> 泉水激石，泠泠作響；好鳥相鳴，嚶嚶成韻。

Springs strike the rocks with clear, ringing sounds. Birds call to one another in harmonious tones.

> 蟬則千轉不窮，猿則百叫無絕。

Cicadas continue their calls; the cries of gibbons or monkeys seem never to end.

The numbers here create a sense of abundance and continuity rather than literal counts. The sounds expand the visual scene into an immersive environment.

This is also why the passage lends itself so naturally to music. Its balanced clauses, repeated sounds and alternating movement already possess an audible architecture.

## Beyond Scenery: When the Peaks Quiet Ambition

> 鳶飛戾天者，望峰息心；經綸世務者，窺谷忘反。

The soaring kite is a metaphor for those who strive upward after fame and status. The reference to the management of worldly affairs evokes people immersed in official responsibility.

Wu Jun does not present a formal treatise on resignation from public life. He allows the landscape to suggest another possibility: a moment in which ambition loosens its grip, and an occupied mind becomes quiet.

The work ends with an interplay of darkness and sunlight:

> 橫柯上蔽，在晝猶昏；疏條交映，有時見日。

Overhanging branches obscure the sky, making daytime seem almost dark; through the scattered leaves, sunlight sometimes appears.

The concluding image does not shout a moral. It leaves the traveller inside the changing light.

## 📖 Complete English Translation

**Wu Jun — A Letter to Zhu Yuansi (The Fuchun River Passage)**

The haze has entirely cleared, and sky and mountains share one colour. Following the current, I drift freely, moving east or west as the river carries me. From Fuyang to Tonglu, for roughly a hundred *li*, the mountains are extraordinary and the water remarkable—unmatched under heaven.

The river is clear blue-green; it seems possible to see the bottom even at immense depths. Fish swimming in the water and small stones below can be viewed without obstruction. Swift rapids are faster than arrows; powerful waves surge like galloping horses.

High mountains rise on either bank, clothed in trees that cast a cool shade. The peaks, drawing strength from their terrain, seem to compete in their ascent, reaching ever higher and farther until countless summits stand before the eye. Springs strike stones with bright, ringing notes; beautiful birds answer one another in tuneful calls. Cicadas continue their endless trilling; the cries of monkeys and gibbons do not cease. Those driven to soar after status and success look at the mountains and calm their ambition; those engrossed in affairs of the world gaze into the valleys and forget to return. Overhanging branches block the sky, so that even daytime resembles dusk; thin branches overlap, and now and then a beam of sunlight can be seen.

*Translation note: This English translation is provided for literary understanding. The recorded song is performed in Classical Chinese, not in English. “A thousand zhang” is poetic hyperbole, not a literal measurement.*
