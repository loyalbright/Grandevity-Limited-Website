A single orange.

Its appearance is radiant:

> 燁然，玉質而金色。
>
> Brilliant in appearance, with the texture of jade and the colour of gold.

Its market price is extraordinary.

People compete to buy it.

Then the narrator cuts one open.

Smoke-like vapour seems to strike his nose.

Inside:

> 乾若敗絮。
>
> Dry like ruined wadding.

At first, the moral appears obvious.

The seller is a fraud.

The buyer confronts him.

But then the seller smiles.

And from that moment,

the entire structure of the essay reverses.

He does not deny that the orange is beautiful outside and ruined within.

Instead he asks:

is this really the only deception worth examining?

By the time Liu Ji reaches:

> 金玉其外、
> 敗絮其中也哉？
>
> Gold and jade on the outside,
> ruined wadding within,

the orange has become much more than fruit.

It has become a way of looking at power.

Grandevity Studio’s **The Orange Seller’s Tale** uses the project-established version of Liu Ji’s original text as 100% of the sung words.

There is no modern lyrical expansion.

No vernacular rewrite.

No newly invented chorus.

No added lyric line.

No original sentence is repeated to extend the song.

The composition moves once from:

> 杭有賣果者

to:

> 而託于柑以諷耶？

Nothing is placed into Liu Ji’s mouth.

Music and cinema provide the new frame.

The words remain his.

### Liu Ji: beginning with fruit, ending with society

Liu Ji, courtesy name Bowen, was an important writer, thinker and political figure of the transition from the Yuan to the Ming dynasty.

The brilliance of **The Orange Seller’s Tale** lies in its scale.

It does not begin with government.

It does not begin with political theory.

It begins with a fruit seller.

An orange.

A customer.

A complaint.

The entrance to the story is small.

The implications become enormous.

---

### A radiant orange

The seller is skilled at preserving oranges:

> 善藏柑，涉寒暑不潰。

They survive changing seasons without visibly decaying.

When brought out:

> 出之燁然，玉質而金色。

They are radiant.

Jade-like in substance.

Golden in colour.

Placed in the market:

> 賈十倍，人爭鬻之。

They command ten times the price.

People compete for them.

Three signals reinforce one another:

beauty,

price,

popularity.

The mind easily adds a fourth conclusion:

quality.

But that conclusion has not yet been tested.

---

### Cutting it open

The narrator buys one:

> 予貿得其一，剖之。

He cuts it open.

The sensory world reverses immediately:

> 如有煙撲口鼻。

Then he looks inside:

> 則乾若敗絮。

The interior is dry like ruined wadding.

The contrast is complete.

Golden outside.

Rotten within.

---

### “What a fraud!”

The narrator asks whether these oranges are meant for ritual vessels, sacrifices and honoured guests,

or whether their beautiful appearance exists merely to deceive those who do not know better.

He concludes:

> 甚矣哉，為欺也！

What an extreme deception.

At this point the narrator appears to possess the moral advantage.

The evidence is obvious.

Then:

> 賣者笑曰。

The seller smiles.

---

### The seller changes the subject

He says he has practised this trade for years.

It feeds him.

He sells.

People buy.

No one complains.

Then:

> 世之為欺者不寡矣，
> 而獨我也乎？

There are many deceivers in the world.

Am I the only one?

The seller does not make the bad orange good.

Instead,

he changes the scale of the accusation.

---

### Tiger tallies and the commander’s seat

The seller points first to those who:

> 佩虎符、坐皋比者。

They carry the tiger tally.

They occupy the commander’s place.

They appear:

> 洸洸乎干城之具也。

Impressive,

as though they were the very instruments of national defence.

But the seller asks:

> 果能授孫、吳之略耶？

Can they truly command with the strategic ability associated with Sun Wu and Wu Qi?

Appearance is not competence.

Symbol is not skill.

---

### High caps and long official sashes

Next:

> 峨大冠、托長紳者。

High ceremonial caps.

Long sashes.

A dignified exterior.

They appear:

> 昂昂乎廟堂之器也。

Like men fit for the highest affairs of government.

But:

> 果能建伊、皋之業耶？

Can they truly achieve the works associated with the exemplary ministers Yi Yin and Gao Yao?

Again,

status and performance are separated.

---

### Four failures

Then Liu Ji shifts from appearance to results:

> 盜起而不知御，  
> 民困而不知救，  
> 吏奸而不知禁，  
> 法斁而不知理。

Banditry rises, yet they do not know how to resist it.

The people suffer, yet they do not know how to relieve them.

Officials become corrupt, yet they do not know how to restrain them.

The law decays, yet they do not know how to restore order.

This is the actual test.

Not what a person resembles.

What that person does.

---

### Consuming public grain without shame

Then:

> 坐糜廩粟而不知恥。

They sit consuming the grain of public office without knowing shame.

The critique has moved beyond incompetence.

The position still provides status and resources.

Responsibility is absent.

---

### High halls, great horses and fine food

The seller asks the narrator to look at people who:

> 坐高堂、騎大馬、
> 醉醇醴而飫肥鮮者。

They sit in high halls.

Ride great horses.

Become drunk on fine wine.

Feast on rich food.

Who among them does not appear:

> 巍巍乎可畏，
> 赫赫乎可象？

Majestic enough to inspire awe.

Splendid enough to be taken as a model.

The exterior signals are overwhelming.

And then comes the blade of the entire essay.

---

### Gold and jade outside, ruined wadding within

> 又何往而不——  
> 金玉其外、  
> 敗絮其中也哉？

Where can one go without finding gold and jade on the outside,

and ruined wadding within?

The orange returns.

But it is no longer merely an orange.

Its structure has become social:

a beautiful exterior,

trusted symbols,

high status,

public admiration,

and an interior that does not match what the surface promises.

The famous expression survives because the structure survives.

---

### “Yet you examine my orange”

Then comes the seller’s final reversal:

> 今子是之不察，
> 而以察吾柑！

These things you do not examine,

yet you choose to examine my orange.

The narrator answers:

> 予默然無應。

He falls silent.

The spoiled orange has not become honest.

What changes is the narrator’s awareness that his standard of scrutiny may be too narrow.

---

### Is the orange only a vehicle for satire?

After leaving, the narrator continues thinking.

The seller reminds him of the witty, satirical tradition associated with Dongfang Shuo.

The essay ends not with a declaration,

but with questions:

> 豈其憤世疾邪者耶？
> 而託于柑以諷耶？

Was he perhaps a man angered by the world and disgusted by corruption?

Did he use an orange as the vehicle for satire?

Liu Ji refuses to close the argument too neatly.

The question remains alive after the text ends.

### Music: from a marketplace exchange to social indictment

The musical direction is:

**Classic 1990s Hong Kong–Taiwan Mandopop Narrative Ballad / Subtle R&B**

Tempo: 78 BPM.

Vocal: mature, expressive but restrained Mandarin male vocal.

The arrangement uses:

- piano
- warm synth pads
- fretless bass
- clean electric guitar
- soft drums
- elegant strings
- occasional xiao

The opening remains warm and recognisably human.

A marketplace.

A purchase.

A piece of fruit.

When the orange is cut open, the harmony cools.

At:

> 賣者笑曰

the dramatic centre transfers to the seller.

The arrangement does not explode.

It grows steadier.

More certain.

The military and official sections increase the weight of bass and strings.

The passage beginning:

> 盜起而不知御

marks the move from satire toward social consequence.

The musical and intellectual climax belongs to:

> 又何往而不——  
> 金玉其外、  
> 敗絮其中也哉？

The delivery remains controlled.

The words provide the force.

At:

> 予默然無應

the arrangement withdraws sharply.

The final question is left suspended over the outro.

### The MV: the world grows colder as the argument grows larger

The MV is set within an imagined Hongwu-era Hangzhou and Jiangnan visual world.

The recurring narrator is approximately forty-two years old.

The orange seller is approximately fifty-five.

Both maintain consistent faces, clothing and physical appearance.

The opening marketplace is warm.

Golden fruit.

Wooden stalls.

Bamboo baskets.

Cloth awnings.

People bargaining.

The orange appears completely credible.

Once it is cut open,

the palette begins to cool.

As the seller moves from fruit to official power,

the film expands beyond the market.

Military officials.

Civil officials.

Government halls.

Mounted elites.

Banquets.

People in hardship.

Dusk streets.

The visual progression is deliberate:

the greater the surface splendour,

the more serious the underlying failure becomes.

At:

> 金玉其外、
> 敗絮其中

the film returns to the physical orange.

One intact golden fruit.

One opened fruit with a dry and ruined interior.

Object and society become one visual metaphor.

The seller eventually leaves.

The narrator remains in the market at dusk.

The city continues moving.

Nothing in the outside world has visibly changed.

Only the way he sees it has.

### What Really Needs to Be Cut Open?

**The Orange Seller’s Tale** does not say that beauty is false.

It does not say that high prices are inherently fraudulent.

It asks a more precise question:

when external signals appear convincing enough,

do we stop examining what lies inside?

Price.

Uniform.

Office.

Prestige.

Luxury.

Authority.

Public admiration.

All can tell us something.

None automatically proves substance.

Liu Ji begins with a knife cutting through an orange peel.

The deeper question is whether we are willing to apply the same act of scrutiny

to everything else we are taught to admire.

### About This Project

**The Orange Seller’s Tale** is part of Grandevity Studio’s **Classical Literature Reimagined in Music** series.

The purpose is not to rewrite Liu Ji.

His question remains contemporary without any new lines.

An orange can be:

radiant,

golden,

expensive,

highly desired,

and still be:

> 乾若敗絮。

Dry like ruined wadding.

More than six centuries later,

our forms of “gold and jade” have changed.

The essential question has not.

What lies inside?
