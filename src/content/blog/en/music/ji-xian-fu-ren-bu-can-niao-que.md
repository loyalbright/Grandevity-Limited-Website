Some forms of trust require no shared language.

A living creature simply observes you long enough to discover that you will not harm it.

**On My Late Mother’s Refusal to Harm Birds** is a Mandarin male-vocal work setting 100% of the original prose by Northern Song writer Su Shi, also known as Su Dongpo, to music.

There are no expanded lyrics, no modern rewriting and no newly written chorus.

The prose unfolds entirely in Su Shi’s own words, beginning with an almost idyllic childhood courtyard and ending with one of the most severe conclusions in the piece:

> Harsh government is fiercer than a tiger, truly so!

At first, the memory is gentle.

Outside the study of Su Shi’s childhood home, bamboo, cypress and flowers fill the courtyard. Birds build nests among the branches.

His mother, Lady Wuyang, detests killing living creatures. Children and servants are forbidden from catching the birds.

Years pass.

Something changes.

The birds begin to nest lower and lower, until their young can be seen simply by bending down.

Then an even rarer visitor appears: the **Tonghua Feng**, a bird described as exceptionally beautiful and seldom seen. For four or five days it flies and gathers within the courtyard, remarkably tame and unafraid of people.

The neighbours consider this extraordinary.

Su Shi gives a much simpler explanation:

> There was nothing else to it: the sincerity of a heart that did no harm was trusted even by creatures of another kind.

Trust has become visible.

But the prose does not end there.

### Lady Wuyang: Su Shi Lets the Birds Testify for His Mother

The “late mother” of the title is Su Shi’s mother, née Cheng, referred to in the prose by her title Lady Wuyang.

Su Shi does not offer an elaborate moral portrait.

Instead, he records one household rule:

> Lady Wuyang detested killing living creatures. Children and servants were all forbidden from catching the birds.

She does not tame them.

She does not call them toward her.

She merely ensures that no one in the household harms them.

Over time, the birds themselves respond.

This is the elegance of Su Shi’s remembrance.

He does not need to tell us that his mother was compassionate.

The wild creatures around her house provide the evidence.

---

### “They All Nested on Low Branches”: Trust Changes Distance

> Over several years, they all began nesting on low branches; one could bend down and look upon their nestlings.

For a wild bird, distance is usually protection.

Height is protection.

A nest close to people can be dangerous.

Yet in this courtyard the logic has changed.

Because people do not kill them, proximity becomes safer than distance.

The movement of the nests from high branches to low ones is therefore more than a charming natural detail.

It is a physical measure of trust.

No human declaration is required.

How closely another creature is willing to live beside you may reveal more than any proclamation of kindness.

---

### The Tonghua Feng: The Rarest Thing Is Not Its Plumage, but Its Lack of Fear

Su Shi then introduces the most visually striking creature in the prose:

> There was also a Tonghua Feng which, for four or five days, flew and gathered among the trees. Its plumage was exceptionally rare and beautiful, yet it was tame and showed no fear of people.

The exact modern species corresponding to the old name **Tonghua Feng** is difficult to establish with certainty.

For that reason, the MV does not force the bird into a modern zoological identity.

It is presented as a rare but naturalistic small bird: refined, unusual and visually memorable, yet never supernatural.

Its importance lies not in fantasy.

The extraordinary fact is that even this rare bird does not fear the people of the household.

---

### “There Was Nothing Else to It”: Kindness Becomes Credible Only through Time

The neighbours regard the scene as something marvellous.

Su Shi refuses a supernatural explanation:

> There was nothing else to it: the sincerity of a heart that did no harm was trusted even by creatures of another kind.

The key is duration.

A single harmless encounter does not produce trust.

Repeated harmless encounters do.

Eventually another creature no longer needs to guess what you might do.

It knows.

That knowledge changes behaviour.

This is why the passage feels unexpectedly modern: trust is not demanded, declared or performed.

It is accumulated.

---

### The Old Villager Speaks: The Gentle Memory Gains a Second Explanation

An elderly countryman then offers a practical observation.

When birds nest too far from people, their young face danger from snakes, rodents, foxes, owls and kites.

If humans do not kill them, the birds may deliberately live closer to human habitation in order to escape those predators.

The explanation is ecological rather than sentimental.

The birds are not expressing gratitude.

They are making a survival decision.

And that simple observation suddenly opens a much darker question.

If birds may choose people over natural predators, why were birds traditionally so afraid to nest near people in the first place?

---

### “They Considered Humans Worse than Snakes and Rodents”

Su Shi answers:

> In former times, when birds did not dare to nest near people, it was because they regarded humans as worse than snakes and rodents.

The entire emotional structure of the prose turns at this moment.

The compassionate courtyard is no longer merely a pleasant memory.

It has become an accusation.

What kind of human behaviour is required before a wild creature considers us more dangerous than its natural predators?

The question is no longer about birds alone.

It is about power.

---

### “Harsh Government Is Fiercer than a Tiger”

The prose ends:

> Harsh government is fiercer than a tiger, truly so!

The famous idea of harsh government being fiercer than a tiger is associated with the classical tradition preserved in the *Book of Rites*.

Its force lies in comparison.

A tiger is visibly dangerous.

Its violence has claws and teeth.

Human power can be more terrifying precisely because it may become systematic, unavoidable and enduring.

Su Shi’s short prose therefore moves with remarkable economy:

mother → birds → trust → low nests → rare bird → predators → humans → government.

The final political thought is not detached from the childhood memory.

It is its logical completion.

Fear creates distance.

Mercy creates proximity.

### Cinematic Neo-Classical Chinese Narrative: Letting Warmth Slowly Acquire Weight

The final musical production is built around a **Cinematic Neo-Classical Chinese Narrative Song**, approximately 72 BPM in 6/8, with a through-composed structure.

The arrangement features:

- A cultured, restrained Standard Mandarin baritone with clear classical-Chinese diction.
- Solo cello, low piano and distant birds opening the world of childhood memory.
- Guqin and xiao establishing the quiet architecture of the Northern Song courtyard.
- Pipa textures and chamber strings widening the sound as the rare Tonghua Feng appears.
- A deliberate pause after the neighbours consider the event extraordinary, allowing “There was nothing else to it” to arrive almost alone.
- A long orchestral interlude before the old villager’s reasoning, transforming domestic memory into philosophical reflection.
- Gradually darkening harmony in the final paragraph.
- A single grave climax reserved for “Harsh government is fiercer than a tiger, truly so!”
- A long decrescendo ending not in triumph, but in unresolved birdsong.

Nothing is added to Su Shi’s text.

The music simply gives the original prose more time to reveal what was already there.

### A Song with No Expanded Lyrics and No Need for Them

There is no modern chorus.

No explanatory refrain.

No contemporary writer arrives to complete Su Shi’s thought.

The original prose already possesses a complete dramatic arc:

A mother.

A prohibition against killing.

Trust.

Low nests.

A rare bird.

An old villager.

Predators.

Human beings.

Government.

It begins in tenderness.

It ends in warning.

Adding another lyric would risk weakening that architecture.

The production therefore allows Su Shi’s own language to complete the journey.

### About the Project

**On My Late Mother’s Refusal to Harm Birds** is part of Grandevity Studio’s *Classical Literature Reimagined in Music* series.

The aim is not simply to make old prose sound modern.

It is to restore the experience of reading it slowly enough for its structure to become visible again.

Su Shi begins with birds.

He ends with government.

Between those two points lies a single principle:

When living creatures expect harm, they keep their distance.

When they learn they are safe, they come closer.

The distance between a high nest and a low branch may be only a few feet.

The distance between that low branch and the words “harsh government is fiercer than a tiger” contains an entire vision of human society.

Nearly a thousand years later, the question remains:

Do those with less power feel safer when they come closer to us,

or safer when they stay away?
