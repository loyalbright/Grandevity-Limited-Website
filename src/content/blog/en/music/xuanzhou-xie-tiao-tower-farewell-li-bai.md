## Li Bai in Xuanzhou: a poet beyond the court

**Li Bai (701–762)**, courtesy name **Taibai**, is traditionally celebrated as the *Poet Immortal*. His writing can be exuberant, startlingly imaginative and intensely personal—often within the same poem.

He entered the Tang imperial court during the Tianbao era and served in the Hanlin circle, yet his hopes for a greater political role were not fulfilled. After leaving Chang'an, he returned to a wandering life of travel, friendship and poetry.

The poem is commonly assigned to **the autumn of 753**, although its precise date and circumstances are not settled beyond dispute. **Xuanzhou** was a prefecture in the region of present-day Xuancheng, Anhui. The tower associated with the Southern Qi poet **Xie Tiao** evoked for Li Bai the achievements of an earlier literary age. He admired Xie Tiao deeply and wrote more than one poem connected with Xuancheng and the northern tower.

The title itself has a textual complication. In the familiar title, the person being seen off is **“Shu Yun,”** conventionally associated with a kinsman called Li Yun and with the work of a court librarian or textual editor. Another transmitted title reads **“Song on Ascending the Tower with Uncle Hua, the Censor”** (*Pei Shiyu Shu Hua Denglou Ge*). Some scholars therefore connect the gathering instead with **Li Hua**. The finished recording retains the established title, without pretending this identification is certain.

## A farewell poem with an extraordinary emotional arc

### Yesterday is gone; today's sorrow remains

“Those who abandon me—yesterday's days—cannot be kept. / Those who trouble my heart—today's days—bring endless cares.”

Although the poem is named for a farewell gathering, it opens with a stark personal confession. Time cannot be recalled. The present feels overwhelmed. With no explanation or preamble, Li Bai gives voice to an experience still familiar today.

### The autumn wind opens the sky

“A mighty wind bears autumn geese across ten thousand miles; / Such a sight calls for drinking high in the tower.”

Suddenly the poem widens from the troubled mind to sky, birds, wind and shared wine. The poet has not solved his problems. He has remembered that friendship and the landscape can briefly restore his sense of possibility.

### “Jian'an spirit” and “Little Xie”: literary inheritance

The lines about **Penglai writings, the Jian'an spirit and Little Xie** are dense with literary history. *Penglai* invokes the imperial library or archives and the scholarly role associated with the person addressed. *Jian'an* recalls a powerful, forthright style of late Han and early Three Kingdoms literature. **“Little Xie”** refers to **Xie Tiao**, whose clear and graceful poetry Li Bai admired.

The allusions turn a personal farewell into a conversation with the wider Chinese poetic tradition: the friends' literary aspirations stand alongside those of earlier writers.

### To gaze upon the sun and moon

“Together we harbour soaring inspiration; / We would rise into the blue heavens to behold the sun and moon.”

For this recording, the adopted text reads **覽日月**, “to gaze upon the sun and moon,” following the main text of **volume 177 of the Siku Quanshu edition of the Imperially Commissioned Complete Tang Poems** (*Yuding Quan Tang Shi*). Other received texts differ. The finished vocal version is preserved rather than retroactively rewritten.

The image is both astonishing and deeply human. A poet whose plans have met with obstacles can still imagine a freedom larger than the world before him.

### Cutting water, drinking sorrow

“Draw a blade to cut the water—the water flows on; / Raise a cup to dissolve sorrow—the sorrow only grows.”

Few poetic images express helplessness so forcefully. A blade meets no resistance it can overcome; a drink cannot alter the cause of grief. The soaring ambition of the previous lines turns inward, giving the poem its unforgettable emotional reversal.

### Unbinding one's hair and taking a little boat

“If life in this world cannot fulfil one's heart, / Tomorrow I shall loosen my hair and take a small boat.”

Unbinding the hair suggests loosening the strictures of official and social life; the little boat evokes the freedom to wander beyond worldly obligations. This is a **poetic wish for withdrawal**, not documentary proof that Li Bai literally departed in a boat the following morning.

## 🎼 The music: intimate Mandopop Soul rather than an epic anthem

This arrangement chooses **acoustic Mandopop Soul with poetic Chinese folk contours**, moving at **70 BPM in flowing 6/8**. Fingerpicked nylon-string guitar leads; felt piano, upright acoustic bass, brushed percussion, solo cello and gentle viola supply warmth and motion. Guqin harmonics and distant bamboo *xiao* colour the spaces between phrases.

A mature, close-miked Mandarin male baritone tells the story with clear articulation and restrained emotion, supported by understated call-and-response backing voices. The room remains audible; natural, human-scale detail takes precedence over polished grandiosity.

The first verse stays private. The autumn landscape opens the music. The phrase **“覽日月”** rises, then the melody darkens for **“水更流，愁更愁.”** The last image of the boat is a release, not an explosive finale.
