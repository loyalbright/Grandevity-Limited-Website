Some books are impossible to put down when we borrow them.

Yet once we buy our own copies, they can sit untouched on a shelf for years.

More than two centuries ago, the Qing-dynasty writer Yuan Mei recognized this contradiction with remarkable clarity.

> 書非借不能讀也。

Books are never read with quite the same urgency as when they are borrowed.

At first, the statement sounds unreasonable.

Yuan Mei was not arguing that owning a book makes reading impossible.

He was asking why the same object can feel so different depending on whether it is temporarily entrusted to us or permanently ours.

Why do we cherish an opportunity when it may soon disappear, yet postpone it when it seems certain to remain?

And what happens when, years later, we finally possess what we once desperately wanted, only to discover that the time and intensity of youth cannot be recovered?

**Huang Sheng Borrows Books** is about reading.

But its deepest subject is time.

Grandevity Studio has set the entire project-established original essay to new music.

There are no modern lyrical expansions.

No rewritten lines.

No newly invented chorus.

No added Chinese lyrics.

No deliberate repetition of original sentences merely to extend the song.

The words remain Yuan Mei's.

The new expression comes through music and imagery.

### Yuan Mei: From an Impoverished Student to the Master of Sui Garden

Yuan Mei lived from 1716 to 1798.

His courtesy name was Zicai, and his literary name was Jianzhai.

He was also known as the Master of Sui Garden.

Born in Qiantang, in the region of present-day Hangzhou, Zhejiang Province, Yuan Mei became one of the Qing dynasty's most influential poets, essayists and literary critics.

He is traditionally grouped with Zhao Yi and Jiang Shiquan among the Three Great Masters of the Qianlong Era.

His early family circumstances were modest, but he was devoted to reading and learning.

In 1739, the fourth year of the Qianlong reign, he passed the imperial jinshi examination.

He subsequently served as a county magistrate in several places in Jiangsu.

A county magistrate in Qing China was responsible for local administration, judicial affairs and taxation, although the role cannot be equated precisely with a modern county mayor.

In 1749, Yuan Mei withdrew from official service and settled at Sui Garden near Xiaocang Mountain in Nanjing.

He devoted much of his later life to poetry, prose, literary criticism, books and intellectual exchange.

Yuan Mei valued the genuine expression of personal feeling and temperament.

His writing often favors lucid prose and concrete experience over unnecessary displays of erudition.

**Huang Sheng Borrows Books** reflects that approach.

A substantial reflection on human behavior begins with a simple event:

a young man comes to borrow some books.

### Who Was Huang Sheng?

**Huang Sheng Borrows Books** appears in Volume 22 of Yuan Mei's *Xiaocang Shanfang Wenji*, his collected prose.

The essay begins:

> 黃生允修借書，隨園主人授以書，而告之曰：

The young scholar Huang Yunxiu came to borrow books.

The Master of Sui Garden handed him the books and spoke to him.

Huang Yunxiu is the Huang Sheng of the title.

Little reliable biographical information about him survives.

The essay itself provides the clearest account of his role.

He is a young scholar of limited means, borrowing books from an older writer who remembers being in much the same situation.

Yuan Mei does more than lend.

He writes an essay to accompany the books.

That personal relationship gives the work much of its warmth.

---

### “Books Are Best Read When Borrowed”

The opening proposition is:

> 書非借不能讀也。

Yuan Mei does not immediately explain his own experience.

He begins by pointing toward two kinds of book owners:

emperors,

and wealthy households.

Imperial collections could be immense.

Rich families could accumulate enough books to fill entire rooms.

But how many of their owners actually read attentively?

The accumulation of books is not the same thing as the accumulation of understanding.

Even books gathered with great effort by one generation can be neglected or discarded by the next.

The problem is not ownership itself.

It is the mistaken belief that possession guarantees use.

---

### The Qilüe, the Four Repositories and the Siku Quanshu

Two historical references matter.

The *Qilüe* was an important bibliographic catalogue compiled by Liu Xin during the Western Han dynasty.

The Four Repositories, or *Siku*, belongs to the tradition of classifying written learning, eventually associated with the four broad divisions of classics, history, philosophical writings and literary collections.

Yuan Mei invokes these references to evoke the richness of imperial book collections.

The passage should not be read as a claim that ordinary private collectors owned complete copies of the much later *Siku Quanshu*.

His concern is not the precise number of imperial books.

It is the difference between having them and reading them.

---

### “It Is Not Only Books”

Yuan Mei expands the argument:

> 非獨書為然，天下物皆然。

It is not only books that are like this.

All kinds of possessions can affect human attention in similar ways.

When something belongs to another person, we know we must eventually return it.

We worry that the owner may ask for it back.

We handle it carefully.

We think:

> 今日存，明日去，吾不得而見之矣。

It is here today.

Tomorrow it may be gone.

I may never see it again.

But when something is securely ours, we may put it on a high shelf and say:

> 姑俟異日觀云爾。

I shall look at it another day.

The immediate loss appears to be a missed reading session.

The deeper loss is the disappearance of attention and time.

---

### The Young Yuan Mei Who Dreamed of Books

Midway through the essay, the argument becomes personal.

> 余幼好書，家貧難致。

When I was young, I loved books, but my family was too poor to acquire them.

A certain Zhang possessed a rich collection.

Yuan Mei asked to borrow books from him and was refused.

The longing was so intense that the books appeared in his dreams after he returned home.

> 歸而形諸夢。

It is a remarkably intimate detail.

The young Yuan Mei did not lack the desire to read.

He lacked access.

Whenever he did obtain an opportunity, he read attentively and worked to remember what he saw.

Scarcity had sharpened his concentration.

---

### “My Salary Went Out, and Books Came In”

Years later, Yuan Mei entered official life.

His salary allowed him to purchase books that had once seemed impossibly distant.

He writes:

> 通籍後，俸去書來，落落大滿。

His salary went out.

Books came in.

His collection became large.

Yet something had changed.

Some volumes sat long enough to gather dust, cobwebs and traces of insects.

> 素蟫灰絲，時蒙卷軸。

The books he had once wanted so desperately were finally his.

But ownership had not preserved the urgency of youth.

---

### The Years of Youth Cannot Be Bought Back

The emotional center of the essay arrives here:

> 然後歎借者之用心專，而少時之歲月為可惜也。

Only then did I sigh over how single-minded a borrower could be, and how precious the years of youth had been.

When Yuan Mei was young, he lacked books.

Yet he possessed extraordinary urgency.

As an adult, he possessed books.

But he could no longer recover the time and intensity of those earlier years.

The deepest regret is therefore not merely that certain books were once unavailable.

It is that youth itself has passed.

Books can be collected.

Time cannot.

---

### Was Yuan Mei Unfortunate, and Huang Sheng Fortunate?

The essay returns to Huang Sheng:

> 今黃生貧類予，其借書亦類予。

Huang Sheng is poor, as Yuan Mei once was.

His need to borrow resembles Yuan Mei's own youthful experience.

But there is a difference.

Zhang refused to lend.

Yuan Mei chooses to share.

He then asks:

> 然則予固不幸而遇張乎？生固幸而遇予乎？

Was I necessarily unfortunate to have met Zhang?

Is Huang Sheng necessarily fortunate to have met me?

The questions do not suggest that unkindness is a virtue.

Nor do they diminish the value of generosity.

They ask something subtler.

Difficulty may sharpen attention.

Easy access does not guarantee appreciation.

An opportunity becomes valuable through the way we use it.

---

### “Let This Essay Accompany the Books”

The essay ends with a practical instruction:

> 知幸與不幸，則其讀書也心專，而其歸書也必速。

If Huang Sheng understands fortune and misfortune, he will read attentively and return the books promptly.

Then Yuan Mei concludes:

> 為一說，使與書俱。

I have written this essay to accompany the books.

The conclusion is remarkably modest.

After reflections on possession, desire, regret and the passing of youth, Yuan Mei returns to two simple responsibilities.

Read with concentration.

Return what has been borrowed.

The essay itself becomes part of the gift.

### 📖 Complete English Translation

The young scholar Huang Yunxiu came to borrow books. The Master of Sui Garden gave him the books and said:

"Books are never read with such urgency as when they are borrowed. Have you not heard about those who hoard books?

"The *Qilüe* and the Four Repositories belong to the emperor's collections. Yet how many emperors truly read?

"The books of wealthy households are so numerous they could exhaust the oxen that carried them and fill the rooms where they are kept. Yet how many wealthy people truly read?

"And those books accumulated by grandfathers and fathers, only to be thrown away by their descendants, there is no need even to speak of them.

"It is not only books that are like this; all things under heaven are so.

"When something is not your own and you have had to borrow it, you worry that its owner may press you to return it. You examine and handle it with anxious care, thinking, 'It is here today, but tomorrow it will be gone, and I shall no longer be able to see it.'

"But if it were already yours, you would surely bind it up, put it away on a high shelf and say, 'I shall wait until another day to look at it.'

"When I was young, I loved books, but my family was too poor to acquire them.

"A certain Zhang possessed a very rich collection. I went to borrow from him, but he refused.

"When I returned home, the books appeared in my dreams. Such was the intensity of my longing.

"So whenever I did have something to read, I would take care to reflect on and remember it.

"After I entered official life, my salary went out and books came in, until my collection grew large and abundant.

"Bookworms, dust and grey threads of cobwebs sometimes covered the volumes.

"Only then did I sigh over how single-minded a borrower could be, and how much there was to regret in the passing of my younger years.

"Now Huang Sheng is poor, as I once was, and his borrowing of books is much like mine.

"But my willingness to share my books is not like Zhang's reluctance to lend his.

"Was I, then, truly unfortunate to have met Zhang? Is Huang Sheng truly fortunate to have met me?

"If he understands fortune and misfortune, he will read with a concentrated mind and return the books promptly."

And so I wrote this essay to accompany the books.

*Translation note: The passage above is an English rendering of the complete text sung in the MV. It is not a separate set of English lyrics. The recorded performance remains in Classical Chinese.*
