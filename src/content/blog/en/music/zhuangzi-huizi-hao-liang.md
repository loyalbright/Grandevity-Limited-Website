Two friends are standing on a bridge.

Below them, the river flows gently.

Small fish swim at their ease.

> 鰷魚出遊從容，

The other friend immediately asks:

> 子非魚，

With a single question, an ordinary riverside walk becomes one of the most celebrated philosophical exchanges in Chinese literature.

It is traditionally known as the **Hao Bridge Debate**, or the **Debate on the Happiness of Fish**.

### Zhuangzi and Huizi: Two Philosophers, Two Ways of Thinking

**Zhuangzi**, traditionally identified as Zhuang Zhou, was an important Chinese philosopher of the Warring States period, generally placed in the fourth and third centuries BCE.

Later generations regard him as one of the central thinkers of the Daoist tradition.

The writings associated with him frequently explore the relativity of human judgments, the limitations of knowledge, the diversity of living beings and the possibility of freedom from rigid distinctions.

Rather than presenting only formal arguments, the *Zhuangzi* often uses stories, dreams, conversations and striking images.

**Huizi**, also known as Hui Shi, lived in approximately the same historical period.

He is traditionally associated with the School of Names, a current of early Chinese thought concerned with distinctions, concepts, language and argument.

Huizi appears several times in the transmitted *Zhuangzi*.

Sometimes he challenges Zhuangzi directly.

Sometimes their conversations seem playful.

Their exchanges repeatedly bring different intellectual temperaments into contact.

Zhuangzi is often portrayed as attentive to lived experience, spontaneity and the unexpected possibilities of perception.

Huizi frequently demands conceptual clarity and a defensible basis for what is being claimed.

The Hao Bridge dialogue places these approaches beside one another.

One begins with a feeling inspired by watching fish.

The other asks how that feeling can amount to knowledge.

### Where Does the Hao Bridge Story Come From?

The dialogue appears at the end of the chapter **Qiu Shui**, usually translated as *Autumn Floods* or *The Floods of Autumn*.

This chapter belongs to the Outer Chapters of the *Zhuangzi*.

The received text of the *Zhuangzi* is conventionally divided into three groups:

the Inner Chapters,

the Outer Chapters,

and the Miscellaneous Chapters.

Although traditionally associated with Zhuang Zhou, the complete transmitted book is generally understood to reflect a longer process of composition, compilation and transmission.

The Outer and Miscellaneous Chapters should not all be assumed to have been personally written by Zhuang Zhou.

The Hao Bridge story is therefore best approached as a philosophical narrative preserved in the classical *Zhuangzi* tradition.

It vividly portrays Zhuangzi and Huizi as intellectual counterparts, but it is not a demonstrably verbatim historical transcript of an actual conversation.

The Hao was an ancient waterway traditionally associated with the region around modern Fengyang County in Anhui Province.

The Chinese word **liang** (梁) can refer to a bridge.

Thus, **Hao Liang** evokes a bridge over the Hao River.

The exact structure and location of the bridge described in the story cannot be securely reconstructed.

The wooden bridge used in the MV is an imaginative period-inspired design, not a claim of archaeological reconstruction.

### The Fish Swimming at Their Ease

The dialogue begins:

> 鰷魚出遊從容，
> 是魚之樂也。

The fish swim with ease.

Zhuangzi sees this and calls it happiness.

The word *congrong* (從容) suggests ease, unhurried movement and a relaxed manner.

Zhuangzi does not begin with a scientific account of fish behaviour.

He makes an observation that contains an element of personal perception.

The fish seem at home in the water.

They move freely.

Their movement gives him a sense of delight.

Within the wider philosophical imagination of the *Zhuangzi*, such scenes often invite reflection on how different beings follow their own ways of living.

But Huizi is not content to leave the statement unexamined.

He asks the question that has made this passage famous.

### "You Are Not a Fish. How Do You Know Fish Are Happy?"

> 子非魚，
> 安知魚之樂？

This is not merely a trivial objection.

It poses a question about the grounds of knowledge.

How can one being know the inner experience of another?

We can see a fish moving through the water.

But does its movement prove that it is happy?

We can observe a person's expression.

But does a smile always mean that the person is experiencing joy?

The difference between observable behaviour and subjective experience remains an important issue in philosophy and the study of animal minds.

Huizi's challenge may therefore be understood as a serious demand for justification.

He is asking Zhuangzi to explain what makes his claim more than an attractive interpretation of what he sees.

---

### "You Are Not Me. How Do You Know I Do Not Know?"

Zhuangzi replies:

> 子非我，
> 安知我不知魚之樂？

You are not me.

How do you know that I do not know the happiness of fish?

Zhuangzi turns Huizi's challenge back toward its source.

If one cannot know another being's experience because one is not that being, then how can Huizi be certain of what Zhuangzi does or does not know?

The response challenges the confidence with which Huizi has denied his claim.

But it is important not to exaggerate the result.

This counter-question does not, by itself, establish that the fish are objectively happy.

Instead, it exposes the difficulty of applying a strict principle about the limits of knowledge consistently.

Huizi responds with another carefully structured argument.

---

### Huizi's Counterargument: Does Zhuangzi Actually Win?

Huizi answers:

> 我非子，
> 固不知子矣；

Then continues:

> 子固非魚也，
> 子之不知魚之樂，
> 全矣！

Huizi accepts that he is not Zhuangzi and therefore does not fully know Zhuangzi's inner experience.

But he insists that Zhuangzi is not a fish either.

If the original rule stands, then Zhuangzi likewise cannot directly know the fish's experience.

This is not a foolish or meaningless response.

Huizi applies the same principle to both participants.

His position has a genuine philosophical structure.

The dialogue is therefore richer than a simple story about one clever philosopher defeating a less clever opponent.

The real tension lies in what the two speakers understand by the word **know**.

Must knowledge always be grounded in formal justification?

Or can an immediate sense of another creature's state also be a meaningful form of understanding?

---

### "Let Us Return to the Beginning"

Zhuangzi's final reply begins:

> 請循其本。

Let us return to the beginning.

He then says:

> 子曰「汝安知魚樂」云者，
> 既已知吾知之而問我，
> 我知之濠上也。

The response turns on the possibilities of the classical Chinese expression **an zhi** (安知).

In the earlier question, Huizi appears to be asking:

**How do you know?**

That is a challenge to the grounds of Zhuangzi's knowledge.

Zhuangzi's response redirects the expression toward:

**Where did you come to know it?**

His answer becomes:

> 我知之濠上也。

I knew it on the bridge over the Hao.

The shift is ingenious.

Instead of continuing an abstract argument about the possibility of knowing another creature's inner life, Zhuangzi brings the conversation back to the place where it began.

Two friends.

A bridge.

Flowing water.

Fish swimming below.

Zhuangzi's initial statement arose from his immediate experience of that scene.

From the standpoint of strict formal logic, this does not conclusively demonstrate the subjective happiness of fish.

But as a literary and philosophical ending, it restores something that the debate had gradually set aside:

the living moment that inspired the question in the first place.

### What Makes This a Story About Friendship?

There is another small but important detail.

Zhuangzi and Huizi are not arguing in a royal court.

They are not conducting a formal public trial.

Nor are they competing for an official position.

They are taking a walk together.

A seemingly minor observation becomes an important intellectual disagreement.

Yet the entire exchange takes place within a shared experience.

Both are looking at the same water.

Both see the same fish.

They simply interpret the scene differently.

That is part of the charm of the story.

Friendship does not require identical ways of thinking.

An argument does not necessarily destroy mutual interest.

Sometimes another person's challenge helps clarify what one really means.

The lively dialogue itself becomes an image of intellectual companionship.

### 📖 Complete English Translation of the Sung Text

**Zhuangzi and Huizi Strolling on the Hao Bridge**

Zhuangzi and Huizi were strolling together on the bridge over the Hao River.

Zhuangzi said:

"Look at those little fish swimming about at their ease. This is the happiness of fish."

Huizi said:

"You are not a fish. How do you know the happiness of fish?"

Zhuangzi replied:

"You are not me. How do you know that I do not know the happiness of fish?"

Huizi answered:

"I am not you, so of course I do not fully know you. But you are certainly not a fish. Therefore, that you do not know the happiness of fish is completely established!"

Zhuangzi said:

"Let us return to the beginning. When you said, 'Where do you know the happiness of fish?' you were already treating me as knowing it, and asking me where I knew it. I know it here, on the bridge over the Hao."

*Translator's note: This is a literary English rendering of the complete original dialogue used in the finished song. The recorded performance remains in Classical Chinese. The final exchange contains a deliberate interpretive shift in the meaning of the question "an zhi" (安知), which cannot be reproduced perfectly in a single English phrase.*

### Does One of Them Win?

Readers have debated the meaning of this exchange for centuries.

Huizi asks an important question about the justification of knowledge.

Zhuangzi redirects attention toward the immediacy of lived experience.

Huizi's concern should not be dismissed merely because Zhuangzi delivers the memorable final line.

Nor should Zhuangzi's reply be reduced to an empty trick of language.

The philosophical fascination lies partly in the fact that the speakers are not asking exactly the same kind of question.

One asks how knowledge can be warranted.

The other responds from an experienced relationship with the world.

The story invites readers to consider the strengths and limitations of both approaches.

It does not require us to settle the debate before we can enjoy the scene.

And that may be the most fitting way to listen to this particular song.

The fish continue swimming.

The river continues flowing.

The two friends continue to challenge one another.
