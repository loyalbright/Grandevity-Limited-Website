## 📖 Han Yu, Meng Jiao, and the Middle Tang World

**Han Yu (768–824)**, courtesy name Tuizhi, was one of the leading prose writers and thinkers of the middle Tang dynasty. A central figure in the Classical Prose Movement, he advocated clear, substantive writing grounded in thought and moral purpose rather than ornament for its own sake. Later generations placed him first among the “Eight Great Prose Masters of the Tang and Song.”

**Meng Jiao (751–814)**, courtesy name Dongye, came from Wukang in what is now Deqing, Zhejiang. Seventeen years older than Han Yu, he spent many years attempting the civil-service examinations before finally passing in middle age. After further delay, he was appointed county commandant (*xianwei*) of Liyang, in the Jiangnan region—a relatively modest local post. His poetry became associated with painstaking composition, austerity, and the intimate expression of hardship and compassion. His celebrated poem *A Wanderer’s Song* (《遊子吟》) remains widely read today.

The farewell essay is commonly dated to **around 802 CE**, in the reign of Emperor Dezong, as Meng Jiao prepared to take up service in Jiangnan. Han Yu respected Meng’s poetic gifts and had long valued their literary friendship. Rather than offer conventional farewell compliments, he wrote an ambitious reflection on the relationship between creative expression and human adversity.

Two other writers appear near the conclusion: **Li Ao (李翱)**, associated with the revival of classical prose, and **Zhang Ji (張籍)**, a poet known especially for his interest in ordinary lives and social experience. Together with Meng Jiao, they are the “three men” whose literary gifts Han Yu praises. The essay itself does not describe a specific, historically documented riverside farewell scene.

## 🔔 “When Things Are Uneasy, They Sound”: The Central Idea

The essay opens not with a traveler, but with the natural world. Trees and grasses make sound when the wind disturbs them; water sounds when it is churned, obstructed, or heated. Human voices are similar: feeling and thought seek expression when they cannot remain contained.

This is the force of **不平則鳴** (*bu ping ze ming*), often understood as “grievance finds a voice.” Yet Han Yu’s argument is broader than complaint alone. His idea of *ming*—to sound, speak, or make oneself heard—also embraces music, learning, literature, and the urge to give form to powerful inward experience.

From the legendary age of Yao and Shun, the essay moves through Confucius, Zhuangzi, Qu Yuan, Sima Qian, and other cultural voices. It eventually reaches the Tang writers Chen Zi’ang, Li Bai, Du Fu, and Li Guan. Each became audible to posterity through a different kind of achievement.

Only after that sweeping history does Han Yu name the person he is truly writing for: Meng Jiao, whose poetry, though produced outside the highest ranks of official life, deserves a place in the long history of Chinese literature.

## 🌫️ A Question Without an Easy Answer

Han Yu then asks whether Heaven will harmonize the voices of these gifted men so that they may celebrate the flourishing state—or condemn them to hunger, hardship, and sorrow, so that their writings express private misfortune.

The emotional force lies in the uncertainty. Han Yu does not claim to know whether talent will be rewarded. Instead, he reminds his friend that worldly rank and lasting worth are not the same thing: **why rejoice simply because one has risen, or despair simply because one remains below?**

This is both a farewell gift and a larger statement of sympathy for talented people whose gifts may not receive their due. The essay’s closing comfort is gentle, but it never erases the pain behind the question.

## 🌍 Complete English Reading Translation (Not Sung)

This is a reading translation of the full essay in the same order as the sung Chinese text. It is provided to explain the literature; **the recording contains no English lyrics**.

**Opening — The principle**

In general, things give voice when their equilibrium has been disturbed.

**Verse 1 — The sounds of nature and humanity**

Grass and trees have no voice of their own; when the wind stirs them, they sound. Water has no voice of its own; when the wind moves it, it sounds. When it leaps, something may have struck it; when it rushes, something may have blocked it; when it boils, something may have heated it. Metal and stone are silent until they are struck.

The same is true of people and their speech: they speak when they can no longer remain silent. Their songs contain longing, and their cries carry feeling.

**Pre-Chorus — The question**

Of everything that issues from the mouth as sound, is there anything that does not arise from some inward unrest?

**Chorus — Music as expression**

Music is what has accumulated within and finds its release outside. It chooses those things best able to sound and makes them give voice. Metal, stone, silk, bamboo, gourd, clay, leather, and wood—the eight traditional classes of instruments—are the things most capable of making music.

**Verse 2 — The voices of the seasons**

Heaven acts in much the same way in the changing seasons: it chooses those most capable of sounding and makes them speak. Thus birds announce spring, thunder announces summer, insects announce autumn, and wind announces winter. As the seasons push and displace one another, must there not also be something out of balance within them?

**Verse 3 — Voices across antiquity**

It is the same among human beings. Of human sounds, the most refined is speech; and of speech, literary expression is finer still. Heaven particularly chooses those best able to give voice and lets them speak.

In the age of the sage-kings Yao and Shun, Gao Yao and Yu were among those especially gifted in expression, and so were made to speak. Kui could not make himself heard through literary words; instead, he spoke through the music of the *Shao*. During the Xia, the Five Princes made their voices heard in song. Yi Yin gave voice to the Shang, and the Duke of Zhou gave voice to the Zhou. All that is recorded in the *Book of Songs*, the *Book of Documents*, and the Six Arts bears witness to those who gave voice with distinction.

**Verse 4 — Philosophers and poets**

As the Zhou declined, Confucius and his followers spoke out, and their voices carried far and wide. It is written in the traditions: “Heaven will make the Master its wooden-tongued bell.” Could those words have been false?

Toward the dynasty’s end, Zhuang Zhou made himself heard through his extraordinary, unrestrained writing. Chu was a mighty state; as it fell, Qu Yuan became its voice. Zangsun Chen, Meng Ke, and Xun Qing spoke through their teachings of the Way.

**Rhythmic Bridge — Schools of thought and histories**

Yang Zhu, Mo Di, Guan Yiwu, Yan Ying, Lao Dan, Shen Buhai, Han Fei, Shen Dao, Tian Pian, Zou Yan, Shi Jiao, Sun Wu, Zhang Yi, Su Qin, and others like them all spoke through their own arts and doctrines. When Qin rose, Li Si gave it voice. In the Han age, Sima Qian, Sima Xiangru, and Yang Xiong were among the finest voices.

**Verse 5 — The Wei and Jin comparison**

In the subsequent Wei and Jin eras, those who gave voice did not match the ancients, although their voices never entirely disappeared. Even among the better writers, their sound was clear but insubstantial, their rhythms crowded and hurried, their language overindulgent and mournful, their ambitions lax and unrestrained, their words disordered and without structure.

Had Heaven come to dislike their character and cease to look upon them? Why did it not allow those best able to sound to make themselves heard?

**Build — The Tang literary tradition and Meng Jiao**

When the Tang came to rule, Chen Zi’ang, Su Yuanming, Yuan Jie, Li Bai, Du Fu, and Li Guan each made themselves heard through their particular gifts. Among those still living but occupying a lowly position was Meng Jiao, called Dongye, who began to make his voice heard through his poetry. At its best, his writing rises above the Wei and Jin and strives tirelessly toward antiquity; in other respects, it is deeply steeped in the Han tradition.

Of those who have studied and traveled with me, Li Ao and Zhang Ji are especially outstanding. The voices of these three men are truly excellent.

**Final Chorus — Recognition or hardship?**

But I do not know whether Heaven will harmonize their voices and let them sing of the country’s prosperity—or whether it will reduce their bodies to hunger and poverty, fill their hearts with sorrow, and make them give voice to their own misfortune.

**Resolution — A life’s worth beyond rank**

The fates of these three men rest with Heaven. If they rise to high position, why should they be overjoyed? If they remain in low position, why should they be overcome by grief?

**Outro — The purpose of this farewell**

As Dongye set out for his official duties in Jiangnan, he seemed unable to put his worries aside. Therefore I spoke to him of the fate that rests with Heaven, in the hope of offering him comfort.
