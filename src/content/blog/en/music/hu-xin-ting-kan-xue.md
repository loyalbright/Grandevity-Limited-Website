Some forms of solitude begin when no one else is present.

A kindred spirit may appear when, in the one place no one should have come, another lamp is already burning.

**Watching Snow at the Lake Pavilion** is a Mandarin male-vocal work that sets the complete original text of Zhang Dai’s celebrated prose piece **“Huxinting Kan Xue”** to music.

The song contains no modern expansion, rewriting or additional lyrics. No original line is repeated. Zhang Dai’s text unfolds once, in its original order, while melody, instrumentation, silence and instrumental space extend the cold, scale and emotional movement already present in the prose.

The story begins after three days of heavy snow on West Lake.

Human voices have vanished.

Birdcalls have vanished as well.

After the night watch has been set, Zhang Dai wraps himself in a fur-lined robe, carries warmth from a brazier, takes a small boat and crosses the lake alone to watch the snow.

He believes himself to be the only person possessed by such devotion.

Then, inside the pavilion, someone looks up in delight and exclaims:

> How could there be another person like this on the lake!

This is more than a greeting.

It is the instant in which one solitary person recognises another.

It is also the warmest, briefest and most unforgettable moment in **Watching Snow at the Lake Pavilion**.

### “The Fifth Year of Chongzhen, the Twelfth Month”: A Precise Date Opens into an Eternal Snow Night

The prose does not begin by describing scenery. It first establishes time and place:

> In the twelfth month of the fifth year of Chongzhen, I was living by West Lake.

“Chongzhen, fifth year” fixes the event within the late Ming world. “The twelfth month” establishes winter. The apparently simple statement “I was living by West Lake” tells us that Zhang Dai is not a hurried visitor.

He knows the lake.

He knows its ordinary voices, gatherings and pleasures.

That familiarity makes the lake after three days of snow feel radically unfamiliar.

A celebrated site of boating, sightseeing and convivial life has been removed from everyday society and transformed into an almost unclaimed field of white.

---

### “Every Human Voice and Birdcall Had Ceased”: The World Is Emptied through Sound

> Snow fell heavily for three days; every human voice and birdcall on the lake had ceased.

Zhang Dai does not accumulate elaborate adjectives for snow.

He writes “three days,” allowing duration itself to imply depth and weight. He then empties the lake through sound rather than sight.

The sentence does not say that no person or bird could be seen.

It says that none could be heard.

Snow covers roads, roofs, the long causeway and the distant hills. It also seems to cover the ordinary acoustic life of the world.

The lake remains.

The mountains remain.

The pavilion remains.

Human society has temporarily withdrawn, leaving a silence that feels nearly absolute.

The song therefore refuses to fill its opening too quickly. Winter wind, lake ambience, guqin harmonics and xiao establish the weight of absence before the full arrangement enters.

### “I Went Alone”: Solitude as a Deliberate Journey

> That day, after the night watch had been set, I took a small boat, wrapped myself in a fur-lined robe beside the brazier’s warmth, and went alone to the Lake Pavilion to watch the snow.

The watch has been set: the night is already deep.

After three days of heavy snow, travel is difficult and the lake is bitterly cold. Most people have shut their doors.

Zhang Dai chooses this precise moment to depart.

The fur-lined robe and brazier show that he understands the cold and prepares for it. The small boat makes the journey narrow and exposed. The word “alone” separates him from ordinary social life completely.

He has not merely been left in solitude.

He enters it by choice.

He is not travelling toward a conventional banquet. He is keeping an appointment with snow, water, darkness and a distant pavilion.

The “obsession” or *chi* of the final line is therefore not simple foolishness.

It is devotion to beauty without practical reward: the willingness to spend time, comfort and effort upon an experience that cannot be possessed.

---

### “Above and Below, All Was One White”: When Boundaries Disappear

> Rime and mist spread in a boundless haze. Sky and cloud, mountain and water—above and below, all was one white.

This is the widest visual centre of the prose and the point at which the song’s melody fully opens.

Within rime and frozen mist, sky, cloud, mountain and water cease to remain distinct.

Above and below lose their boundary.

Near and far lose their scale.

Things that could ordinarily be named and separated dissolve into a single field of white.

“Everything was white” does not merely indicate the quantity of snow. It describes a transformation of perception. Once the world holds only one colour, its familiar hierarchy is temporarily erased.

In the production, the cold space of guqin and xiao expands at this moment. Fender Rhodes, Neo-Soul bass, brushed drums and strings enter in layers, yet the climax never becomes bombastic.

The melody grows wider and more luminous, while the human figure remains small.

The grandeur belongs not to the traveller, but to the world he is willing to face.

### “One Trace, One Point, One Mustard Seed, Two or Three Grains”: Humanity Reduced to Dust

After the enormous whiteness of heaven and earth, Zhang Dai abruptly turns to a sequence of miniature measures:

> Of the shadows upon the lake, there remained only one trace of the long causeway, one point of the lake pavilion, my boat like one mustard seed, and the people within it merely two or three grains.

The causeway is no longer a road, but one trace.

The pavilion is no longer a building, but one point.

The boat becomes a mustard seed.

The people become two or three grains.

The imagined viewpoint seems to rise higher and higher.

Human beings, architecture and the boat recede until they are nearly indistinguishable from particles within the landscape.

Yet this smallness does not deny human value.

It makes human attention more precious. A person who is almost nothing in the scale of the world still chooses to depart, to look, to feel and to remember.

The MV translates this sequence into extreme wide shots, elevated perspectives and generous negative space. “One trace, one point, one mustard seed, two or three grains” becomes not only a lyric, but a visible change in scale.

---

### “How Could There Be Another Person Like This?”: Recognition Before Introduction

When Zhang Dai reaches the pavilion, the prose turns suddenly from cold to warmth:

> Upon reaching the pavilion, I found two men seated opposite each other upon felt mats. A boy was heating wine, and the pot was boiling.

Outside: one white world, emptied of human and bird sound.

Inside: felt mats, wine, fire and two other people who have also come into the snow night.

When they see Zhang Dai, they do not first ask his name, family or rank. They exclaim:

> How could there be another person like this on the lake!

“This person” means more than another visitor.

It means another human being who, after three days of snow and in the depth of night, would cross the lake for the sole purpose of seeing it.

They recognise a shared disposition before learning one another’s identities.

This is the prose’s distinctive vision of kinship.

A kindred spirit need not be someone known for years.

Sometimes it is simply the person who appears at the one hour when everyone else has chosen to remain away.

---

### “They Were from Jinling, Lodging Here”: The Names Vanish, but the Meeting Remains

> They drew me in to drink with them. I obliged by drinking three large cups and then took my leave. When I asked their names, I learned only that they were people from Jinling, lodging here.

The meeting does not become a long feast, and the prose does not preserve complete names.

Zhang Dai learns only that the two men are from Jinling and are temporary guests in Hangzhou.

Three large cups are followed by departure.

Its brevity makes the encounter feel almost miraculous.

No promise of reunion is made. No future relationship is arranged.

Yet one cry of surprise, a few cups of warm wine and a shared field of snow are enough to be remembered.

In many stories, the absence of names creates regret.

Here it purifies the meeting. These men are not drawn together by status, lineage or advantage. They meet because each possesses the same seemingly impractical devotion to the snow.

---

### “There Are Others as Obsessed as You”: The Boatman Completes the Meaning

> When I descended to the boat, the boatman murmured, “Do not say, sir, that you alone are obsessed; there are others even as obsessed as you.”

The prose does not end with Zhang Dai explaining his feelings.

The final judgement belongs to the boatman, who has rowed throughout the journey and watched from the edge of the event.

He has seen Zhang Dai travel into the snow.

He has seen the two guests from Jinling.

In a voice close to private muttering, he gives the entire encounter its final name: *chi*, obsession, devotion, a beautiful kind of folly.

There is mild exasperation in the line, but also understanding.

The boatman may not share the scholars’ aesthetic passion, yet he recognises that his passenger is not alone.

The word *chi* does not mean stupidity here.

It means a willingness to give time, warmth and distance to something “useless” but deeply loved.

The prose returns to silence.

The guests remain behind.

The wine pot cools.

The small boat moves back into the snowy night.

But the resonance of devoted souls recognising one another remains upon the lake.

### Cinematic Mandarin Soul and Chinese-Inspired R&B: Moving from Stillness to Vastness, Warmth and Return

The final production is built around **Premium Cinematic Mandopop Soul with Refined Chinese-Inspired R&B**, at approximately 68 BPM in a slow-burning 6/8 metre, using a through-composed narrative structure.

The arrangement features:

* **A cultured, warm and restrained Standard Mandarin male vocal**, with clear classical-Chinese diction and no shouting, operatic delivery or excessive melisma.
* **Guqin, xiao, winter wind and lake ambience**, establishing space before the traveller enters it.
* **Fender Rhodes and nylon-string guitar**, creating warm modern harmony beneath the late-Ming prose.
* **Deep Neo-Soul bass and brushed drums**, maintaining the pulse of a slowly advancing boat without disturbing the silence.
* **Pipa, light guzheng and elegant strings**, shaping rime, reflected snowlight and the pavilion’s warmth without becoming theatrical.
* A further hollowing of the sound field at **“every human voice and birdcall had ceased,”** allowing silence itself to function as music.
* The song’s widest, most luminous melodic expansion at **“sky and cloud, mountain and water—above and below, all was one white.”**
* A delicate contraction at **“one trace, one point, one mustard seed,”** where lighter instrumentation mirrors the shrinking visual scale.
* A warmer harmonic colour around the boiling wine and brazier, while maintaining the restraint of a scholarly meeting.
* A quiet emotional peak at **“How could there be another person like this on the lake!”**, expressed through wonder rather than volume.
* A graceful, nearly half-spoken conclusion at **“there are others even as obsessed as you,”** returning the song to snow, distance and water.

The work runs approximately 4 minutes and 10 seconds. Zhang Dai’s text is sung only once.

Its duration is created not by repeating lyrics, but through the introduction, instrumental bridges, breathing space between sentences and the extended final outro.

### The MV: From a Cold Blue Lake to One Point of Human Warmth and Back into Silence

The MV places the narrative entirely within West Lake during the twelfth month of the fifth year of Chongzhen.

The exterior palette is built from snow white, mist blue, ink-dark teal and distant mountain grey. Inside the pavilion, wood, charcoal and soft amber light create a human counterpoint.

Zhang Dai is consistently depicted as a thirty-six-year-old late-Ming scholar wearing a moon-white patterned cross-collar inner robe, a grey-blue outer robe, a deep ink-blue fur-trimmed winter cloak and a black scholar’s soft cap.

He is not presented as a theatrical hero. He is quiet, refined and fully aware of the cold, yet unable to resist the journey.

The boatman is a forty-five-year-old Jiangnan ferryman in a dark padded coat and weathered conical hat. The small wooden craft retains the same simple woven canopy throughout the film.

The two scholars and the young attendant inside the pavilion also maintain fixed appearances, ensuring that the meeting is read as a clear human encounter rather than a collection of anonymous silhouettes.

To preserve the late-Ming atmosphere, lamps use wooden or bamboo frames with white silk or plain oiled-paper shades, lit by small oil vessels or short candles. Modern broad glass lanterns are excluded.

The visual arc moves through three principal transitions:

* From the faint warmth of the lakeside residence into the immense cold of the open snow lake.
* From the extreme scale of “all was one white” toward the boiling wine and concentrated warmth of the pavilion.
* From the brief meeting of kindred spirits back into the boat and the final silence of one trace, one point and one mustard seed.

By the closing images, the people recede.

Heaven, water and snow become the principal subjects again.

This is the visual scale at the heart of the prose: the human figure is almost nothing, yet the feeling of one night can travel across centuries.

### A Song with No Expanded Lyrics and No Need for Them

**Watching Snow at the Lake Pavilion** adds no modern chorus, explanatory paraphrase or newly written emotional maxim.

The original prose already contains a complete and precisely controlled dramatic arc:

Three days of snow establish absolute silence.

A solitary boat after the night watch begins the human action.

“One white above and below” reaches the landscape’s vast climax.

“One trace, one point, one mustard seed, two or three grains” contracts the scale.

The pavilion fire restores human warmth.

“How could there be another person like this?” completes the recognition of kindred spirits.

The boatman’s final remark leaves the last resonance.

Additional lyrics could easily disturb this exact movement from cold to warmth, vastness to minuteness, solitude to encounter, and encounter back to solitude.

The production therefore allows Zhang Dai’s own language to complete the story.

Music does not explain the prose.

It gives audible space to snow, water, darkness, fire and pause.

### About the Project

**Watching Snow at the Lake Pavilion** is part of Grandevity Studio’s *Classical Literature Reimagined in Music* series.

The project seeks to bring classical Chinese prose beyond textbooks, memorisation, annotation and translation, allowing it to re-enter contemporary melody, sound, imagery and emotional experience.

Zhang Dai preserved more than a view of snow upon West Lake.

He preserved an unusual form of human recognition:

No name is required first.

No status needs to be established.

Two people discover that, on the same deserted night, they have chosen to see the same snow, and that is enough to recognise one another.

Not every kindred spirit must be known for years.

Not every meeting must be prolonged.

Sometimes the rarest gift is simply this:

When heaven and earth have turned white and every sound has ceased, someone else is still looking at the same snow.
