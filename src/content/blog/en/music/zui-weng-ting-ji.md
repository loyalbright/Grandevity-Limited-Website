Most readers remember one line from Ouyang Xiu’s **Zuiweng Ting Ji**:

> The Old Drunkard’s intention is not in the wine, but in the mountains and waters.

It is one of the most famous sentences in Classical Chinese prose.

But it is not the final answer of the essay.

Read to the end, and the meaning of joy keeps expanding.

The birds know the joy of the mountain forest.

The people of Chuzhou know the joy of wandering through it.

The guests know the joy of the banquet.

And the governor finds his own joy in theirs.

> The people know the joy of following the governor on his outings, but they do not know that the governor takes joy in their joy.

That final transformation is the heart of **Zuiweng Ting Ji**.

Grandevity Studio’s musical setting uses Ouyang Xiu’s complete original text, with no modern lyrical expansion, no paraphrased chorus and no newly written lyrics.

The composition follows the actual sung progression of the finished work:

mountains and pavilion → morning, evening and four seasons → the people of Chuzhou and the governor’s feast → the guests’ joy and the governor’s drunkenness → sunset and the birds → the governor’s joy and Ouyang Xiu’s final self-identification.

What begins as landscape finally becomes a vision of shared human joy.

### The Essay Begins with Mountains, Not with the Governor

The first sentence is famously economical:

> 環滁皆山也。
>
> All around Chuzhou are mountains.

Ouyang Xiu does not begin with himself.

He begins with geography.

The prose then narrows its field of vision.

The southwestern peaks.

The beautiful forests and valleys.

Langya Mountain.

A mountain path.

The sound of running water.

Brewing Spring.

And only after the peaks turn and the road bends does the pavilion appear.

This movement from the immense to the intimate gives the prose a cinematic quality long before cinema existed.

The human figure does not dominate the landscape.

He enters it.

---

### The Old Drunkard’s Intention Is Not in the Wine

Ouyang Xiu introduces himself with humour.

The governor comes to drink with his guests.

He becomes drunk after little wine and, being the eldest among them, calls himself the Old Drunkard.

Then comes the celebrated sentence:

> 醉翁之意不在酒，在乎山水之間也。
>
> The Old Drunkard’s intention is not in the wine, but in the mountains and waters.

Yet the next sentence is equally important:

> 山水之樂，得之心而寓之酒也。
>
> The joy of the landscape is received in the heart and entrusted to the wine.

Wine is a vessel.

The real event occurs in perception.

The cup may be small, but what is placed within it is an entire landscape and the human capacity to respond to it.

---

### Morning, Evening and the Four Seasons

The second movement of the essay gives the landscape to time.

Sunrise opens the forest mist.

Returning clouds darken the rocky hollows.

Spring flowers release a subtle fragrance.

Summer trees produce abundant shade.

Autumn wind and frost bring clarity.

Winter lowers the water and exposes stone.

Ouyang Xiu never declares one season superior to another.

The reason is contained in his conclusion:

> 四時之景不同，而樂亦無窮也。
>
> The scenery of the four seasons is different, and the joy is inexhaustible.

Joy is inexhaustible precisely because the world does not remain the same.

Attention can meet it again and again.

---

### When the People of Chuzhou Enter the Landscape

The essay then makes a decisive transition.

Ordinary people appear.

Those carrying loads sing along the road.

Travellers rest beneath trees.

Those ahead call.

Those behind answer.

The elderly and children move together.

People come and go without end.

Ouyang Xiu calls this:

> 滁人遊也。
>
> The outing of the people of Chuzhou.

The landscape is no longer a private aesthetic possession.

It has become shared space.

This is where the meaning of joy begins to move beyond the cultivated individual.

The mountain belongs not merely to the governor who appreciates it, but to the people who walk, rest, sing and gather within it.

---

### The Governor’s Feast Grows Directly from the Landscape

The feast does not arrive from some distant world of luxury.

Fish come from the stream.

Wine is made from Brewing Spring.

Mountain foods and wild vegetables are arranged before the guests.

The banquet belongs to the same landscape that produced the opening of the essay.

Nature, local life and human fellowship remain continuous.

---

### “The Joy of the Feast Was Not in Strings or Flutes”

> 宴酣之樂，非絲非竹。
>
> The joy of the feast does not depend on strings or flutes.

There is no need for elaborate entertainment.

Some guests score in pitch-pot.

Others win at board games.

Cups and tally sticks cross.

People rise, sit, speak and laugh.

The essential conclusion is simple:

> 眾賓歡也。
>
> The guests are joyful.

And among them sits the governor himself:

grey-faced,

white-haired,

relaxed,

drunk.

He does not stand apart from the happiness of others.

He is physically within it.

---

### When the Visitors Leave, the Birds Recover the Mountain

Soon the sun reaches the mountain.

Human shadows scatter.

The governor returns.

His guests follow.

The woods grow dark.

Bird calls rise and fall.

Then Ouyang Xiu writes:

> 遊人去而禽鳥樂也。
>
> The visitors have gone, and the birds are joyful.

This matters because human joy does not erase the joy of the natural world.

People come.

People celebrate.

People leave.

The forest continues.

The essay that began with mountains now begins to return to them.

---

### The Governor Takes Joy in Their Joy

The final movement establishes several levels of joy.

Birds know the joy of the mountain forest, but not the joy of human beings.

People know the joy of following the governor on his outings.

But they do not understand the governor’s own joy:

> 人知從太守遊而樂，而不知太守之樂其樂也。

The phrase **樂其樂** is the philosophical centre of the work.

The governor takes joy in their joy.

His deepest pleasure is therefore not simply private enjoyment of beautiful mountains.

It is relational.

He is happy because the people around him are able to be happy.

This is why the true climax of **Zuiweng Ting Ji** comes later than its most famous sentence.

“The Old Drunkard’s intention is not in the wine” opens the idea.

“The governor takes joy in their joy” completes it.

---

### Drunk, He Shares Their Joy; Sober, He Writes It Down

The final lines join drunkenness, fellowship and literature:

> 醉能同其樂，
> 醒能述以文者，
> 太守也。

Drunk, he can share their joy.

Sober, he can describe it in writing.

A mountain outing passes.

A feast ends.

Sunset arrives.

People go home.

But writing preserves the experience.

Only at the end does Ouyang Xiu ask:

> 太守謂誰？
>
> Who is the governor?

And answer:

> 廬陵歐陽修也。
>
> Ouyang Xiu of Luling.

The Old Drunkard, the governor and the author finally become one.

---

### Cinematic Chinese Narrative: Letting the Prose Create Its Own Climax

The musical setting is built around **Cinematic Chinese Narrative / Cinematic Guofeng / Classical Chinese Fusion**.

No modern chorus has been added.

No contemporary lyric interrupts the prose.

No newly written line attempts to summarise Ouyang Xiu.

Instead, the finished song follows six narrative-musical movements:

**Mountains, Spring and the Old Drunkard’s Pavilion**

The journey moves from Chuzhou’s mountains to Langya, Brewing Spring, the pavilion and finally the Old Drunkard himself.

**Morning, Evening and the Four Seasons**

Human activity recedes while time and seasonal change take control of the musical space.

**The People of Chuzhou and the Governor’s Feast**

The quiet mountain begins to fill with human voices and shared activity.

**The Guests Rejoice, the Governor Becomes Drunk**

The social energy reaches its highest point.

**Sunset and the Joy of the Birds**

The human world retreats and the sonic field returns to forest, evening and birdsong.

**The Governor’s Joy — Ouyang Xiu of Luling**

The final movement turns from collective pleasure toward the deeper idea of taking joy in the joy of others, before the author calmly reveals his own name.

The music does not manufacture a climax for the prose.

It allows the original prose enough time to reach its own.

### From “My Joy” to the Joy of Others

“The Old Drunkard’s intention is not in the wine” has survived for nearly a thousand years.

But the phrase that may deserve equal attention comes much later:

> the governor takes joy in their joy.

The joy of landscape means that one person remains capable of responding to the world.

The joy of the people of Chuzhou means that ordinary people are able to move through and enjoy that world.

The joy of the guests means that people can gather.

The governor’s joy is larger still.

He finds happiness in seeing these other forms of happiness exist.

The mountains remain.

The wine remains.

The Old Drunkard still becomes drunk.

But what the essay finally preserves is fellowship.
