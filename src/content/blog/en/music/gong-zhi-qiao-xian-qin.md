A craftsman creates an exceptional qin.

Its sound is described as:

> 金聲而玉應。
>
> Resonant like metal, answering like jade.

He believes it to be one of the finest instruments in the world.

He presents it for official inspection.

The verdict consists of only two characters:

> 弗古。
>
> Not ancient.

The qin is returned.

Gong Zhiqiao does not make a better instrument.

Instead, he makes the same instrument look older.

Artificial age cracks are added.

Ancient-style marks are carved into it.

The qin is placed in a case and buried in the earth.

A year later it is taken out again.

This time, a man of high status sees it.

He pays a hundred pieces of gold.

The qin is presented at court.

The music officials pass it from hand to hand.

Their judgment is unanimous:

> 希世之珍也。
>
> A treasure rarely seen in the world.

Did the qin become better?

No.

What changed was the way people saw it.

Grandevity Studio’s **Gong Zhiqiao Presents a Qin** sets the project’s established version of Liu Ji’s complete original text to newly composed music.

There is no modern lyrical expansion.

No paraphrased rewriting.

No newly invented chorus.

No added lyrics.

No original sentence is repeated simply to extend the song.

The music moves through the narrative once:

fine paulownia → making the qin → presentation → “not ancient”  
rejection → artificial cracks → ancient marks → burial  
market → one hundred gold → court → “a rare treasure”  
realisation → lament → “is it only one qin?”  
departure → the remote mountains → disappearance

The allegory itself becomes the musical structure.

### Liu Ji and the Yuli Zi

**Gong Zhiqiao Presents a Qin** is transmitted in Liu Ji’s **Yuli Zi**, a work associated with the late Yuan and early Ming intellectual world.

Liu Ji, courtesy name Bowen, was an important writer, thinker and political figure.

The **Yuli Zi** repeatedly uses allegories, anecdotes and arguments to examine human judgment, power, institutions and social behaviour.

This episode is remarkably short.

Its construction is also remarkably precise.

First:

the qin is genuinely excellent.

> 金聲而玉應。

The result:

> 弗古。

Not ancient.

Second:

the instrument’s sound and craftsmanship have not improved.

Only its external signs have changed.

Artificial cracks.

Ancient-looking marks.

A year in the soil.

The result:

> 希世之珍也。

A treasure rarely seen in the world.

The same object receives two completely different judgments.

The reader knows why.

The qin itself has not changed.

### “金聲而玉應”: The Story First Establishes Genuine Quality

Liu Ji does not leave the qin’s quality uncertain.

He writes:

> 弦而鼓之，
> 金聲而玉應。

It sounds beautiful.

That fact matters.

If the instrument were poor, the story would merely concern a mistaken method of appraisal.

Instead, genuine quality is established before the rejection.

The official examiner therefore faces a revealing choice.

Does he judge the instrument by how it sounds?

Or by whether it looks sufficiently old?

His answer is only:

> 弗古。

---

### “Not Ancient”: When a Label Replaces Evaluation

The official craftsman does not say:

The tone is poor.

The wood is poor.

The craftsmanship is poor.

The resonance is poor.

He says:

Not ancient.

A condition external to musical quality replaces evaluation of the instrument itself.

For this reason, the song does not turn the moment into a theatrical confrontation.

There is no shouting.

No dramatic historical declamation.

The arrangement simply stops.

The colder the judgment,

the sharper the irony.

---

### Artificial Cracks and Ancient Marks: Manufacturing Evidence

After the qin is returned,

Gong Zhiqiao does not try to improve its sound.

There is nothing to improve.

Instead, he changes the evidence people will use to interpret it.

> 謀諸漆工，
> 作斷紋焉；
>
> 又謀諸篆工，
> 作古窾焉。

Cracks suggest age.

Ancient-style markings suggest provenance.

Then:

> 匣而埋諸土，
> 期年出之。

The appearance of time itself is manufactured.

The allegory becomes an experiment:

If genuine quality is not enough to persuade people,

will the symbols of value succeed instead?

The answer is yes.

---

### “A Hundred Gold”: Price Joins the Story

> 貴人過而見之，
> 易之以百金。

The qin that was once rejected is now worth a hundred gold.

The change in price becomes another sign.

It looks old.

Therefore it seems valuable.

Someone powerful pays heavily for it.

Therefore the high price appears justified.

That powerful owner presents it at court.

Institutional prestige now surrounds the instrument.

A chain of validation begins to form:

appearance,

price,

status,

court,

experts.

Each signal appears to confirm all the others.

And the original question quietly disappears:

Is the qin itself good?

---

### The Most Important Word May Be “All”

The original says:

> 樂官傳視，
> 皆曰：
>
> 「希世之珍也。」

The music officials pass the qin from one to another.

**All** declare it a rare treasure.

The satire is no longer about one mistaken expert.

It is about collective judgment.

People do not only inspect the object.

They also observe the reactions of other people.

Price validates prestige.

Prestige validates institutional approval.

Institutional approval validates expertise.

Expertise validates price.

Soon everyone possesses evidence.

Few may still be judging independently.

---

### “豈獨一琴哉?”: Is This Really Only About a Qin?

Gong Zhiqiao does not celebrate his successful experiment.

He laments:

> 悲哉世也！
>
> 豈獨一琴哉？
> 莫不然矣！

Alas for the world.

Is it only one qin?

Is not everything like this?

With those questions,

the scale of the allegory suddenly expands.

Talent.

Art.

Scholarship.

Reputation.

Price.

Social position.

Authority.

Institutional prestige.

How much of what we value comes from the thing itself?

And how much comes from the signs that tell us we are supposed to value it?

That is why this short allegory remains unsettlingly contemporary.

---

### “不知其所終”: There Is No Comfortable Resolution

The story closes:

> 遂去，
> 入於宕冥之山，
>
> 不知其所終。

Gong Zhiqiao leaves.

He enters the remote mountains.

No one knows where he finally ends.

There is no public revelation.

No reform of the system.

No triumphant victory.

He has proved his point.

That does not mean the world changes.

The coldness of the ending is essential.

The problem remains.

Gong Zhiqiao simply withdraws from it.

### Music: The More Artificial the Antiquity, the More Luxurious the Surface

The musical foundation is:

**Sophisticated Mandarin Chamber-Pop Satire / Refined Chinese-inspired R&B**

Tempo: 72 BPM.

Meter: 6/8.

Form: through-composed.

No line from Liu Ji’s text is repeated.

The arrangement combines:

- guqin
- muted wooden percussion
- Fender Rhodes
- nylon-string guitar
- neo-soul bass
- brushed drums
- pipa
- xiao
- chamber strings

The opening is clear, sincere and warm.

The qin is genuinely beautiful.

At:

> 弗古。

the music leaves a dry and exposed pause.

The ageing sequence gradually darkens the harmony.

Yet an intentional paradox develops:

as the qin becomes more artificial,

the surface of the arrangement becomes more refined.

At:

> 希世之珍也。

the sound reaches its most luxurious state.

It is also its most emotionally hollow moment.

Then:

> 悲哉世也！

rhythm retreats.

The ornament falls away.

Guqin and low strings return.

At:

> 不知其所終。

there is no triumphant cadence.

Only residual resonance,

low strings,

and distant mountain wind.

### The Qin Did Not Change

The disturbing power of **Gong Zhiqiao Presents a Qin** does not come simply from an expert failing to recognise quality.

It comes from the way an entire system of signals can replace direct judgment.

Without the expected appearance,

genuine excellence is rejected.

Give the same object:

the appearance of age,

marks of pedigree,

a high price,

a prestigious owner,

a court setting,

and unanimous professional approval,

and suddenly everyone sees value.

Gong Zhiqiao therefore asks:

> 豈獨一琴哉？
>
> Is it only one qin?

More than six centuries later,

the question remains open.
