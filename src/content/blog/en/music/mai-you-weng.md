One archer could hit the target eight or nine times out of ten.

One elderly oil seller stopped to watch.

The archer expected admiration.

The old man merely nodded.

> 無他，但手熟爾。
>
> Nothing extraordinary. It is simply a matter of a practised hand.

The story's most memorable moment does not come when the arrow leaves the bow.

It comes when a fine thread of oil passes through the square hole of a copper coin into a gourd, and the coin remains dry.

In a remarkably short prose work, the Northern Song writer Ouyang Xiu explores the distance between skill and pride, public reputation and everyday mastery.

Why should an accomplished archer be offended by a quiet nod?

And what could an elderly oil seller possibly know about archery?

The answer comes not through an argument, but through a demonstration.

> 我亦無他，惟手熟爾。
>
> I have nothing extraordinary either. My hand is simply practised.

The same principle is stated twice.

The first time, the archer rejects it.

The second time, the oil seller demonstrates it.

### Ouyang Xiu and Gui Tian Lu: History Told Through Anecdote

**Ouyang Xiu (1007–1072)**, courtesy name Yongshu, was a major writer, historian and statesman of China's Northern Song dynasty.

Known by the literary names Zuiweng, meaning "the Old Drunkard", and later Liuyi Jushi, or "Recluse of the Six Ones", he is counted among the **Eight Great Masters of Tang and Song Prose**.

His ancestral home was in Luling, in the region of present-day Ji'an, Jiangxi Province.

After passing the imperial examinations in 1030, Ouyang served in government while becoming one of the leading advocates of a more lucid and substantive style of classical prose.

His works range from political and historical writing to intimate essays about landscape, friendship and the passage of time.

*The Oil Seller* is preserved in his anecdotal collection *Gui Tian Lu* (歸田錄), often translated as *Records of Returning to the Fields*.

Ouyang dated the preface to that collection to **1067**.

It gathers court memories and stories about scholar-official society that a formal dynastic history might not record in the same way.

The archer in this episode was a historical person, but the specific exchange should be read as a literary anecdote rather than assumed to be a verbatim transcript of a documented conversation.

This distinction is important.

The historical background helps us understand the social positions of the characters.

The literary form helps us understand why a slight nod, an angry question and a small practical demonstration have such extraordinary narrative force.

### Chen Yaozi: A Real Master Archer with a Proud Temperament

Chen Kangsugong is **Chen Yaozi (970–1034)**, a Northern Song official who placed first in the imperial examinations in **1000** and was renowned for his skill with the bow.

"Kangsu" is his posthumous title.

Ouyang Xiu does not begin by discrediting Chen's ability.

Quite the opposite.

> 陳康肅公堯咨善射，當世無雙，公亦以此自矜。
>
> Chen Yaozi, Lord Kangsu, excelled at archery and was unmatched in his day; he was also proud of his skill.

The first statement establishes mastery.

The last phrase, "proud of his skill", establishes the flaw the anecdote will quietly examine.

Chen's skill is real.

The question is what happens when a person begins to measure personal worth through the admiration that skill receives.

### "He Watched Sideways and Did Not Leave"

While Chen is practising archery in his garden, an elderly oil seller sets down his carrying pole and stands watching.

> 睨之，久而不去。

The verb describing the old man's glance is especially revealing.

He does not stand in reverence.

He simply watches, somewhat obliquely, for a considerable time.

Nor does he interrupt the archer.

He observes.

When Chen hits eight or nine times out of ten, the response is:

> 但微頷之。
>
> He merely nodded slightly.

That is the first real turning point.

Chen expects admiration for a skill that has brought him distinction.

The elderly stranger responds as if he has seen something understandable.

The conflict begins not with a hostile statement, but with a difference in perspective.

### "Do You Know Archery Too?"

Chen asks:

> 汝亦知射乎？吾射不亦精乎？

Do you know archery too?

Is my shooting not excellent?

These are not entirely neutral questions.

Chen wants recognition.

He wants the stranger to acknowledge the quality of his archery.

The old man replies:

> 無他，但手熟爾。
>
> There is nothing special to it. Your hand is simply practised.

He does not explicitly deny that Chen is skilful.

Instead, he explains the source of the skill.

What Chen understands as exceptional personal distinction, the old man understands as the result of repeated practice.

Chen hears this as an insult.

### "How Dare You Make Light of My Archery!"

> 康肅忿然曰：「爾安敢輕吾射！」

Chen grows angry.

His response is no longer simply about whether the arrows struck their target.

Social hierarchy now enters the encounter.

Chen is an official, a distinguished examination graduate and a celebrated archer.

The old man is an ordinary seller of oil.

Yet the oil seller remains calm.

He does not claim a higher rank.

He does not challenge Chen to a contest.

He offers to demonstrate something from his own work.
