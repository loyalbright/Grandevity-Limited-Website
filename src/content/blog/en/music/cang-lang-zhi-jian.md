Some people spend a lifetime guarding what remains unstained.

Some row through layer after layer of turbid water and still retain the breadth to turn away with a smile.

Most of us, however, stand somewhere between the clear and the clouded—trying not to lose ourselves while learning how to continue living.

**Between the Canglang Waters** is a contemporary Mandarin R&B narrative inspired by **The Fisherman** from **Songs of Chu**.

The song is not a word-for-word musical setting of the complete classical text.

It draws upon the work’s central encounter, philosophical structure, river imagery and enduring lines:

> The whole world is turbid, while I alone am clear.  
> Everyone is drunk, while I alone am awake.

> The sage is not bound by external things,  
> but can move with the changing world.

It also reimagines the fisherman’s Canglang song:

> When the Canglang water is clear, I can wash my hat-cords.  
> When the Canglang water is turbid, I can wash my feet.

These ancient lines are brought into a modern emotional landscape.

What happens when moral clarity becomes exhausting?

What happens when commitment hardens into isolation?

Must a person choose only between absolute refusal and passive conformity?

**Between the Canglang Waters** does not abandon integrity, nor does it dismiss the fisherman’s adaptability.

Instead, it tries to honour two difficult forms of wisdom:

**the defiance that refuses to be covered in dust, and the breadth that can turn away without losing itself.**

### The Canglang Waters: Clear and Turbid Are Not Simple Answers

**The Fisherman** preserves one of the most enduring philosophical encounters in classical Chinese literature.

After being banished, Qu Yuan wanders beside the river with his hair unbound.

The fisherman recognises him and asks how he has come to such a condition.

Qu Yuan answers:

> The whole world is turbid, while I alone am clear.  
> Everyone is drunk, while I alone am awake.  
> That is why I have been cast out.

What he protects is the refusal to allow a corrupt world to enter and redefine the self.

For Qu Yuan, exile is more bearable than survival purchased through the abandonment of inner integrity.

The fisherman offers a different response:

> The sage is not bound by external things,  
> but can move with the changing world.

When the water is clear, it may wash the hat-cords.

When it is turbid, it may wash the feet.

The fisherman is not merely advising submission, nor is he mocking Qu Yuan’s integrity.

He represents adaptability, the preservation of life and the ability to avoid becoming permanently trapped by one unchanging response.

**Between the Canglang Waters** does not declare either man the victor.

It preserves clear and turbid water as a question that continues to move:

> If the Canglang waters are clear,  
> let them wash the wounds of my former days.  
> If the Canglang waters are turbid,  
> do not let them rise over the light within me.

Clear water can heal.

Turbid water cannot always be avoided.

The task is not to make the world remain permanently clear.

It is to keep some inner light from being submerged when difficult water passes.

### Qu Yuan and the Fisherman: Two Difficult Forms of Wisdom

Qu Yuan and the fisherman are often understood as opposite choices.

One remains steadfast.

One adapts.

One refuses compromise.

One moves with circumstance.

Yet **Between the Canglang Waters** does not reduce them to right and wrong.

It does not dismiss the fisherman as merely worldly, nor Qu Yuan as incapable of change.

The song states:

> For half a life I learned from Qu Yuan, guarding clear light;  
> later I understood the fisherman and crossed the weathered years.

Youth often understands Qu Yuan more easily.

At that stage of life, ideals appear most truthful when they remain pure. Awareness should never retreat. The authentic self must draw an unmistakable line against the world.

After more years of exhaustion, disappointment and realities that cannot be changed, the fisherman’s words acquire another meaning.

Turning away is not always betrayal.

Silence is not always defeat.

Refusing to spend one’s remaining strength upon an unwinnable conflict does not mean surrendering every conviction.

The song continues:

> Some hide their edge within silence—  
> not admitting defeat, but refusing further depletion.  
> Some place their tears into the river—  
> not because it does not hurt, but because they have learned release.

True breadth is not indifference.

It is the ability to continue caring without allowing every collision to consume the whole self.

### From “I Alone Am Clear” to “Moving with the World”

**Between the Canglang Waters** is not a complete musical setting of **The Fisherman**.

It preserves the classical work’s philosophical structure, its figures, its river imagery and its central lines, then develops a modern narrative through newly written Mandarin lyrics.

The contemporary protagonist is not the historical Qu Yuan.

He is an ordinary man living in a modern river city.

He smiles when expected, gradually gathers back the words he wanted to speak, and understands how to take one step backwards.

Yet he fears that after travelling too far, he may no longer recognise himself.

The classical idea of being “clear” therefore becomes more than political integrity.

It becomes modern self-recognition:

> I am not unable to take one step back,  
> nor do I insist on winning anything.  
> I only fear that after wandering too far,  
> I may no longer recognise myself.

Likewise, “moving with the world” is no longer only an abstract principle.

It becomes a river that bends, a skiff travelling towards the distance and one person finally accepting:

> Not every turning away  
> should be called losing one’s way.

The modern lyrics do not remove the contradiction within the original.

They allow it to remain and return the question to each listener:

How can we refuse to drift with the current without imprisoning ourselves forever upon the shore?

### Contemporary Mandarin R&B as Emotional Philosophy

The production avoids oversized historical spectacle, operatic singing and declamatory heroism.

Its primary language is restrained contemporary Mandarin R&B, shaped by warm harmonic textures, deep low-frequency movement, soft drums and understated Chinese musical colour.

The male vocal does not turn Qu Yuan’s clarity into an angry accusation.

It remains mature and inward, carrying the fatigue and understanding of someone who has already travelled through half a life.

The verses feel like quiet self-narration.

The chorus opens emotionally but never becomes a triumphant historical anthem.

The true culmination is not a promise that the protagonist will never change.

It is the line:

> May I remain awake, and may I remain open and vast.

This allows the song to move beyond opposition.

Wakefulness means not forgetting oneself.

Breadth means refusing to let wakefulness become another prison.

The recurring R&B pulse, deep warmth and carefully preserved space transform an ancient philosophical debate into something intimate: a modern man speaking quietly to himself beside a river.

### The MV: One Face, Two Eras and a River That Continues to Move

The MV takes place in a fictional contemporary East Asian river city.

Late autumn moves gradually towards early winter. Cold mist passes across the water, while wet blue-grey stone steps extend along the shore. An old dark-timber teahouse, weathered jetty, small skiffs and a long bridge exist within the same geography as distant glass towers.

The contemporary male lead is thirty-five years old.

Wearing a white shirt, charcoal-grey overcoat and black trousers, he moves through the riverbank, stone lane, teahouse and wooden jetty.

Qu Yuan appears with exactly the same face, age and physical proportions as the modern protagonist.

They are not presented as two unrelated men.

They resemble one consciousness reflected across different historical periods.

Qu Yuan wears an ivory cross-collar inner robe beneath a charcoal-blue outer robe. He is restrained, dignified and unwilling to yield.

The fisherman is a sixty-eight-year-old elder.

Lean and weathered, he wears coarse hemp and a muted blue-grey cloak. He crosses the river in a small dark wooden skiff, carrying calm humour and lived wisdom rather than supernatural authority.

The three figures form the philosophical structure of the MV:

* **Qu Yuan** represents awareness that refuses contamination.
* **The fisherman** represents the breadth to adapt and turn.
* **The contemporary protagonist** stands between them, seeking a third direction that belongs to neither.

The clear and turbid currents are never separated by an artificial line.

They intermingle naturally, changing with water movement, cloud shadow, wind and reflected daylight.

The teahouse, stone steps, jetty, bridge and skyline remain part of one continuous physical city, allowing classical thought to enter contemporary space.

Near the end, Qu Yuan’s reflection fades through changing light, water and mist.

The fisherman’s skiff continues towards the distance.

The modern protagonist does not become either of them.

At last, he returns his heart to the Canglang waters.

### Why This Ancient Dialogue Still Matters

**The Fisherman** is not a long work.

It does not construct complete philosophical systems through extended argument.

It simply places two people beside the water and allows each to state what he believes.

Yet the encounter has endured because it reaches a question that still has no universal answer:

How should a person preserve the self within an imperfect world?

A refusal to yield may protect integrity, but it may also consume the life that carries it.

Adaptation may preserve life, but repeated compromise may gradually erase direction.

**Between the Canglang Waters** does not attempt to end the debate.

It carries the question into the present and offers it to anyone who has remained silent in a crowd, been wounded by reality or questioned themselves after turning away.

Perhaps maturity does not mean finally choosing Qu Yuan or the fisherman.

Perhaps it means understanding them both and still finding a direction of one’s own.

May understanding the world never require us to abandon our original heart.

May guarding that heart never turn the rest of life into desolation.

**May I remain awake, and may I remain open and vast.**

More than two thousand years ago, Qu Yuan and the fisherman left an unresolved conversation beside a river.

One refused to allow integrity to be covered in dust.
