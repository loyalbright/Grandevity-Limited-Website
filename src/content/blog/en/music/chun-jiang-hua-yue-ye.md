Some moonlight belongs to a single night.

Some moonlight passes through a thousand years and continues to shine upon everyone who has ever longed for someone far away.

**A Moonlit Night on the Spring River** is a Mandarin male-vocal work composed entirely from Zhang Ruoxu’s celebrated Tang dynasty poem **Chun Jiang Hua Yue Ye**.

The lyrics preserve 100% of the original poem, in its original sequence. Nothing has been expanded, rewritten or replaced with modern verses. The song begins with “The spring river tide lies level with the sea” and concludes with “The falling moon stirs longing through all the riverside trees.”

The production is built upon ethereal Mandopop R&B, combining warm Rhodes piano, nylon-string guitar, deep neo-soul bass, soft brushed hip-hop drums, airy guzheng harmonics, delicate pipa, distant xiao flute and cinematic strings.

The purpose is not merely to place ancient words over contemporary accompaniment.

It is to allow the poem itself to become a living song again.

The poem begins with the rising spring tide.

River and sea appear to meet at the horizon, while the bright moon rises together with the water. Flowering woods, fragrant meadows, white sand and frost-like moonlight gradually lose their boundaries until the world seems to contain only a clear river sky and one solitary moon.

Then Zhang Ruoxu asks:

> Who first saw the moon beside this river?  
> In what year did this river moon first shine upon humankind?

At that moment, landscape becomes philosophy.

Human generations pass one after another.

The river moon appears unchanged.

A single white cloud then drifts away, and the poem turns from the scale of the cosmos toward the private world of separation.

A traveller remains upon a solitary boat.

A woman waits within a moonlit tower.

They look toward the same moon but cannot hear one another.

One is carried by the river.

The other remains beside the window.

Moonlight becomes the only presence capable of touching them both.

> At this moment we gaze beneath the same moon but cannot hear each other.  
> I wish to follow its radiance and flow to shine upon you.

This is the emotional centre of **A Moonlit Night on the Spring River**.

The greatest distance is not always the road between Jieshi and Xiaoxiang.

It may also be the space between two people who stand beneath the same moon and still cannot deliver a single sentence of longing to one another.

### “The Spring River Tide Lies Level with the Sea”

Zhang Ruoxu does not begin with a human figure.

He first opens an almost boundless field of water:

> The spring river tide lies level with the sea,  
> and over the sea the bright moon rises with the tide.

The river expands until it appears to meet the distant sea.

The moon is not simply suspended above the landscape.

It seems to be born together with the rising water.

Through this single opening image, moon, tide, river and sea begin to share one movement and one breath.

The poem immediately establishes an immense scale.

Moonlight travels over countless waves, reaching every bend of the spring river.

The MV enters this world through three opening images.

First comes an uninhabited river beneath the moon, covered in silver-blue mist and framed by flowering trees.

A moored wooden boat then appears beside drifting petals and a faint golden lamp.

Only in the third image does the young traveller stand at the bow and look toward the rising moon.

The character does not dominate the landscape.

He first appears as a small human presence within the much larger order of water, sky, tide and moon.

Throughout **A Moonlit Night on the Spring River**, the human figure remains physically small yet emotionally profound.

### “The Winding River Circles the Fragrant Meadow”

The river curves around a meadow filled with spring growth.

Moonlight touches the flowering woods and makes them gleam like fine particles of ice.

> The winding river circles the fragrant meadow;  
> moonlight upon the flowering woods makes them resemble sleet.  
> Frost seems to flow through the air, though none is seen to fall;  
> upon the islet, white sand disappears from sight.

Moonlight is no longer merely illumination.

It becomes frost, sleet, mist and a continuous field of silver.

The poem does not describe an actual winter landscape.

The spring night is so clear that the air appears filled with flowing frost.

The white sand of the river islet can no longer be distinguished from the brightness around it.

The boundaries between things begin to dissolve.

River and sky, sand and water, flowers and frost all enter the same luminous world.

The MV uses silver-blue moonlight, low mist, flowering trees and reflective water to preserve this visual unity.

It is not a dark night.

It is a night made almost transparent by light.

### “River and Sky Are One Colour, Without a Speck of Dust”

The river and sky merge into a single clear expanse:

> River and sky are one colour, without a speck of dust;  
> only a radiant solitary moon wheels in the heavens.

There is no visual clutter.

There is only the river, the sky and a single moon.

Zhang Ruoxu then asks the two questions that open the philosophical centre of the poem:

> Who first saw the moon beside this river?  
> In what year did this river moon first shine upon humankind?

The questions cannot be answered.

They do not require an answer.

They transform one spring night into a meditation upon beginnings, memory and the immeasurable depth of time.

No one looking at the moon tonight is the first person to do so.

Nor will anyone be the last.

In the MV, the traveller stands among reeds and flowering branches beside the river.

Faint human traces appear along the distant shore, suggesting generations that once stood beneath the same moon.

They are not ghosts or identified historical figures.

They are the nearly invisible presence of time itself.

The moon once illuminated them.

It now illuminates us.

### “Human Generations Continue Without End”

Zhang Ruoxu does not respond to human mortality with complete despair.

He writes:

> Human generations continue without end;  
> the river moon seems the same year after year.

Individual lives disappear.

One generation is followed by another.

Yet human existence does not simply stop.

Someone leaves, and someone else is born.

Someone travels, and someone else waits.

Someone asks a question beneath the moon, and another person asks it again a thousand years later.

The apparent permanence of the moon is balanced by the continuity of human generations.

What endures is not only the moon.

Human feeling also continues: love, memory, departure, homesickness and the need to understand our place within time.

In the MV, Tang dynasty families, children, travellers and returning boatmen pass beneath the same moon.

The young traveller watches them from beside his boat.

He knows that his own sorrow belongs to one life.

But because generation after generation has experienced love, separation and return, his solitude enters a much larger human inheritance.

### “For Whom Does the River Moon Wait?”

> We do not know for whom the river moon waits;  
> we see only the long river sending its waters away.

The moon appears to remain.

The river continues to move.

One suggests waiting.

The other suggests departure.

This contrast marks an essential emotional transition.

The moon holds its position while the water carries everything onward.

For whom does the moon remain?

What does the river take away?

The poem offers no direct answer.

In the MV, a distant riverside tower enters the visual narrative for the first time.

A faint golden window shines across the water.

The traveller looks toward it from his boat.

Whether someone is truly waiting inside has not yet been fully revealed.

The human story begins not with explanation but with a small light in the distance.

Waiting itself becomes visible.

### “A Single White Cloud Drifts Slowly Away”

> A single white cloud drifts slowly away;  
> sorrow overwhelms the shore of Green Maple Reach.

With the departing cloud, the poem turns from cosmic time toward personal separation.

The white cloud carries associations of wandering, distance and a life without a fixed place.

Qingfengpu—Green Maple Reach—becomes a shore of departure, mooring and backward glances.

The river remains vast.

The moon remains bright.

But the person who looks at them is no longer only a philosopher contemplating the cosmos.

He is also someone who has not returned home.

In the MV, the traveller moors his boat beneath blue-green maple shadows.

Moonlight passes through the branches, while a small golden lantern glows within the mist.

There is no exaggerated grief.

His sorrow remains within his posture, his stillness and the length of his gaze.

The sadness of **A Moonlit Night on the Spring River** does not arrive as a sudden dramatic outburst.

It resembles the river.

Quiet, continuous and impossible to stop.

### “Whose Traveller Rides a Solitary Boat Tonight?”

> Whose traveller rides a solitary boat tonight?  
> Where does someone yearn within a moonlit tower?

These two lines establish the poem’s clearest human structure.

Upon the river is a traveller in a small boat.

Within a high tower is someone waiting beneath the moon.

Zhang Ruoxu does not give them names or explain the exact cause of their separation.

This absence of biography makes them universal.

Every person who has travelled far from home may become the figure in the boat.

Every person who has waited through the night may stand within the moonlit tower.

The MV presents the male traveller as a refined Tang dynasty Jiangnan man between approximately twenty-eight and thirty-five, dressed in white and pale-cyan robes.

The female lead is an exceptionally beautiful yet restrained Tang dynasty woman dressed in moon-white and pale-lotus garments, waiting beside the window and corridor of a riverside tower.

A single boat and a single tower stand upon opposite sides of the water.

They share one moon.

Yet they remain within spaces that cannot fully meet.

### “The Moon Lingers Above the Tower”

> How tenderly the moon lingers above the tower;  
> surely it shines upon the separated woman’s dressing mirror.

The moon appears to wander and hesitate.

“Lingering” is ordinarily a human action.

Here, Zhang Ruoxu gives it to moonlight.

It is as though the moon itself understands the unease of separation and cannot immediately depart.

In the MV, the woman first stands upon the outer corridor of the Jianglou, looking across the river.

She then enters her chamber and sits before a Tang dynasty bronze mirror.

Moonlight touches the rim of the mirror and the side of her face.

The mirror should be an object for arranging one’s appearance.

Instead, it reminds her that the person she wishes to see is still absent.

Outside lies the vast moonlit river.

Inside lies the silence of one person.

Moonlight passes between them, joining landscape and emotion.

---

### “It Cannot Be Rolled Away from the Curtain”

> Through the jade chamber’s curtain it cannot be rolled away;  
> brushed from the washing stone, it returns again.

The woman attempts to move the curtain, yet moonlight remains.

She brushes it from cloth upon the washing stone, but it returns.

Moonlight becomes almost physical.

It can be touched, moved and resisted, yet never removed.

The true thing that cannot be dismissed, however, is not the light.

It is the person remembered through that light.

The washing of clothes in classical Chinese poetry is often associated with preparing garments for someone far away.

As the woman touches the fabric, she may be thinking of the traveller still moving upon the distant river.

The MV uses gauze curtains, wooden lattice windows, white cloth, a stone block and silver-blue light to make this passage tactile.

She turns, closes, lifts and brushes.

The moonlight remains.

Longing does not end simply because a person turns away, closes a window or occupies the hands with work.

It changes location and returns.

### “We Gaze but Cannot Hear One Another”

> At this moment we gaze beneath the same moon but cannot hear each other;  
> I wish to follow its radiance and flow to shine upon you.

They may be looking at the moon at exactly the same moment.

Each may believe that the other is somewhere beneath the same light.

Yet no voice crosses the river.

No letter arrives.

No road can immediately remove the distance.

Only moonlight touches the boat and the tower together.

The wish to “follow the moon’s radiance” does not demand a return and does not accuse fate.

It is an exceptionally gentle desire:

If I cannot reach you, may my heart travel with the moonlight and shine where you are.

At this point, the arrangement reaches its most luminous emotional ascent.

Rhodes piano, nylon-string guitar, neo-soul bass, restrained drums, guzheng harmonics and cinematic strings gradually widen the musical space.

The male vocal rises without becoming theatrical.

The song does not replace tenderness with shouting.

The deepest longing often requires no excessive force.

### “Wild Geese Fly Far, Yet Cannot Cross the Light”

> Wild geese fly far, yet cannot cross the moonlight;  
> fish and dragons leap below, tracing patterns upon the water.

In classical Chinese literature, wild geese often carry letters and news across distance.

Here, even they cannot cross the endless field of moonlight.

Fish and dragons move beneath the river but leave only temporary patterns upon its surface.

The creatures of sky and water should offer the possibility of communication.

Instead, both fail.

Words that cannot arrive become flying shadows above and vanishing ripples below.

The MV first shows geese crossing the moonlit sky while the traveller watches from his boat.

It then moves toward the water, where hidden fish and currents produce unwritten patterns.

No literal message appears.

This is a letter that has not been completed and cannot be delivered.

The instrumental bridge continues the feeling through three images: the traveller’s hand resting upon the boat, the woman standing alone within the open corridor, and an elevated view of the silver path connecting the boat and the tower.

### “Last Night I Dreamed of Falling Flowers”

> Last night beside the quiet pool, I dreamed of falling flowers;  
> how sad that half of spring has passed and I have not returned home.

The traveller dreams of falling blossoms.

When he wakes, half of spring has already passed.

The flowers are both landscape and evidence of time.

Perhaps spring had only begun when he left.

Now the petals are falling, yet his return remains distant.

The emotional force of “half of spring has passed and I have not returned home” comes from its simplicity.

It does not require an obscure allusion.

It speaks directly:

Time has passed, and I am still away.

This is understandable to every traveller in every age.

In the MV, the traveller sleeps briefly beside a boat covered in drifting petals.

Dream and reality overlap through mist, moonlight and falling flowers rather than through excessive visual effects.

When he wakes, he holds a small object from home.

The distant Jianglou still carries a faint light.

Spring has not waited.

The river has not stopped.

### “The River Carries Spring Away”

> The river carries spring away until it is almost gone;  
> over the river pool, the setting moon slopes west.

The moon that rose with the tide at the beginning is now descending.

A full night has passed.

The river carries away petals, spring light, waiting and an unrealised return.

The MV gradually shifts from deep silver-blue toward the grey-blue of approaching dawn.

Flowering woods become thinner.

Fewer petals remain upon the water.

Distant lamps begin to fade.

The traveller stands at the stern and looks back toward the tower.

The woman looks toward the river one final time before daylight.

They still do not meet.

Yet the night itself has completed an extended, silent act of mutual gazing.

---

### “From Jieshi to Xiaoxiang, the Road Is Endless”

> The slanting moon sinks deep and hides within sea mist;  
> from Jieshi to Xiaoxiang, the road is endless.

The moon disappears into heavy mist.

The geographical distance between northern Jieshi and southern Xiaoxiang stretches across the imagined world of the poem.

Yet the poem describes more than distance upon a map.

Every traveller unable to return to a loved one possesses a personal Jieshi and Xiaoxiang.

Some distances are created by mountains and rivers.

Others are created by time, responsibility, fate or reasons that cannot be spoken.

In the MV, the small boat enters increasingly dense dawn mist.

Architecture and flowering banks recede until only mountains, water and one lantern remain.

The mist is not horror or disaster.

It represents the unknown road and the tomorrow that has not yet been reached.

---

### “Who Knows How Many Return by Moonlight?”

> Who knows how many return home by moonlight?  
> The falling moon stirs longing through all the riverside trees.

Other boats begin to return through the fading night.

Warm lamps approach the shore.

Houses and pavilions emerge from mist.

Some travellers arrive.

Others remain upon the water.

The poem never tells us whether its solitary traveller reaches the moonlit tower.

It leaves the answer among the falling moon, the river trees and the movement of emotion through the landscape.

“The falling moon stirs longing through all the riverside trees” is more than a description of branches moving in the wind.

Moonlight, river air and human separation seem to enter every tree.

One person’s longing expands until it fills the entire riverbank.

In the final images of the MV, the woman remains beside the window while the traveller’s boat slowly leaves.

Night becomes dawn.

The characters disappear, leaving only flowering trees, river mist, a distant boat and a moon almost erased by morning light.

The story does not end with reunion.

It ends with a wish that still remains.

### Ethereal Mandopop R&B Without Rewriting the Tang Poem

**A Moonlit Night on the Spring River** is long, structurally complex and constantly moving between landscape, philosophy and human longing.

This musical setting does not translate the poem into modern lyrics and does not add a newly written chorus.

It preserves the complete original text and sequence, using melody, rhythm, silence, instrumental space and an extended bridge to create a contemporary song structure.

The production is built around approximately 70 BPM and combines:

* **Warm Rhodes piano**, creating a gentle contemporary harmonic field.
* **Nylon-string guitar**, adding intimate detail and refined Mandopop texture.
* **Deep neo-soul bass**, carrying emotion with the continuous movement of a river.
* **Soft brushed hip-hop drums**, creating groove without breaking the breath of the poem.
* **Airy guzheng harmonics**, answering moonlight, water, flowers and ripples.
* **Delicate pipa**, adding definition to transitions and emotional movement.
* **Distant xiao flute**, suggesting river sky, night mist, solitude and space.
* **Cinematic strings**, gradually opening during “I wish to follow the moon’s radiance” and the closing journey.
* **Subtle vinyl warmth**, adding memory, time and a sense of looking backward across centuries.
* **A clear and emotionally restrained Mandarin male vocal**, preserving classical diction without operatic tone or theatrical shouting.

The song begins with moonlit river ambience and sparse free-time singing, gradually enters a smooth R&B groove and opens into a luminous yet controlled emotional refrain.

The production does not pursue oversized historical spectacle.

Its purpose is to preserve the clarity, spaciousness, philosophical depth and emotional restraint of Zhang Ruoxu’s language while bringing it into the refined sound world of contemporary Taiwan–Hong Kong Mandopop.

### The MV: One Spring River Night, Two People Who Cannot Hear Each Other

The MV remains entirely within a Tang dynasty Jiangnan spring night, imagined around the great river landscapes of Yangzhou and Jiangdu.

Its visual world includes:

spring tide, flowering woods, fragrant meadows, white sand islets, Qingfengpu, a solitary boat, Tang dynasty riverside towers, a moonlit bronze mirror, wooden lattice windows, waterside corridors, distant bridges, mist and the approach of dawn.

Silver-blue moonlight and pale-gold lamps form the principal palette.

The story gradually moves from night toward morning, combining classical Eastern aesthetics with the emotional immediacy of a contemporary cinematic music video.

The young traveller and the woman in the Jianglou are the principal human figures.

He is slender, refined and dressed in white and pale-cyan Tang robes, with an inward and emotionally controlled presence.

She is exceptionally beautiful without becoming ostentatious, wearing moon-white and pale-lotus Tang clothing, with restrained makeup and elegant hair ornaments.

They rarely share the same close space.

Their relationship is created through moonlight, water, windows, lanterns and long-distance gazes.

The MV is structured through forty-five principal images:

* Three prelude images establish the spring river, moon, flowering landscape and traveller.
* Thirty-six images correspond line by line with the complete poem.
* Three instrumental-bridge images continue the emotion after “I wish to follow the moon’s radiance and shine upon you.”
* Three closing images allow the characters to disappear as moonlit night becomes dawn.

The central visual idea is not a direct romantic reunion.

It is to show how one moon illuminates two separate spaces.

How one river both connects and divides two people.

And how a spring night can move from a question about the universe into the quietest form of human longing.

### A Poem About the Moon—and Everyone Who Has Ever Gazed Without Being Heard

**A Moonlit Night on the Spring River** has endured not merely because it describes an exceptionally beautiful night.

Its deeper achievement lies in bringing nature, time, human life and separation into one complete structure.

The spring river is time in motion.

The moon is recurring permanence.

The flowering woods are the brief radiance of life.

The white cloud is wandering.

Qingfengpu is departure.

The solitary boat is the person far from home.

The moonlit tower is the person who waits.

Moonlight that cannot be rolled away is longing that cannot be dismissed.

Wild geese unable to cross the light are letters that cannot arrive.

Falling flowers within a dream are spring and life moving toward disappearance.

The final trees filled with trembling emotion allow one private sorrow to spread across the entire landscape.

Zhang Ruoxu never tells us whether the traveller eventually returns.

He leaves something more enduring than an answer.

As long as someone continues to travel, someone continues to wait, and one person looks toward the moon while thinking of another, **A Moonlit Night on the Spring River** remains a poem of the present.
