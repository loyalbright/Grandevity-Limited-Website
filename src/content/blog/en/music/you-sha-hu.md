An illness.

A physician who could not hear, yet could understand another person with extraordinary clarity.

A journey taken after recovery.

And a stream flowing in an unexpected direction:

west.

From these modest events, Su Shi arrived at one of the most life-affirming passages in classical Chinese literature:

> 誰道人生無再少？
> 君看流水尚能西！
> 休將白髮唱黃雞。
>
> Who says that youth can never return?
> Look, the flowing water can still run west!
> Do not sing of white hair and the yellow cock.

**You Shahu**, or **A Visit to Shahu**, is a short work.

It does not possess the vast scale of the *Red Cliff Rhapsody*, nor the monumental force of Su Shi’s famous Red Cliff lyric.

Its beginning is almost deliberately ordinary.

Su Shi buys farmland at Shahu, some thirty li southeast of Huangzhou.

He goes to inspect the land.

He becomes ill.

He hears of a physician from Maqiao, Pang Anchang, who is skilled in medicine although deaf.

So he goes to seek treatment.

What begins like a small entry from daily life gradually opens into one of Su Shi’s most memorable reflections on age, adversity and renewal.

Grandevity Studio’s **A Visit to Shahu** uses Su Shi’s original writing as 100% of the sung text.

There is no modern lyrical expansion.

No vernacular rewriting.

No newly invented lines placed into Su Shi’s voice.

No newly written modern chorus.

Selected lines from the original *Huanxisha* lyric are repeated to create the finished musical structure.

But every sung word still comes from Su Shi.

The work begins:

> 黃州東南三十里為沙湖，亦曰螺師店。

and eventually returns to ordinary life with:

> 是日，劇飲而歸。

The purpose is not to explain life on Su Shi’s behalf.

It is to allow his own language to become song again.

### A Small Illness Opens into a Larger Question

The story begins:

> 黃州東南三十里為沙湖，亦曰螺師店。
> 予買田其間，因往相田得疾。

Thirty li southeast of Huangzhou lies Shahu, also called Luoshidian.

Su Shi has bought farmland there.

He goes to inspect it.

He becomes ill.

There is no grand literary entrance.

No mountain summit.

No philosophical declaration.

Only an ordinary human problem:

he is sick.

So he looks for a doctor.

That doctor becomes one of the most memorable people in this short work.

---

### Pang Anchang: Unable to Hear, Yet “Surpassing Others in Understanding”

Su Shi writes:

> 聞麻橋人龐安常善醫而聾，遂往求療。

Pang Anchang, the courtesy name of Pang Anshi, was a noted Northern Song physician.

He was deaf.

But Su Shi immediately adds:

> 安常雖聾，而穎悟絕人。

Although Anchang was deaf, his understanding and perception surpassed ordinary people.

The method of communication was simple:

> 以紙畫字，書不數字，輒深了人意。

Characters were written on paper.

Only a few were needed.

Pang could grasp another person’s meaning deeply.

Su Shi responds with humour:

> 余以手為口，君以眼為耳，皆一時異人也。
>
> I use my hand as my mouth, and you use your eyes as your ears. We are both extraordinary men of our time.

There is no pity in the exchange.

Su Shi does not turn Pang into a tragic figure.

Nor does he turn himself into one.

Instead, two unconventional men recognise something interesting in each other.

---

### Why Pang Anchang Matters

If the prose section is removed and only the *Huanxisha* lyric remains, **A Visit to Shahu** can easily look like a general poem of landscape and optimism.

But the prose changes its meaning.

This is first a story about recovery from illness.

It is also a story about companionship.

Pang is deaf.

Su Shi is living through political exile.

Neither man fits a simple image of worldly success.

Yet Su Shi calls them:

> 皆一時異人也。
>
> Extraordinary men of their time.

Then the illness is cured.

And rather than remaining indoors discussing fate, the two men go out together.

---

### “After I Recovered, I Went with Him to Qingquan Temple”

> 疾愈，與之同遊清泉寺。

The sentence is simple.

Within the work, however, it functions almost like a doorway.

Before it:

illness.

After it:

landscape.

Qingquan Temple stands outside the city gate of Qishui.

Nearby is the spring traditionally associated with Wang Yishao, Wang Xizhi:

> 有王逸少洗筆泉。

Its water is:

> 水極甘。

Exceptionally sweet.

But the decisive detail lies below:

> 下臨蘭溪，溪水西流。

Below lies Lanxi Stream.

And the stream flows west.

---

### Why Does Westward Water Matter?

Flowing water repeatedly serves as an image of time in classical Chinese literature.

Water moves onward.

Youth disappears.

White hair comes.

Human life seems to travel in only one direction.

Yet the stream in front of Su Shi is doing something unexpected.

It flows west.

The fact is geographical.

The response is spiritual.

If water does not always move in the direction one expects,

must the human spirit accept a single predetermined direction toward decline?

Su Shi writes:

> 余作歌云：

and the prose opens into the *Huanxisha* lyric.

---

### Young Orchid Shoots, a Clean Sandy Path and Evening Rain

> 山下蘭芽短浸溪，
> 松間沙路淨無泥，
> 蕭蕭暮雨子規啼。

Young orchid shoots dip into the stream.

A sandy path beneath the pines is clean and free of mud.

Evening rain falls.

A cuckoo calls.

The scenery is not monumental.

There are no immense peaks.

No great river rushing toward the sea.

Su Shi notices small things.

New growth.

Water.

Sand.

Rain.

Birdsong.

For someone who has just recovered from illness, these small things are enough to say:

life is still happening.

---

### “Who Says That Youth Can Never Return?”

Then Su Shi asks:

> 誰道人生無再少？

Who says youth can never return?

The line does not require literal rejuvenation.

Its deeper force is psychological and spiritual.

After adversity, can a person begin again?

After illness, can one feel the world freshly again?

After years have already passed, can vitality, curiosity and openness return?

Su Shi does not answer by constructing an abstract theory.

He points toward what is directly in front of him.

---

### “Look, the Flowing Water Can Still Run West!”

> 君看流水尚能西！

The words **君看**, “look,” matter.

This is not merely an internal monologue.

Su Shi asks someone beside him to look as well.

Look at the stream.

Water itself can run west.

Why, then, must human life be understood only as a one-directional movement toward decline?

This does not deny age.

It does not deny pain.

Su Shi is living through years of demotion.

On this particular day, he has only just recovered from illness.

The landscape does not solve his circumstances.

It changes the angle from which he sees them.

---

### “Do Not Sing of White Hair and the Yellow Cock”

The lyric ends:

> 休將白髮唱黃雞。

White hair signals age.

The yellow cock belongs to a cultural tradition of lamenting passing time.

Su Shi says:

stop.

Do not see white hair and conclude that all remaining life has already been defined by decline.

The sentence has weight precisely because its speaker already knows that life can be difficult.

That is why:

> 君看流水尚能西！

still sounds alive nearly a millennium later.

---

### Music: From Everyday Prose to a Building Epic Chorus

The production is built around:

**Cinematic Folk / Traditional Chinese Instruments / Storytelling / Musical Theater Style / Building Epic Chorus / Expressive Clear Male Vocal**

The first half remains primarily narrative.

Buying land.

Falling ill.

Seeking treatment.

Writing on paper with Pang Anchang.

The arrangement deliberately avoids manufacturing an early climax.

The real expansion begins with:

> 疾愈，與之同遊清泉寺。

From there the soundstage opens.

Spring water.

Lanxi.

Pines.

Evening rain.

Traditional Chinese instrumental colours and cinematic space gradually increase.

With:

> 山下蘭芽短浸溪

the melodic line becomes broader.

Then:

> 誰道人生無再少？
> 君看流水尚能西！
> 休將白髮唱黃雞。

forms the emotional centre of the song.

The repeated original lines do not introduce new language.

Their dramatic function changes through repetition.

The first statement is recognition.

The later repetition becomes conviction.

Then the arrangement withdraws again.

Because Su Shi ends with the wonderfully ordinary:

> 是日，劇飲而歸。

After speaking about life:

drink.

Then go home.

### Why Does a Westward-Flowing Stream Still Matter?

**A Visit to Shahu** does not attempt to prove that life is always beautiful.

It preserves something more useful:

the ability to look again.

Two people can stand before the same stream.

One sees water.

Su Shi sees the possibility that life may not have to move only in the direction others have assigned to it.

Resilience does not mean never being wounded.

It means that after illness, disappointment, exile or change, one can remain interested in the world.

Still willing to meet people.

Still willing to walk a mountain road.

Still willing to listen to rain.

Still willing to drink.

Still willing to ask:

> 誰道人生無再少？
>
> Who says that youth can never return?

### About This Project

**A Visit to Shahu** is part of Grandevity Studio’s **Classical Literature Reimagined in Music** series.

We want classical literature to live beyond textbooks, annotations and examinations.

It can become sound again.

A country road outside Huangzhou at dawn.

A sheet of paper on a physician’s wooden table.

Two extraordinary men smiling because they understand one another.

The clear water of a spring beside Qingquan Temple.

Young orchid shoots at the foot of a mountain.

A clean sandy road beneath pine trees.

Soft evening rain.

A cuckoo calling somewhere beyond the trees.

And finally,

a stream flowing in the unexpected direction:

west.

Perhaps classical literature does not need new words in order to cross a thousand years.

Perhaps its original words simply need to be heard again.

> 誰道人生無再少？
> 君看流水尚能西！

Nearly a millennium later,

the water is still flowing.
