> “A hundred worries trouble the heart; ten thousand affairs weary the body.”  
> 百憂感其心，萬事勞其形。

One autumn night, a scholar hears a strange sound coming from the southwest. It begins as a gentle rustle, then surges like waves and the silent advance of an army. His young attendant goes outside to investigate, but finds only a clear sky, stars, moonlight and the sound of wind among the trees.

What Ouyang Xiu hears that night ultimately leads him beyond the season itself, towards the burdens and limits of human life.

Grandevity Music presents *Ode to the Sound of Autumn* (*Qiu Sheng Fu*), a 4-minute-50-second song based on **100% of Ouyang Xiu’s original classical Chinese text**. No modern lyrics have been added, and the prose has not been rewritten. This is a fully sung musical setting, rather than a spoken reading accompanied by music.

## 1. Historical background: an official at the height of his career

Ouyang Xiu (1007–1072), courtesy name Yongshu, was a Northern Song statesman, historian and writer, traditionally counted among the Eight Great Prose Masters of the Tang and Song. Raised by his widowed mother in modest circumstances, he passed the imperial examination in 1030. His defence of Fan Zhongyan and support for the Qingli reforms exposed him to political opposition. After his demotion to Chuzhou in 1045, he wrote the celebrated *Record of the Old Drunkard’s Pavilion* the following year. He later returned to court and worked on the *New Book of Tang*.

In 1057 he presided over the metropolitan examinations, encouraging clearer and more substantive prose. Su Shi, Su Zhe and Zeng Gong were among the successful candidates. In 1058 he became an acting administrator of Kaifeng Prefecture, following Bao Zheng. The post was broadly comparable to an acting mayor of the imperial capital, with wider responsibilities for public order and judicial administration; no modern office is an exact equivalent.

The *Ode* is conventionally dated to 1059, the fourth year of the Jiayou era under Emperor Renzong. Ouyang Xiu was 52 by elapsed years, or 53 by traditional East Asian age reckoning. He had asked to relinquish his demanding capital post on health grounds that spring, but remained a court official and took responsibility for the imperial horse administration, an important component of military logistics. These central responsibilities were not municipal posts. He was also still engaged in historical scholarship.

In 1060 the *New Book of Tang* was completed, and Ouyang Xiu became deputy commissioner of military affairs. In 1061 he was appointed *canzhi zhengshi*, roughly equivalent to a deputy chief minister. He later faced further political disputes, held provincial posts, retired in 1071 and died in 1072. The essay therefore belongs to a moment of personal fatigue within a career approaching its political high point, not to the quiet retirement of an elderly recluse.

## 2. Literary interpretation: from autumn wind to the human heart

A landmark of Song prose-style *fu* (rhapsodic prose), the work begins with vivid auditory imagery. Wind becomes waves, clashing metal and marching soldiers. The attendant’s serene account of the night sky stands in contrast to the scholar’s inward unease.

The middle passage draws on traditional Chinese correspondences between autumn, yin, the Five Phases, punishment, warfare and musical pitch. Shang is associated with the west, and Yize with the seventh lunar month. These are elements of a historical cosmological and rhetorical system, not claims about modern acoustics or meteorology.

The final passage turns from the seasonal decline of vegetation to human anxiety and physical wear. Ouyang Xiu’s reference to once-black hair becoming flecked with white is an image of ageing, not literally of stars. The central question is whether autumn itself deserves the blame for what human beings do to their own minds and bodies. When the boy falls asleep, the sound of insects returns the grand meditation to the intimacy of one quiet room.

## 3. Music: an orchestral journey through sound and silence

The arrangement is a cinematic orchestral Mandopop ballad in flowing 6/8 at 72 BPM. A mature, expressive AI-generated Mandarin baritone moves between intimate phrasing, clipped rhythmic articulation and controlled soaring melodic peaks.

Wind textures, low cello, felt piano, guqin and distant xiao establish the opening atmosphere. Rolling strings and restrained timpani intensify the descriptions of autumn wind; warm strings, French horns, grand piano and cinematic drums carry the late emotional crest. Solo cello and faint insects bring the piece back towards silence. The approximately 17-second introduction, 29-second instrumental transition and 21-second ending give the visual narrative room to breathe.

## 4. Visual design: a Northern Song autumn night

The MV is set in a historically inspired Northern Song residence in 1059. Ouyang Xiu and his young attendant anchor the narrative, with recurring study, courtyard and corridor settings. Warm bronze oil-lamp light contrasts with cool moonlight. Landscape and symbolic inserts evoke sweeping wind, restless water, immense formations of period soldiers, changing vegetation and the fragility of human life.

The army sequences visualise a literary comparison; they do not depict a battle witnessed by Ouyang Xiu that night. Daylight and seasonal passages likewise belong to the symbolic visual narrative. Faces, interiors and landscapes are artistic reconstructions rather than verified records of the actual setting.

## 5. Complete lyrics — original Chinese text

The following is the final sung text in Traditional Chinese. It follows the selected transmitted reading, retaining the textual variants settled during production. The English section labels indicate the arrangement and are not sung lyrics.

[Verse 1]  
歐陽子方夜讀書，  
聞有聲自西南來者，  
悚然而聽之，曰：「異哉！」  
初淅瀝以蕭颯，  
忽奔騰而砰湃；  
如波濤夜驚，  
風雨驟至。  

[Pre-Chorus 1]  
其觸於物也，  
鏦鏦錚錚，金鐵皆鳴；  
又如赴敵之兵，  
銜枚疾走，  
不聞號令，  
但聞人馬之行聲。  

[Verse 2]  
余謂童子：  
「此何聲也？汝出視之。」  
童子曰：  
「星月皎潔，明河在天，  
四無人聲，聲在樹間。」  

[Chorus 1]  
余曰：「噫嘻，悲哉！  
此秋聲也，胡為乎來哉？」  

[Verse 3]  
蓋夫秋之為狀也，  
其色慘淡，煙霏雲斂；  
其容清明，天高日晶；  
其氣慄冽，砭人肌骨；  
其意蕭條，山川寂寥。  

[Pre-Chorus 2]  
故其為聲也，  
淒淒切切，呼號憤發。  
豐草綠縟而爭茂，  
佳木蔥蘢而可悅；  
草拂之而色變，  
木遭之而葉脫；  
其所以摧敗零落者，  
乃其一氣之餘烈。  

[Instrumental Transition]  

[Verse 4]  
夫秋，刑官也，  
於時為陰；  
又兵象也，  
於行為金。  
是謂天地之義氣，  
常以肅殺而為心。  
天之於物，  
春生秋實。  
故其在樂也，  
商聲主西方之音，  
夷則為七月之律。  

[Bridge 1]  
商，傷也，  
物既老而悲傷；  
夷，戮也，  
物過盛而當殺。  

[Bridge 2]  
嗟乎！草木無情，  
有時飄零。  
人為動物，  
惟物之靈。  

[Final Chorus]  
百憂感其心，  
萬事勞其形。  
有動乎中，  
必搖其精。  
而況思其力之所不及，  
憂其智之所不能。  
宜其渥然丹者為槁木，  
黝然黑者為星星。  

[Climax]  
奈何以非金石之質，  
欲與草木而爭榮？  
念誰為之戕賊，  
亦何恨乎秋聲！  

[Outro]  
童子莫對，  
垂頭而睡。  
但聞四壁蟲聲唧唧，  
如助余之歎息。  

[End – Instrumental]  

## 6. English translation of the complete sung text

The following is a new English rendering for readers. It follows the final Chinese text above and is not an additional sung lyric, nor a claim that the song has an English vocal version. The traditional wordplay on Shang and Yi is rendered by meaning.

[Verse 1]  
Master Ouyang was reading at night when he heard a sound coming from the southwest.  
Startled, he listened and exclaimed, “How strange!”  
At first it rustled and whispered; suddenly it surged and roared,  
like waves breaking in the night, like wind and rain arriving without warning.  

[Pre-Chorus 1]  
Where it touched things, they clanged and rang as though all metal sounded at once.  
It was like troops rushing toward the enemy, each holding a wooden gag between his teeth,  
running swiftly without a word of command; only the sound of men and horses moving could be heard.  

[Verse 2]  
I asked the boy attendant, “What is that sound? Go outside and see.”  
He replied, “The stars and moon are bright, and the Milky Way spans the sky.  
There is no human voice anywhere; the sound is among the trees.”  

[Chorus 1]  
I said, “Ah, how sad! It is the sound of autumn. Why has it come?”  

[Verse 3]  
Consider the appearance of autumn: its colours are subdued and desolate, with drifting mist and gathering clouds.  
Its aspect is clear and bright, the sky high and the sun brilliant.  
Its air is piercingly cold, penetrating flesh and bone;  
its mood is bleak, with mountains and rivers silent and solitary.  

[Pre-Chorus 2]  
Thus its sounds are mournful and urgent, rising into fierce cries.  
The luxuriant green grasses vie in their growth, and beautiful trees with dense foliage delight the eye.  
Yet when autumn brushes the grass, its colour changes; when it reaches the trees, their leaves fall.  
What brings about such ruin and scattering is but the lingering force of its vital breath.  

[Verse 4]  
Autumn is the officer of punishment; among the seasons it belongs to yin.  
It is also an emblem of arms; among the Five Phases it belongs to Metal.  
This is the righteous force of Heaven and Earth, whose disposition is to bring things to their austere end.  
Heaven causes things to grow in spring and bear fruit in autumn.  
Thus in music, the Shang tone governs the sound of the west,  
and Yize is the pitch standard associated with the seventh lunar month.  

[Bridge 1]  
Shang suggests sorrow: things grow old and become sorrowful.  
Yi suggests cutting down: when things have passed their prime, they are due to be cut back.  

[Bridge 2]  
Alas! Grasses and trees have no feelings, yet in their season they wither and fall.  
Human beings are living creatures, the most spiritually aware among all things.  

[Final Chorus]  
A hundred worries trouble the heart; ten thousand affairs weary the body.  
Whatever stirs within must shake the vital spirit.  
How much more so when we dwell on what our strength cannot reach,  
and worry over what our wisdom cannot accomplish!  
No wonder the once ruddy complexion becomes like withered wood,  
and once black hair turns flecked with white.  

[Climax]  
Why, when our bodies are not made of metal or stone,  
should we wish to compete with grasses and trees in flourishing?  
Consider what truly injures us: why resent the sound of autumn?  

[Outro]  
The boy made no reply; his head drooped and he fell asleep.  
Only the chirping of insects could be heard around the four walls,  
as though joining in my sigh.
