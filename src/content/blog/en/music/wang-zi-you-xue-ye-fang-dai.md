A night of falling snow. A small boat crossing a moonlit river.

A man travels through the night to visit an old friend. After finally reaching his friend's home, he turns around without entering.

When asked why, he gives an answer that has echoed through Chinese literature for more than a thousand years:

> 吾本乘興而行，興盡而返，何必見戴？

*I set out because I felt inspired. Now that the inspiration has passed, why must I see Dai?*

This is the story of **Wang Ziyou's Snowy Night Visit to Dai**, a celebrated anecdote from *Shishuo Xinyu* (*A New Account of the Tales of the World*), a fifth-century collection associated with Liu Yiqing of China's Liu Song dynasty.

Its few lines capture something remarkably elusive: the freedom to act on a genuine impulse without becoming a prisoner of the outcome.

Grandevity Studio has transformed the original Classical Chinese prose into a new 3-minute, 57-second composition, blending **Elegant Chinese City Pop × Guofeng**.

The lyrics remain entirely within the original text. No modern lines, paraphrased passages or newly written lyrics have been added. Selected original phrases are repeated as choruses and reprises to support the musical structure.

The result is not a story about an unsuccessful visit.

It is a musical reflection on spontaneity, friendship and the freedom to turn back when the original impulse has run its course.

## Liu Yiqing and Shishuo Xinyu: Preserving the Spirit of the Wei-Jin Literati

*Shishuo Xinyu* is one of the most influential collections of character anecdotes in classical Chinese literature.

Compiled under **Liu Yiqing (403–444)** and associated scholars during the Liu Song dynasty of the Southern Dynasties, the work preserves conversations, personal encounters and memorable incidents involving prominent figures from the Eastern Han through the Eastern Jin periods.

Liu Yiqing was a member of the Liu Song imperial family and held the title Prince of Linchuan. Known for his literary interests and association with scholars, he played a major role in assembling a work that became an enduring record of the personalities and cultural ideals of the Chinese elite.

The transmitted collection is divided into thirty-six thematic categories.

Wang Ziyou's story appears in the section titled **Ren Dan (任誕)**, commonly associated with uninhibited conduct, unconventional behaviour and the freedom to follow one's natural disposition.

The story's historical setting belongs to the Eastern Jin dynasty, but the collection itself was compiled later, during the Liu Song.

It is therefore best understood as a literary anecdote preserving the memory and cultural image of historical figures, rather than a contemporary eyewitness account of every detail.

That distinction matters.

The significance of this short narrative lies not only in what may have happened on one particular night, but also in the vision of Wei-Jin intellectual life that the story has preserved for later generations.

## Wang Ziyou and Dai Andao: Two Remarkable Figures of the Eastern Jin

**Wang Ziyou** was the courtesy name of Wang Huizhi, the fifth son of Wang Xizhi, the celebrated master of Chinese calligraphy.

Born into the prominent Wang family of Langya, Wang Huizhi became especially remembered for his independent spirit, love of nature and unconventional behaviour.

Another anecdote in *Shishuo Xinyu* describes his affection for bamboo.

Even when staying only temporarily in an empty residence, he insisted on planting bamboo, declaring:

> 何可一日無此君？

*How could I spend even one day without this gentleman?*

The "gentleman" was the bamboo itself.

This attachment reflects a cultural sensibility in which natural beauty was closely connected with personal character, spiritual companionship and freedom from convention.

The friend he wished to visit was **Dai Andao**, also known as **Dai Kui**.

Dai Kui was a respected Eastern Jin recluse and artist, accomplished in painting, sculpture, music and literary pursuits. He preferred a life of independence and artistic cultivation to the pursuit of official honours.

Wang Ziyou was living in Shanyin, in the area of present-day Shaoxing, Zhejiang Province.

Dai Kui was then in Shan, associated with the area of present-day Shengzhou.

The rivers and waterways of eastern Zhejiang made travelling by small boat a natural part of life in the region.

Against this landscape of mountains, rivers and cultivated retreat, the story unfolds.

One man, stirred by a snowy night and the poetry of reclusion, suddenly wishes to visit a friend whose way of life embodies many of the same ideals.

## "Everything Was Bright and Clear": Awakening to a Snowy Night

The account begins with remarkable simplicity:

> 王子猷居山陰，夜大雪，眠覺，開室，命酌酒，四望皎然。

One night, heavy snow falls.

Wang Ziyou awakens, opens his room, orders wine to be served and looks out into the surrounding landscape.

The original text says:

> 四望皎然。

Everywhere he looks, the world is bright and clear.

Snow has transformed an ordinary winter night into a luminous, almost otherworldly landscape.

The prose does not need an elaborate description.

A few words are enough to establish the sense that the familiar world has suddenly become extraordinary.

The MV develops this image through a moonlit snowy courtyard and silver-white landscape. The moonlit treatment is a cinematic interpretation; the original anecdote does not provide a detailed astronomical account of the night.

Moved by the scene, Wang Ziyou rises and wanders about.

Then he begins reciting a poem.

## Zuo Si's Zhao Yin: Why Poetry Leads to a Friend

> 因起彷徨，詠左思《招隱》詩。忽憶戴安道。

Wang Ziyou wanders about, reciting Zuo Si's *Zhao Yin*, or *Poems Inviting the Recluse*.

The term rendered here as "wanders about" describes his pacing. It need not imply the modern psychological sense of painful uncertainty.

Zuo Si, a poet of the Western Jin, wrote about the appeal of mountain solitude and withdrawal from worldly ambitions.

This is an important literary detail.

As Wang Ziyou contemplates the snowy landscape and recites poetry celebrating reclusion, he suddenly remembers Dai Andao, a friend whose life embodied many of those ideals.

The thought arrives without preparation.

The snowfall evokes wonder.

The poem evokes the beauty of seclusion.

The memory of Dai gives that feeling a human direction.

The words "忽憶" — "suddenly remembered" — change the movement of the entire story.

What began as contemplation becomes action.

He decides to go immediately.

## A Boat Through the Night

> 時戴在剡，即便夜乘小舟就之。

Dai Andao was then staying in Shan.

Wang Ziyou immediately set out by small boat to visit him.

The original anecdote does not describe every detail of the route.

Instead, it gives a wonderfully concise account of the journey's duration:

> 經宿方至。

It took an entire night before he arrived.

The brevity is striking.

Wang Ziyou does not simply cross a courtyard or walk a short distance.

He commits himself to a substantial journey.

The cinematic MV expands this short sentence into a visual progression through snow-covered banks, moonlit water, distant mountains and waterways inspired by eastern Zhejiang.

A young attendant and an older boatman accompany him in the film, helping establish the practical reality of the journey and a consistent visual narrative.

These details serve the adaptation rather than claiming to reconstruct an exact route documented in the ancient text.

The important fact within the literary narrative is simple:

Wang Ziyou genuinely makes the journey.

He travels through the night.

And he arrives.

What follows is entirely unexpected.

## "He Reached the Gate, but Turned Back"

> 造門不前而返。

He reached Dai Andao's residence but did not go forward to meet his friend.

Instead, he turned back.

For most people, such behaviour seems almost inexplicable.

Why spend an entire night travelling only to abandon the intended meeting at the very last moment?

Why not knock on the door?

Why not see the friend whose memory inspired the journey?

The answer comes in one of the most famous lines in Chinese anecdotal literature:

> 吾本乘興而行，興盡而返，何必見戴？

*I set out because I felt inspired. Now that the inspiration has passed, why must I see Dai?*

The beauty of this response lies in its unexpected simplicity.

Wang Ziyou does not appear troubled by the journey or disappointed at its conclusion.

He does not explain himself with elaborate arguments.

He simply recognises that the impulse which brought him there has come to an end.

## Setting Out on a Whim, Returning When It Fades

Wang Ziyou's answer can be understood in three movements.

**Setting out because of inspiration.**

The initial decision arises from a genuine moment of enthusiasm, rather than a calculation of reward or social obligation.

**Returning when the inspiration has passed.**

The effort already invested in a journey does not necessarily make its original objective indispensable.

**Why must I see Dai?**

Meeting his friend was the occasion for the journey, but not necessarily the sole measure of its value.

The story can be read as an expression of the spontaneous, individualistic spirit associated with certain Wei-Jin literati.

It does not necessarily suggest that friendship was unimportant to Wang Ziyou.

Rather, the act of travelling had become an experience valuable in itself, one that did not require a conventional conclusion.

Of course, his decision may strike modern readers as impractical, inconsiderate or even absurd.

That tension is part of what makes the anecdote memorable.

It does not need to be treated as a universal rule for how to honour commitments.

Instead, it invites us to reflect on the relationship between a genuine impulse, the experience of a journey and our attachment to a predetermined outcome.

Must every journey be justified by reaching its planned destination?

Or can the experience itself have meaning?

## 📖 Complete English Translation of the Original Prose

**Wang Ziyou's Snowy Night Visit to Dai**

Wang Ziyou was living in Shanyin.

One night, heavy snow fell. Awakening from sleep, he opened his room and ordered wine to be served. Looking out in every direction, he found the world bright and clear.

He rose and wandered about, reciting Zuo Si's poem *Inviting the Recluse*.

Suddenly, he remembered Dai Andao.

At that time, Dai was staying in Shan. Wang immediately set out by small boat to visit him, travelling through the night.

It took an entire night before he arrived. Yet when he reached the gate, he did not go in. Instead, he turned back.

Someone asked him why.

Wang replied:

> I set out because I felt inspired. Now that the inspiration has passed, why must I see Dai?

*Translator's note: This English translation is provided for literary understanding. The song is performed in the original Classical Chinese, not in English. Musical repetitions of original lines are not repeated in the prose translation.*
