Sometimes the hardest thing is not the distance still left to travel.

It is the voice inside us saying:

**Not yet.**

**You have not arrived.**

**Keep going a little longer.**

Su Shi is walking beneath Songfeng Pavilion in Huizhou when his strength begins to fail.

He looks upward.

The pavilion is still high among the treetops.

Like so many of us, he assumes that rest belongs somewhere ahead, that he must first reach the proper destination.

Then, after a long pause, a thought breaks the entire structure open:

> 此間有甚麼歇不得處！
>
> Why should there be anywhere here where I cannot rest?

The mountain has not changed.

The pavilion has not moved closer.

The road remains exactly where it was.

What changes is the assumption that peace must wait until arrival.

**A Visit to Songfeng Pavilion** is part of Grandevity Studio’s *Classical Literature Reimagined in Music* project.

The song uses **100% of Su Shi’s original text, in its original sequence, without modern lyrical expansion, paraphrase, newly written chorus or added lyrics.**

Nothing has been written on Su Shi’s behalf.

The aim is simpler:

to allow a moment of insight from more than nine centuries ago to enter contemporary melody, rhythm and emotional experience without replacing the words that created it.

### A Very Short Work That Begins with Being Tired

**Ji You Songfeng Ting** is preserved in the first volume of **Dongpo Zhilin**.

The prose begins directly from an ordinary physical experience:

> 余嘗寓居惠州嘉祐寺，
> 縱步松風亭下，
> 足力疲乏，
> 思欲就林止息。

“I once stayed at Jiayou Temple in Huizhou. Walking beneath Songfeng Pavilion, my legs became exhausted, and I thought of stopping to rest among the trees.”

There is no monumental opening landscape.

No philosophical thesis announces itself.

Nothing tells us that a great insight is about to occur.

He is simply tired.

That ordinariness is central to the beauty of the piece.

Deep understanding does not always arrive in a study, a lecture hall or a dramatic crisis.

Sometimes it appears halfway up an ordinary path.

The body grows tired.

The mind wants somewhere to stop.

And suddenly, an assumption that had always seemed natural becomes visible.

### “The Pavilion Was Still at the Treetops”

> 望亭宇尚在木末，
> 意謂是如何得到？

Looking up, Su Shi sees that the pavilion is still high among the treetops and wonders how he will ever reach it.

The pavilion can be seen.

That is precisely the problem.

It is visible enough to become a goal, yet distant enough to feel burdensome.

This moment remains strikingly contemporary.

We live among our own distant pavilions:

When this project is finished, I can rest.

When I reach the next position, I can breathe.

When I earn enough, I can slow down.

When this problem is solved, life can finally begin.

Just a little farther.

A little longer.

Then everything will be all right.

But there is always another pavilion.

When rest is permanently postponed until a future condition has been satisfied, the burden is no longer simply the road.

It is the destination itself.

---

### “Why Can I Not Rest Here?”

The entire piece turns on four quiet words:

> 良久忽曰。
>
> After a long while, he suddenly said:

The pause matters.

This is not an instant slogan.

He remains with the problem.

Then something loosens:

> 此間有甚麼歇不得處！
>
> Why should there be anywhere here where I cannot rest?

Why must the pavilion be reached first?

There are pines here.

Shade here.

Stones here.

Ground beneath the feet here.

The absence of a resting place was never the true problem.

The problem was the condition placed upon rest:

**I may rest only after I arrive.**

Su Shi then gives the change an extraordinarily physical image:

> 由是如掛鉤之魚，忽得解脫。
>
> From that moment, I was like a fish released from a hook, suddenly set free.

The pavilion has not moved.

His legs have not magically recovered.

The hook has come free.

---

### Release Is Not the Same as Giving Up

It is easy to reduce the passage to a message of withdrawal:

If the road is tiring, stop trying.

But that is not what Su Shi writes.

He never declares that destinations are meaningless.

He never says that effort should cease.

The freedom here is subtler:

**One may continue forward without believing that only arrival can justify the journey.**

One may work without postponing every form of peace until success.

One may have a destination without allowing that destination to become a hook in the mind.

“Rest,” therefore, is not surrender.

It is an inner release from unnecessary compulsion.

The road remains.

One may still walk it.

But the road no longer drags the person behind it.

---

### “Even When Battle Lines Meet”

Had Su Shi ended with the fish released from the hook, the piece would already have been complete.

Instead, he pushes the insight into an extreme thought experiment:

> 若人悟此，
> 雖兵陣相接，鼓聲如雷霆，
> 進則死敵，退則死法……

If a person truly understands this, then even when battle lines meet and the drums sound like thunder, advance and one may die by the enemy; retreat and one may die under military law.

The mountain path has vanished.

Now there appears to be no safe direction at all.

That is precisely why the final statement carries such force:

> 當甚麼時也不妨熟歇。
>
> Even then, there need be no obstacle to resting deeply.

The point is not that danger is unreal.

The danger remains.

The pressure remains.

The external situation may offer no immediate solution.

The question is whether the crisis must also occupy every part of the inner world.

Su Shi’s answer is startling:

**Circumstance may surround us without being allowed to possess us completely.**

### Contemplative Mandopop Soul: Making Release Audible

**Ji You Songfeng Ting** was not written as a modern song.

There is no conventional verse-chorus cycle.

There is no repeating lyrical hook.

This setting therefore uses a **through-composed structure**, moving through the original text once and in order.

The musical language centres on **Premium Contemplative Mandopop Soul / Chinese-inspired R&B / Neo-soul / Chinese Fusion**:

* **70 BPM in slow 6/8**, giving the music the breathing motion of a measured mountain walk.
* **Guqin and xiao**, creating the open air of pine forest and distant hillside.
* **Fender Rhodes**, introducing the warmth of contemporary Mandarin Soul harmony.
* **Nylon-string guitar**, keeping the sound close to the human body and physical movement.
* **Deep neo-soul bass and brushed drums**, providing restrained forward motion.
* **Pipa and elegant strings**, widening the emotional space between contemplation and the imagined battlefield.
* **A mature, warm and restrained Mandarin baritone**, preserving clarity of Classical Chinese diction without theatrical or operatic delivery.

At “足力疲乏,” the sound carries weight.

At “望亭宇尚在木末,” the harmony suspends.

Then:

> 此間有甚麼歇不得處！

The arrangement opens.

Not into a triumphant climax.

Into space.

The emotional centre of the song is not victory.

It is the sound of something inside finally letting go.

### The MV: From an Afternoon at Jiayou Temple to the Pine Wind at Dusk

The music video remains entirely within a Northern Song visual world.

There is no modern timeline and no present-day parallel narrative.

The journey begins near Jiayou Temple in Huizhou.

Late-spring to early-summer greenery fills the mountainside: pines, uneven paths, stone slopes and layered distant hills.

Su Shi appears as an older scholar approaching sixty.

He is lean, gentle and dignified, carrying the quiet severity of a life repeatedly shaped by displacement and political reversal.

His moon-white inner garment and muted blue-grey robe remain consistent throughout the film.

At first, Songfeng Pavilion appears far above among the treetops.

The path rises.

His steps become slower.

The body grows tired.

Then he stops.

The central line arrives:

> 此間有甚麼歇不得處！

After that moment, the same forest begins to feel wider.

Nothing supernatural has happened.

The landscape has not changed its nature.

The change belongs to perception.

For “雖兵陣相接,” the MV briefly introduces an imagined Northern Song battlefield, with drums, banners, armour and compressed formations, as an internal visual metaphor rather than a literal change of setting.

Then the battle disappears.

Pines return.

The path returns.

Dusk approaches.

The pavilion remains where it has always been.

It simply no longer has power over the man below it.

### Perhaps Freedom Is Not the Same as Finally Arriving

We are accustomed to placing peace in the future.

After the work is finished.

After life becomes stable.

After the problem is solved.

After we become the person we intended to become.

Then, we tell ourselves, we may finally rest.

**A Visit to Songfeng Pavilion** proposes something more difficult.

Some roads remain worth walking.

Some mountains remain worth climbing.

Some pavilions remain worth reaching.

But breathing does not have to wait until arrival.

Su Shi does not make the pavilion disappear.

He releases the hook.

More than nine centuries later, the question remains:

> **此間有甚麼歇不得處？**
>
> **Why should there be anywhere here where I cannot rest?**

### About This Project

**A Visit to Songfeng Pavilion** is part of Grandevity Studio’s **Classical Literature Reimagined in Music** project.

The aim is to bring Classical Chinese literature beyond textbooks, annotations, translation, recitation and memorisation, allowing the original words themselves to enter contemporary melody, sound and visual storytelling.

Some texts do not need to be rewritten.

They may simply need another way to be heard.

More than nine centuries ago, an exiled scholar grew tired on a mountain path in Huizhou.

He did not write a story about conquering the mountain.

He asked instead:

> 此間有甚麼歇不得處？

We still build our lives around one “after I arrive” after another.

Perhaps the freedom preserved in this very short piece is more difficult than achievement:

**The road may continue.**

**The mind does not have to be dragged by the destination.**
