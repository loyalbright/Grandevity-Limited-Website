Some feelings cannot explain when they began.

They may arise in a dream, deepen through a single gaze, and continue through illness, death, the unseen world and the laws of society without coming to an end.

> Love arises from no known beginning, yet grows ever deeper.
> The living may die for it, and the dead may return to life.

**Mudan Ting Ji Tici — Preface to The Peony Pavilion** is a Mandarin female-vocal work composed entirely from Tang Xianzu’s original Ming-dynasty text.

The song contains no expanded passages, modern lyrics, rewritten lines or newly composed chorus.

Every sung word comes from Tang Xianzu’s original preface, performed once and in its original sequence—from “Among all women under heaven who possess feeling” to the final question: “How can one know what feeling may yet bring into being?”

The preface stands before the story of **The Peony Pavilion**, but it is far more than a conventional introduction.

It is Tang Xianzu’s defence of Du Liniang.

It is also his declaration of a philosophy of *qing*: feeling, love, emotional truth and the power of inward experience.

Du Liniang dreams of a man and awakens into illness.

The illness does not pass.

She paints her own likeness by hand, leaves the portrait within the human world and dies.

Three years later, she is still able to seek the person she dreamed of in the dark and unknowable realm beyond ordinary life—and, through love, return.

For Tang Xianzu, this is not merely a supernatural plot.

It raises a more fundamental question:

**When reason, ritual and ordinary experience all declare something impossible, may feeling still open another path?**

### “Among All Women Under Heaven, Could There Be Another Like Du Liniang?”

The preface begins with an astonished rhetorical question:

> Among all women under heaven who possess feeling, could there be another like Du Liniang?

Tang Xianzu does not begin with historical setting or family background.

He begins with feeling.

All women may possess emotion and desire, he suggests—but has anyone carried feeling to the depth reached by Du Liniang?

The question transforms her from the heroine of a supernatural romance into an emblem of *zhiqing*: feeling at its utmost.

She is not simply a sheltered young woman waiting for fate.

She dreams.

She loves.

She falls ill because the waking world offers no place in which to realise what the dream has awakened.

She paints her own image and entrusts her existence to a meeting that has not yet occurred.

The MV therefore places Du Liniang at the centre of its visual world.

The instrumental opening begins with the peony garden, a lattice window, a round fan, white silk and her reflection in the pond. She stands within the late-spring dusk of a Ming-dynasty Jiangnan garden, not yet knowing the identity of the person in her dream, but already sensing that destiny is approaching.

### “She Dreamed of the Man and Immediately Fell Ill”

> She dreamed of the man and immediately fell ill.
> The illness lingered,
> until she painted her own likeness by hand, left it to the world, and then died.

A dream is without physical form.

Once the dreamer wakes, it should disappear.

Du Liniang’s dream does not remain within the night.

It enters her body as illness.

It enters time as unending longing.

Finally, it enters her brush and becomes a self-portrait left within the human world.

The act of painting her own form is one of the preface’s most important gestures.

Du Liniang knows that her life is approaching its end, yet she refuses to vanish completely.

Through white silk, ink and brushwork, she preserves both her appearance and the feeling that has not yet found an answer in waking life.

The MV establishes three recurring objects as emblems of her fate:

the pale-pink round fan,

the white-silk self-portrait,

and the white-jade peony hairpin.

The fan belongs to her life before death.

The portrait allows her to be recognised afterwards.

The peony hairpin connects her to late spring, youth, flowering, disappearance and return.

### “Love Arises From No Known Beginning”

> Love arises from no known beginning,
> yet, once set in motion, grows ever deeper.

These words have endured because they acknowledge the part of feeling that resists explanation.

We may analyse how a relationship developed.

We may identify the time and place of a meeting.

But feeling often begins before the person experiencing it knows that it has begun.

“To know no beginning” does not make love careless.

It recognises that inward life does not always follow the visible order of cause and effect.

“To grow ever deeper” is not a passing impulse.

Once feeling finds its direction, it continues until it changes how a person understands the body, the world, life and death.

The music begins to open here.

Guqin and xiao flute preserve the atmosphere of moonlit space, while Fender Rhodes harmony, neo-soul bass, brushed drums and soft strings enter gradually.

The phrase does not arrive as an abrupt pop climax.

It blooms layer by layer, like peonies opening beneath the moon.

---

### “The Living May Die; the Dead May Return to Life”

> The living may die for it.
> The dead may return to life.

These words do not simply romanticise death.

They propose that feeling at its utmost can transform the perceived boundary of life itself.

Du Liniang does not die because she lacks strength.

Her death reveals that the waking world has no place capable of containing what the dream awakened.

Her return is not only a miraculous event.

It means that the feeling dismissed as impossible, unreal and contrary to ordinary reason ultimately acquires the power to enter reality.

These lines form the song’s single full emotional summit.

The vocal does not rely upon shouting, operatic projection or theatrical excess.

A warm Mandarin mezzo-soprano rises from inward restraint toward quiet radiance.

Pipa, bass, percussion and cinematic strings open together, then quickly withdraw, leaving a sacred stillness after the recognition that the dead may return.

---

### “Why Must Feeling Born in a Dream Be Unreal?”

> Feeling born in a dream—why must it be unreal?
> Are there so few people in the world who appear in dreams?

Ordinary thought often treats dreams as the opposite of truth.

Tang Xianzu asks why.

If a dream changes the body, decisions and destiny of the person who awakens, can it still be dismissed as nothing more than illusion?

**The Peony Pavilion** does not require us to believe that every dream will become literal reality.

Its deeper challenge is directed at the assumption that only events occurring within public, observable life may be considered true.

The encounter in Du Liniang’s dream has no official recognition.

No social witness.

No formal promise.

At first, not even a clearly known name.

Yet her feeling is real.

The illness, death, waiting and search that follow are also real.

The MV allows the waking chamber and dream garden to overlap.

Bed curtains, lattice windows, a moon gate, peonies, water and Liu Mengmei’s distant figure coexist, making it increasingly difficult to decide where the dream ends and reality begins.

### “These Are Merely Arguments of the Physical Form”

> To insist that intimacy must depend upon the pillow and formal union,
> or wait upon outward position before closeness may exist—
> these are merely arguments confined to the physical form.

Tang Xianzu challenges perspectives that recognise only external procedure, social status and bodily form while denying inward emotional truth.

If feeling is permitted to exist only after ritual, status and visible relationship have approved it, then what has been recognised is merely the external form.

True feeling does not cease to exist because it lacks conventional proof.

Nor does it become unreal because it arises in a dream, in the unseen world or within a work of literature.

At this point, the MV gradually leaves the romantic garden and enters Tang Xianzu’s study.

An official cap, historical books, manuscripts, brush, inkstone and candlelight bring feeling and ritual, inward truth and outward form, dream and social judgement into the same philosophical space.

---

### “I Altered It Slightly and Developed It Into a Play”

> Those who transmitted the story of Governor Du described events resembling earlier accounts.
> I altered them slightly and developed them into a play.

Tang Xianzu does not claim that every narrative element appeared without precedent.

He identifies earlier stories and historical traditions that resemble the material surrounding Governor Du, the scholar Liu and the interrogation scene.

Yet the importance of **The Peony Pavilion** lies in more than its sources.

The decisive phrase is:

> I altered it slightly and developed it.

Tang Xianzu reorganised fragments of transmitted narrative into new characters, dramatic movement and a coherent philosophy of feeling.

Du Liniang became more than a woman who returned from death.

She became one of the most recognisable embodiments of emotional truth in Chinese literature.

The MV makes Tang Xianzu visible during this passage.

He reads historical volumes in a candlelit study.

He stops, reflects and lowers his brush toward the manuscript.

Beyond the window, Du Liniang and Liu Mengmei gradually take form among peonies and moonlight.

The author is not observing a story that already exists.

It is coming into being upon his desk.

---

### “The Affairs of the Human World Cannot Be Exhausted by the Human World”

> Alas, the affairs of the human world
> cannot be exhausted by the human world itself.

Here, the preface moves from narrative source toward philosophical conclusion.

Tang Xianzu does not argue that human life possesses no order.

He argues that human knowledge, social convention and rational judgement cannot exhaust every possibility within human existence.

We judge the unknown through what is already known.

We reject the rare through the evidence of the common.

We dismiss what cannot yet be demonstrated.

Yet dream, memory, creativity and inward experience have never been fully contained by external measurement.

Du Liniang’s story does not ask reason to disappear.

It asks reason to acknowledge its limits.

---

### “How Can One Know What Feeling May Yet Bring Into Being?”

> Unless one possesses penetrating understanding,
> one constantly blocks such things with reason.
>
> People speak only of what reason declares impossible—
> but how can they know what feeling may yet bring into being?

The word *ge* here carries the force of obstruction and rejection.

A mind without openness uses “reason” to prevent anything beyond ordinary experience from being considered.

It says:

According to established logic, this cannot exist.

Tang Xianzu reverses the judgement.

You know what reason excludes.

But how do you know what feeling may create?

This is not a rejection of reason itself.

It is a challenge to reason when it becomes closed, absolute and unwilling to recognise its own boundary.

A person of deeper understanding does not immediately declare the unfamiliar impossible.

Such a person leaves room for feeling, dream, imagination, art and the parts of human experience that have not yet been explained.

The song does not return to a conventional chorus.

It does not repeat “Love arises from no known beginning.”

Instead, it passes through historical allusion and the author’s own voice before opening again at the final question.

The harmony remains unresolved.

The question stays open.

Feeling remains.

### Dreamlike Cinematic Mandopop Soul Without Rewriting the Original Text

**Preface to The Peony Pavilion** was not written according to the structure of a modern song.

It contains no recurring chorus and does not rely upon repeated lines.

The prose moves from Du Liniang’s story through the declaration of ultimate feeling, the relation between dream and truth, the sources of the drama and Tang Xianzu’s final confrontation between feeling and reason.

This musical setting does not translate the text into modern lyrics, add a newly written refrain or repeat any original sentence.

It preserves the complete text and sequence, using melody, silence, instrumental interludes and dynamic development to create a contemporary song form.

The production is built around approximately 68 BPM and a slow, flowing 6/8 pulse, combining:

* **Guqin harmonics**, establishing the cool texture of dream, manuscript and inward thought.
* **Xiao flute**, extending moonlit gardens, mist and distant space.
* **Restrained pipa**, adding delicate definition at grammatical and emotional turns.
* **Fender Rhodes piano**, bringing warm contemporary harmony.
* **Deep neo-soul bass**, supporting the continuous deepening of feeling.
* **Soft brushed drums**, maintaining the 6/8 movement without disturbing the breath of the classical prose.
* **Cinematic strings**, opening at the work’s single climax: “The living may die; the dead may return to life.”
* **Distant wordless harmonies**, suggesting dream, the unseen world and rebirth.
* **A mature, luminous and restrained Mandarin mezzo-soprano**, preserving clear classical diction without operatic tone, Kunqu-style singing, theatrical vibrato, shouting or anthem-like production.

The opening is mysterious and delicate.

The arrangement blooms at “Love arises from no known beginning.”

It reaches its single summit at “The living may die; the dead may return to life.”

The historical allusions withdraw into sparse chamber texture.

At the final question, the music opens once more and continues into a long instrumental resonance, leaving an answer that reason has not closed.

### The MV: From the Peony Garden Into Dream, Death, Writing and Dawn

The MV is set within a late-Ming Jiangnan visual world during late spring.

Its principal spaces and objects include:

a peony garden, white walls and dark roof tiles, a moon gate, covered corridors, pond water, Taihu rocks, a lattice-window chamber, translucent bed curtains, a writing desk, white silk, a round fan, the self-portrait, historical books, brushes, inkstone and Tang Xianzu’s study.

Moon white, pale lotus pink, light cyan, smoky violet, bamboo green, water blue and restrained candle gold form the principal palette.

Du Liniang is the central visual figure.

She is portrayed as an eighteen-year-old Jiangnan gentlewoman wearing a white-jade peony hairpin, moon-white and pale-pink Ming robes, a translucent light-cyan outer layer and a pale-pink round fan.

Liu Mengmei first appears as an indistinct figure within the dream and becomes gradually clearer.

Tang Xianzu enters during the later movement as author, adapter and philosophical observer.

The MV unfolds through seventy-four principal images.

The opening begins with the peony garden and Du Liniang’s solitary profile.

The main movement follows dream, illness, self-portrait, death and rebirth.

In the middle, Du Liniang and Liu Mengmei face one another through silk, mist and water, transforming “Why must feeling born in a dream be unreal?” into visible distance.

The later movement enters Tang Xianzu’s study.

Historical books open.

The brush descends.

Du Liniang and Liu Mengmei take form beyond the window among moonlit blossoms.

As the confrontation between feeling and reason reaches its final question, night gradually moves toward dawn.

In the closing sequence, the lovers walk together through the corridor.

Tang Xianzu rolls the completed manuscript.

The fan, self-portrait and peony hairpin remain within the morning light.

The dream appears to have ended.

Yet it has not entirely disappeared.

### A Text About Du Liniang—and Everyone Whose Experience Cannot Be Exhausted by Ordinary Reason

**Preface to The Peony Pavilion** has endured for more than four centuries not only because “Love arises from no known beginning” is beautiful.

Its deeper force lies in Tang Xianzu’s refusal to present feeling as something weak, private or valid only when regulated by society.

Within his vision:

Dream is not simply the opposite of reality.

Feeling is not the enemy of reason.

Death is not the conclusion of the story.

Rebirth is not merely supernatural spectacle.

Dream is where inward life begins to speak.

Feeling is the force that carries a person beyond established identity and external form.

Death is the fracture that appears when the waking world cannot contain feeling at its utmost.

Rebirth is the moment in which that feeling finally passes from dream into human life.

Du Liniang is the dreamer.

Liu Mengmei is the person found through the dream.

Tang Xianzu is the writer who gives that dream language, theatre and a continuing life among later generations.

Today, we may not believe that the dead can literally return.

Yet we still understand that some dreams change an entire life.

Some encounters are awaited before they occur.

Some feelings do not disappear merely because ordinary reason refuses them.

And some works of literature endure precisely because they refuse to let reason become the only boundary of the human world.

### About the Project

**Preface to The Peony Pavilion** is part of Grandevity Studio’s *Classical Literature Reimagined in Music* series.

The project seeks to bring classical Chinese prose and dramatic writing beyond textbooks, annotation, recitation, stage performance and memorisation, allowing it to re-enter contemporary melody, rhythm, sound, imagery and emotional life.

More than four centuries ago, Tang Xianzu wrote:

> People speak only of what reason declares impossible—but how can they know what feeling may yet bring into being?

Today, we still measure the unknown through the known.

We reject the exceptional through the evidence of the ordinary.

We decide whether a feeling deserves belief according to whether it can be proved.

Literature matters because it preserves a place for experiences that have not yet been fully understood.

May this song accompany everyone who has been told that something was impossible, yet continued to face their inward truth honestly.

And beyond reason, may we still leave a moon gate open for dream, feeling, imagination and the possibilities that the human world has not yet exhausted.
